Magnetic fields and poles

A magnetic field exists around magnets and around moving charges (currents). Its direction at any point is the direction a compass needle's north pole would point. Its strength is the magnetic flux density \(B\), measured in tesla (T). Earth's field at the surface is about \(5\times10^{-5}\ \text{T}\); a strong lab magnet is about 1 T.

  • Outside a magnet, field lines run from N to S. Inside, S to N. They form closed loops.
  • Like poles repel; unlike poles attract.
  • A horseshoe or pair of flat pole pieces facing each other produces a nearly uniform field in the gap: this is the field used in motors and generators.

Earth's magnetic field

Earth behaves roughly like a giant bar magnet tilted slightly from its rotation axis. The north end of a compass is attracted towards the geographic North Pole, which means there is a magnetic south pole near the geographic North Pole. Field lines leave the Southern Hemisphere, run northward across the surface and dip into the ground in the Northern Hemisphere. In Melbourne the field points roughly north (horizontally) and upward (out of the ground), because we're in the Southern Hemisphere.

Dot and cross notation

The field around a straight current-carrying wire

A current in a straight wire creates circular field lines centred on the wire. The field is stronger close to the wire and weaker further away (non-uniform).

Point your right thumb in the direction of the conventional current (+ to −). Your fingers curl in the direction of the magnetic field.

The grip rule uses conventional current (positive to negative). If a question describes the flow of electrons, the conventional current is in the opposite direction. This catches many students in both field and force questions.

Loops and solenoids

Bending the wire into a loop concentrates the field in the middle. A solenoid (a long coil of many loops) produces a field just like a bar magnet: strong and uniform inside, weak and spread out outside.

Curl your right fingers in the direction of the conventional current around the loops. Your thumb points to the north pole end of the solenoid (the direction of the field inside).

Ways to make a solenoid's field stronger: more current, more turns per metre, and an iron core (which makes an electromagnet).

Adding magnetic fields as vectors

Where two fields overlap, the total field is their vector sum. A compass needle lines up with the resultant field.

A long vertical wire carries a current. At a point near the wire, the wire produces a horizontal field of \(3.0\times10^{-5}\ \text{T}\) towards the east, and Earth's horizontal field there is \(4.0\times10^{-5}\ \text{T}\) towards the north. Find the magnitude and direction of the resultant field, and the direction a compass points.

The two fields are perpendicular, so use Pythagoras:

\[ B = \sqrt{(3.0\times10^{-5})^2 + (4.0\times10^{-5})^2} = 5.0\times10^{-5}\ \text{T} \]

Direction: \(\tan\theta = \dfrac{3.0}{4.0} \Rightarrow \theta = 37^\circ\) east of north.

\(5.0\times10^{-5}\ \text{T}\) at 37° east of north; the compass needle points along N37°E.

Forces between magnetic fields

  • Two magnets: like poles repel, unlike poles attract.
  • Two parallel wires, currents in the same direction: attract. Opposite directions: repel. (Each wire sits in the other's field and feels a force, see Force on conductors.)
  • Two solenoids end to end behave like two bar magnets.

Practice

A long straight wire runs perpendicular to the page with conventional current flowing out of the page. The magnetic field lines around it are:

  1. straight lines radiating outward
  2. anticlockwise circles
  3. clockwise circles
  4. parallel to the wire

Right thumb out of the page; fingers curl anticlockwise (as seen looking at the page).

Electrons flow upward through a vertical wire. Looking down on the wire from above, the magnetic field around it circulates:

  1. anticlockwise
  2. outward, away from the wire
  3. there is no field, because electrons are negative
  4. clockwise

Electrons upward means conventional current downward. Looking down, the current is going away from you (into the page), so the field is clockwise. Using the electron direction directly gives the wrong answer (A).

Which of these is the best description of the field inside a long current-carrying solenoid?

  1. uniform, parallel to the axis of the solenoid
  2. zero
  3. circular around the axis
  4. strongest at the edges of the coils

Inside a long solenoid the field lines are straight, parallel and evenly spaced.

The north pole of a compass needle points towards Earth's geographic North Pole. This shows that near Earth's geographic North Pole there is:

  1. a magnetic north pole
  2. no magnetic pole
  3. a magnetic south pole
  4. a magnetic monopole

Unlike poles attract: the compass's N pole is pulled towards a magnetic S pole.

A solenoid lies along an east–west line. Looking at it from the east end, the conventional current flows anticlockwise around the coils. Which end is the north pole?

Curl your right fingers anticlockwise as seen from the east: your thumb points towards you, i.e. out of the east end. The field inside points east, so the east end is the north pole. (Quick check: looking at an end, anticlockwise current = North, clockwise = South.)

Two long parallel wires, P and Q, both carry current into the page. Explain, using the field of P and the force on Q, why the wires attract.

P's current into the page makes a clockwise field around P. At Q's position (say Q is to the right of P), that field points downward (towards the bottom of the page).

Q carries current into the page in a downward field. Using the right-hand force rule (fingers down for B, thumb into page for I), the palm faces left: towards P.

By the same argument P is pushed towards Q (third law), so they attract.

Summary

  • Field direction: where a compass N pole points. Unit: tesla.
  • Straight wire: circles, right-hand grip (thumb = conventional current).
  • Solenoid: bar-magnet field, uniform inside; fingers = current, thumb = north.
  • Fields add as vectors; compasses follow the resultant.
