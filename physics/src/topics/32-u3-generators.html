How a generator works

A generator is a coil of wire rotated in a magnetic field (or a magnet rotated inside coils). As the coil turns, the angle between its plane and the field keeps changing, so the flux through it keeps changing, and by Faraday's law an emf is induced. Mechanical energy (from a turbine driven by steam, water or wind) becomes electrical energy.

Structurally, a generator is a motor run backwards: the same coil, magnets and brushes. The only difference between an AC and a DC generator is how the coil connects to the outside circuit.

Where in the rotation is the emf biggest?

Coil positionFlux through coilRate of change of fluxInduced emf
Plane perpendicular to \(B\)Maximum (\(BA\))Zero (momentarily not changing)Zero
Plane parallel to \(B\)ZeroMaximumMaximum

Another way to see it: when the plane is parallel to the field, the long sides of the coil are moving straight across the field lines (cutting them fastest). When the plane is perpendicular, the sides move along the field lines and cut none.

Flux and emf are a quarter of a cycle apart: emf is zero when flux is maximum, and maximum when flux is zero. If a question gives you a flux–time graph (a cosine), the emf graph is its negative gradient (a sine). Drawing the emf in phase with the flux is a classic error.

AC generators (alternators): slip rings

In an alternator each end of the coil is permanently connected to its own slip ring, and a brush presses on each ring. Because the connection never swaps, the output reverses every half turn exactly as the coil's emf does: the output is sinusoidal alternating current.

DC generators: the split-ring commutator

A DC generator uses a split-ring commutator, just like a DC motor. Every half turn, at the moment the coil's emf passes through zero (plane perpendicular to the field), the commutator swaps the coil's connections to the brushes. The emf in the coil still alternates, but the output to the external circuit always has the same polarity.

A single-coil DC generator gives pulsating DC: a series of positive humps that drop to zero twice every rotation. It is not the flat line of a battery. Real DC generators use many coils at different angles to smooth the output.

What changes the output?

ChangePeak emfPeriod / frequency
Double the number of turns \(N\)DoublesNo change
Double the field \(B\)DoublesNo change
Double the coil area \(A\)DoublesNo change
Double the rotation speedDoublesPeriod halves, frequency doubles

Spinning the coil faster makes the flux change faster, so the peak emf goes up and the waves get closer together. Many students double only the frequency and forget the amplitude (or vice versa). On an emf graph: twice as tall and twice as many cycles.

Average emf over a quarter turn

In a quarter turn (time \(T/4\)) the flux goes from \(BA\) to zero (or zero to \(BA\)), so Faraday's law gives the average emf over that quarter:

\[ \varepsilon_{\text{avg}} = N\frac{\Delta\Phi}{\Delta t} = N\frac{BA}{T/4} = 4NBAf \]

An alternator coil has 100 turns and an area of 0.020 m². It rotates at 50 revolutions per second in a 0.25 T field. Calculate the average emf during a quarter turn starting from the position where the coil's plane is perpendicular to the field.

Time for a quarter turn: \(T = 1/50 = 0.020\ \text{s}\), so \(\Delta t = 0.020/4 = 0.0050\ \text{s}\).

Flux change: \(BA = 0.25 \times 0.020 = 5.0\times10^{-3}\ \text{Wb}\) to 0.

\[ \varepsilon_{\text{avg}} = 100 \times \frac{5.0\times10^{-3}}{0.0050} = 100\ \text{V} \]

100 V average. The peak emf is higher: for a sinusoid, \(\varepsilon_{\text{peak}} = 2\pi fNBA = 157\ \text{V}\), which is \(\pi/2\) times the quarter-turn average.

A spinning motor coil is also a generator: as it turns, it induces an emf that opposes the supply voltage (Lenz's law). This back emf grows as the motor speeds up, reducing the current. That's why a motor draws a large current when it starts or when it is jammed (little or no back emf), and why jammed motors burn out.

Practice

In an AC generator, the induced emf is at its maximum when the plane of the coil is:

  1. parallel to the magnetic field, where the flux is zero
  2. perpendicular to the magnetic field, where the flux is maximum
  3. at 45° to the magnetic field
  4. perpendicular to the field, because the most field lines pass through it

Emf depends on the rate of change of flux, which is greatest when the flux is passing through zero (plane parallel to \(B\)).

The rotation speed of an alternator is doubled. The output voltage graph will have:

  1. the same peak voltage and half the period
  2. double the peak voltage and the same period
  3. double the peak voltage and half the period
  4. half the peak voltage and double the period

Faster rotation means the flux changes twice as fast (double peak emf) and each cycle takes half the time.

The difference between an AC generator and a DC generator is that:

  1. an AC generator uses a permanent magnet and a DC generator uses an electromagnet
  2. an AC generator uses slip rings and a DC generator uses a split-ring commutator
  3. the coil in a DC generator does not rotate
  4. a DC generator produces a constant, steady voltage like a battery

The connection is the only difference. A single-coil DC generator gives pulsating (not steady) DC.

A generator coil's flux–time graph is a cosine curve starting at its maximum value at \(t = 0\). The emf–time graph:

  1. is also a cosine curve starting at its maximum
  2. is a straight line
  3. is a cosine curve starting at its minimum
  4. is a sine curve starting at zero

emf ∝ negative gradient of flux. At \(t = 0\) the cosine is at its peak (gradient zero), so the emf starts at zero and follows a sine curve.

A generator produces a peak emf of 12 V at a frequency of 25 Hz. The number of turns on the coil is doubled and the rotation speed is halved. Give the new peak emf and frequency.

Doubling \(N\) doubles the peak; halving the speed halves the peak and halves the frequency. Net peak: \(12 \times 2 \times \tfrac12 = 12\ \text{V}\). Frequency: \(25/2 = 12.5\ \text{Hz}\).

A 250-turn coil of area 40 cm² rotates at 10 revolutions per second in a 0.15 T field. Find the average emf over a quarter turn, and sketch how the output would look with (a) slip rings and (b) a split-ring commutator.

\(A = 4.0\times10^{-3}\ \text{m}^2\); \(\Delta t = T/4 = 0.10/4 = 0.025\ \text{s}\).

\(\varepsilon_{\text{avg}} = NBA/\Delta t = 250 \times 0.15 \times 4.0\times10^{-3}/0.025 = 6.0\ \text{V}\).

(a) A sine wave alternating between + and − peaks, period 0.10 s. (b) The same shape with every negative half flipped positive: humps every 0.050 s, never going below zero.

Explain why a DC generator with a single coil produces a varying rather than a constant voltage, and suggest how its output could be made smoother.

The emf induced in the rotating coil still varies sinusoidally with the rate of change of flux, dropping to zero when the coil's plane is perpendicular to the field. [1]

The commutator only reverses the connections every half turn so the output never changes sign; it can't stop the size of the emf varying, so the output is a series of pulses dropping to zero. [1]

Using several coils at different angles (each with its own commutator segments) means there's always a coil near its maximum emf, giving a much smoother output. [1]

Summary

  • Rotating coil → changing flux → emf. Max emf when flux is zero (plane ∥ B).
  • Slip rings → AC; split-ring commutator → pulsating DC.
  • More N, B or A → bigger peak. Faster rotation → bigger peak and higher frequency.
  • Average emf over a quarter turn \(= NBA/(T/4)\).
