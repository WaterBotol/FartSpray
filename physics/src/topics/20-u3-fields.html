What is a field?

A field is a region of space in which an object experiences a non-contact force because of a property it has: mass (gravitational field), charge (electric field) or magnetism / moving charge (magnetic field).

The field model replaces the puzzling idea of “action at a distance”. A mass creates a gravitational field around it; another mass placed in that field feels a force from the field at its location. Fields are vectors: they have a strength and a direction at every point.

Drawing and reading field diagrams

  • Arrows show the direction of the force on a test mass, a positive test charge, or a north pole.
  • Lines closer together mean a stronger field.
  • Field lines never cross (the field has only one direction at each point).
  • Electric field lines start on positive charges and end on negative charges, meeting the surface at right angles.
  • Magnetic field lines form closed loops: outside a magnet they go N → S, inside they go S → N.

Monopoles and dipoles

  • A monopole is a single isolated source: a mass, or a single positive or negative charge. Its field is radial.
  • A dipole is a pair of opposite sources: a + and − charge, or the N and S poles of a magnet.
  • Gravity only has monopoles (there is no “negative mass”). Electric fields have both. Magnetic monopoles have never been observed. Cut a bar magnet in half and you get two smaller magnets, each with its own N and S pole.

A single pole cannot be isolated. Any question offering “a magnetic monopole such as the north pole of a bar magnet” as correct is wrong. Also note that Earth's geographic North Pole is near a magnetic south pole: that's why the north end of a compass is attracted there.

The inverse square law

For a point mass or point charge, field strength falls off with the square of the distance from the centre:

\[ g = \frac{GM}{r^2} \qquad E = \frac{kq}{r^2} \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \frac{\text{field}_2}{\text{field}_1} = \left(\frac{r_1}{r_2}\right)^2 \]
  • Double the distance → \(\tfrac14\) of the field. Triple → \(\tfrac19\). Halve → 4 ×.
  • The same applies to the force on an object placed in the field.

Picture the field lines spreading out from a point source. At distance \(r\) they pass through a sphere of area \(4\pi r^2\). Double \(r\) and the same number of lines spreads over four times the area, so the density of lines (the field strength) drops to a quarter.

A satellite 6370 km above Earth's surface is \(2R_E\) from Earth's centre, so it experiences \(\tfrac14\) of surface gravity, not \(\tfrac12\) and not zero.

Potential energy changes in fields (qualitative)

Moving an object against the force the field exerts on it takes work and increases its potential energy. Moving with the force decreases potential energy (usually turning it into kinetic energy).

MovementPotential energy
A rocket moving away from EarthGravitational PE increases

A positive charge pushed towards another positive chargeElectric PE increases
A positive charge moving away from a positive chargeElectric PE decreases
An electron moving towards a positive charge (or positive plate)Electric PE decreases (it speeds up)
An electron moving away from a positive chargeElectric PE increases

How two fields interact

  • Masses only ever attract.
  • Charges: like charges repel, unlike charges attract.
  • Magnetic poles: like poles repel, unlike poles attract.
  • Current-carrying conductors: parallel wires with currents in the same direction attract; opposite directions repel. (Each wire sits in the other's magnetic field.)

Practice

Which of the following is not possible?

  1. an electric monopole
  2. an electric dipole
  3. a magnetic monopole
  4. a magnetic dipole

Isolated magnetic poles have never been observed; every magnet has both N and S poles.

The gravitational field strength at a point 2000 km from the centre of a small moon is 1.2 N kg⁻¹. At 4000 km from the centre it is:

  1. 0.60 N kg⁻¹
  2. 0.30 N kg⁻¹
  3. 2.4 N kg⁻¹
  4. 4.8 N kg⁻¹

Doubling the distance gives \(\tfrac14\) of the field: \(1.2/4 = 0.30\ \text{N kg}^{-1}\).

The direction of an electric field at a point is defined as the direction of the force on:

  1. an electron placed at that point
  2. a small mass placed at that point
  3. a compass needle's north pole
  4. a small positive charge placed at that point

By convention, electric field direction is the force on a positive test charge. An electron experiences a force opposite to the field.

An electron is released from rest near a positively charged sphere. As it moves towards the sphere, its electric potential energy:

  1. decreases and its kinetic energy increases
  2. increases and its kinetic energy increases
  3. increases and its kinetic energy decreases
  4. stays constant because the electron is negative

The electron moves in the direction of the force on it (attraction), so the field does positive work on it: potential energy becomes kinetic energy. Don't confuse this with a positive charge moving along the field direction; think about the direction of the force on the object.

Which field is uniform?

  1. the gravitational field around the Moon
  2. the electric field around a point charge
  3. the electric field between two large, parallel charged plates
  4. the magnetic field around a bar magnet

Between parallel plates the field lines are parallel and evenly spaced: same magnitude and direction everywhere (away from the edges).

Two long parallel wires carry currents in the same direction. The wires will:

  1. repel each other
  2. attract each other
  3. experience no force, because currents are not charges at rest
  4. twist to become perpendicular

Each wire lies in the magnetic field of the other. Using the right-hand grip rule and the force rule, parallel currents in the same direction attract.

Compare gravitational and electric fields by giving one similarity and two differences.

Similarity: both are produced by point sources with radial fields whose strength follows an inverse square law (\(g = GM/r^2\), \(E = kq/r^2\)); both are non-contact forces described by a field model.

Differences: gravitational fields only attract, but electric fields can attract or repel; gravitational fields act on mass while electric fields act on charge; electric forces are enormously stronger (about \(10^{39}\) times stronger between a proton and an electron).

The electric field 0.30 m from a small charged sphere is \(4.0\times10^3\ \text{N C}^{-1}\). At what distance is the field \(1.0\times10^3\ \text{N C}^{-1}\)?

The field has dropped to \(\tfrac14\), so the distance must double: 0.60 m from the centre of the sphere.

Draw the magnetic field around a bar magnet and explain why the field lines are drawn as closed loops, while electric field lines around a dipole start and stop.

Field lines leave the N pole, curve around outside and enter the S pole; inside the magnet they continue S → N, closing the loop (see the bar magnet diagram above). [1]

Electric field lines begin on positive charges and end on negative charges: isolated charges (monopoles) exist to act as starting and ending points. [1]

There are no magnetic monopoles for lines to start or end on, so magnetic field lines always form continuous closed loops. [1]

Summary

  • Field direction: force on a mass (g), a positive charge (E), a north pole (B).
  • Gravity attracts only; electric and magnetic fields attract or repel. No magnetic monopoles.
  • Point sources obey the inverse square law, measured from the centre.
  • Moving against the field force raises potential energy; moving with it lowers potential energy.
