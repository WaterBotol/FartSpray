The problem: power lines have resistance

Transmission lines are long, and even thick aluminium cables have resistance. When current flows through them, two bad things happen:

Transmission-line losses
\[ V_{\text{drop}} = I_{\text{line}}R_{\text{line}} \qquad P_{\text{loss}} = I_{\text{line}}^2R_{\text{line}} \]

\(R_{\text{line}}\) is the total resistance of the wires (both the “out” and “return” lines). The voltage drop is lost along the way, so less arrives at the far end; the power loss heats the wires.

Because \(P_{\text{loss}} \propto I^2\), halving the current cuts the loss to a quarter. And for a given power, \(P = VI\) means a higher voltage needs a smaller current.

For a fixed power \(P\), the line current is \(I = P/V\), so \(P_{\text{loss}} = (P/V)^2R\). Increasing the transmission voltage by a factor of \(n\) reduces the power loss by a factor of \(n^2\). Transformers make this possible, and they only work with AC, which is the main reason grids use AC.

The classic comparison

A generator supplies 100 kW at 250 V to a town through transmission lines with a total resistance of 0.50 Ω.

(a) Without transformers, find the line current, the voltage drop along the lines, the power lost and the voltage reaching the town. (b) Repeat with an ideal transformer stepping the voltage up to 10 kV at the generator.

(a) Line current (from the power supplied):

\[ I = \frac{P}{V} = \frac{100\,000}{250} = 400\ \text{A} \]

Voltage drop and power loss:

\[ V_{\text{drop}} = IR = 400 \times 0.50 = 200\ \text{V} \qquad P_{\text{loss}} = I^2R = 400^2 \times 0.50 = 80\ \text{kW} \]

Voltage at the town: \(250 - 200 = 50\ \text{V}\). Only \(100 - 80 = 20\ \text{kW}\) arrives. Useless.

(b) With 10 kV transmission. The transformer keeps the power at 100 kW:

\[ I = \frac{100\,000}{10\,000} = 10\ \text{A} \]

Drop and loss:

\[ V_{\text{drop}} = 10 \times 0.50 = 5.0\ \text{V} \qquad P_{\text{loss}} = 10^2 \times 0.50 = 50\ \text{W} \]

The town end receives 9995 V (before stepping down) and 99.95 kW.

(a) 400 A, 200 V drop, 80 kW lost, 50 V arrives (b) 10 A, 5.0 V drop, 50 W lost. Raising the voltage 40 times cut the loss by \(40^2 = 1600\) times.

Never calculate line loss as \(V^2/R\) using the supply or transmission voltage. That voltage is not across the line resistance. \(10\,000^2/0.50 = 200\ \text{MW}\), which is nonsense. In \(P = V^2/R\) the \(V\) must be the voltage drop across the lines (\(5.0^2/0.50 = 50\ \text{W}\) ✓). The safe route is always: find \(I_{\text{line}}\) first, then use \(I^2R\).

  1. Find the line current: \(I_{\text{line}} = P/V\), using the voltage at the start of the line (after any step-up).
  2. \(V_{\text{drop}} = I_{\text{line}}R_{\text{line}}\) → subtract to get the voltage at the far end.
  3. \(P_{\text{loss}} = I_{\text{line}}^2R_{\text{line}}\) → subtract to get the power delivered.
  4. If there's a step-down transformer, apply its turns ratio to the voltage arriving at it (not the transmission voltage).

A wind turbine generates 50 kW at 400 V rms. It supplies a farmhouse through lines with a total resistance of 2.0 Ω.

(a) Find the power delivered to the farmhouse if no transformers are used. (b) An ideal 1:20 step-up transformer is installed at the turbine and an ideal 20:1 step-down transformer at the farmhouse. Find the line current, the power loss, and the voltage supplied to the farmhouse. (c) By what factor did the power loss decrease?

(a) \(I = 50\,000/400 = 125\ \text{A}\); \(P_{\text{loss}} = 125^2 \times 2.0 = 31\,250\ \text{W}\).

Delivered: \(50\,000 - 31\,250 = 18\,750\ \text{W} \approx 19\ \text{kW}\) (only 38%!). Voltage at the farm: \(400 - 125 \times 2.0 = 150\ \text{V}\).

(b) After step-up: \(V = 400 \times 20 = 8000\ \text{V}\), so

\[ I_{\text{line}} = \frac{50\,000}{8000} = 6.25\ \text{A} \]

Loss and drop:

\[ P_{\text{loss}} = 6.25^2 \times 2.0 = 78\ \text{W} \qquad V_{\text{drop}} = 6.25 \times 2.0 = 12.5\ \text{V} \]

Voltage arriving at the step-down transformer: \(8000 - 12.5 = 7987.5\ \text{V}\).

After stepping down 20:1: \(7987.5/20 = 399\ \text{V}\).

(c) \(31\,250/78.1 = 400\), which is \(20^2\) because the current dropped by a factor of 20.

(a) 19 kW (b) 6.25 A, 78 W lost, 399 V at the farmhouse (c) 400 times smaller.

AC, DC and the grid

  • AC is used for distribution because transformers make changing voltage cheap and efficient, and they only work with AC.
  • Transmission voltages are limited by insulation and safety (arcing to towers, electric fields near the ground), which is why lines are high on tall towers.

For very long cables, especially undersea ones like Basslink between Victoria and Tasmania, high-voltage DC is used. AC in long undersea cables wastes energy charging and discharging the cable's capacitance every cycle. Modern power electronics convert AC to high-voltage DC and back at each end.

Practice

The voltage at which a fixed amount of power is transmitted is doubled. The power lost in the transmission lines becomes:

  1. double
  2. half
  3. one quarter
  4. unchanged

Double \(V\) → half \(I\) → \(I^2R\) is \(\tfrac14\).

A power station transmits 20 MW at 200 kV through lines of total resistance 10 Ω. The power lost in the lines is:

  1. 100 kW
  2. 4.0 GW
  3. 1.0 kW
  4. 2.0 MW

\(I = 20\times10^6/200\times10^3 = 100\ \text{A}\); \(P_{\text{loss}} = 100^2 \times 10 = 1.0\times10^5\ \text{W}\). Option B is the \(V^2/R\) trap: \((200\,000)^2/10\).

The main reason transmission lines operate at very high voltages is to:

  1. increase the power delivered by the power station
  2. make electricity travel faster
  3. reduce the resistance of the lines
  4. reduce the current, and so reduce the \(I^2R\) power loss

High voltage means low current for the same power. The line resistance is unchanged; the loss drops because it depends on \(I^2\).

Power is transmitted at 11 kV to a step-down transformer with a 50:1 ratio. The lines drop 500 V. The voltage at the transformer's output is:

  1. 220 V
  2. 210 V
  3. 11 kV
  4. 230 V

The voltage arriving at the transformer is \(11\,000 - 500 = 10\,500\ \text{V}\); \(10\,500/50 = 210\ \text{V}\). Dividing 11 kV by 50 ignores the drop.

A generator delivers 5.0 kW at 240 V to a shed through lines of total resistance 0.40 Ω. Find the line current, the voltage at the shed and the percentage of power lost.

\(I = 5000/240 = 20.8\ \text{A}\). \(V_{\text{drop}} = 20.8 \times 0.40 = 8.3\ \text{V}\), so the shed receives \(240 - 8.3 = 232\ \text{V}\).

\(P_{\text{loss}} = 20.8^2 \times 0.40 = 174\ \text{W}\), which is \(174/5000 = 3.5\%\).

A town needs 2.0 MW. It is supplied through lines of total resistance 5.0 Ω. The power company wants to keep line losses below 1% of the power supplied. What is the minimum transmission voltage? (Assume the 2.0 MW is the power entering the lines.)

Maximum loss: \(0.01 \times 2.0\times10^6 = 2.0\times10^4\ \text{W}\).

\(I^2R \leq 2.0\times10^4 \Rightarrow I \leq \sqrt{2.0\times10^4/5.0} = 63.2\ \text{A}\).

\(V = P/I \geq 2.0\times10^6/63.2 = 3.2\times10^4\ \text{V}\): at least about 32 kV.

A student suggests that, to reduce transmission losses, the power company should use a step-down transformer at the power station “so that less voltage is lost”. Explain why this is wrong.

For a fixed power, stepping the voltage down increases the line current (\(I = P/V\)). [1]

Power loss is \(I^2R\), so a larger current increases the losses (by the square of the factor), and the voltage drop \(IR\) also increases. [1]

The correct approach is to step the voltage up for transmission (reducing \(I\)), then step it down near the consumer. [1]

Summary

  • \(I_{\text{line}} = P/V\), then \(V_{\text{drop}} = IR\) and \(P_{\text{loss}} = I^2R\).
  • Stepping voltage up by \(n\) cuts the loss by \(n^2\).
  • Never use \(V^2/R\) with the supply voltage.
  • Step-down transformers act on the voltage that arrives, after the drop.
