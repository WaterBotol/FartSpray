When is it binomial?

\(X \sim \text{Bi}(n, p)\) counts the number of successes when:

  1. there's a fixed number \(n\) of trials;
  2. each trial has two outcomes (success/failure);
  3. the probability of success \(p\) is the same every trial;
  4. the trials are independent.
Binomial distribution
\[ P(X = x) = \binom{n}{x}p^x(1 - p)^{n - x},\quad x = 0, 1, \ldots, n \qquad E(X) = np \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = np(1 - p) \]

\(\binom{n}{x} = \frac{n!}{x!(n - x)!}\) counts the arrangements of \(x\) successes among \(n\) trials. For example \(\binom{5}{2} = 10\).

30% of customers buy a coffee. Ten customers are chosen at random. Find the probability that (a) exactly 3 buy coffee, (b) at least 3 buy coffee, (c) given at least one buys coffee, at least 3 do.

(a) \(\binom{10}{3}(0.3)^3(0.7)^7 = 120 \times 0.027 \times 0.0823543 \approx 0.2668\).

(b) \(P(X \ge 3) = 1 - P(X \le 2) \approx 1 - 0.3828 = 0.6172\) (CAS: binomCdf).

(c) \(P(X \ge 3 \mid X \ge 1) = \dfrac{P(X \ge 3)}{P(X \ge 1)} = \dfrac{0.6172}{1 - 0.7^{10}} = \dfrac{0.6172}{0.9718}\).

(a) 0.2668, (b) 0.6172, (c) 0.6352. In (c), “at least 3” is already inside “at least 1”, so the intersection is just \(P(X \ge 3)\).

A machine produces faulty items with probability 0.1. How many items must be tested so that the probability of finding at least one faulty item exceeds 0.95?

\(P(X \ge 1) = 1 - 0.9^n > 0.95 \Rightarrow 0.9^n < 0.05\).

\(n > \dfrac{\log_e 0.05}{\log_e 0.9} \approx 28.43\). (The inequality flips because \(\log_e 0.9 < 0\).)

\(n = 29\). Always round up for “smallest \(n\)”. Check: \(1 - 0.9^{28} \approx 0.948\) (too small), \(1 - 0.9^{29} \approx 0.953\) ✓.

\(X \sim \text{Bi}(n, p)\) with \(E(X) = 6\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(X) = 4.2\). Find \(n\) and \(p\).

Divide: \(\dfrac{np(1 - p)}{np} = \dfrac{4.2}{6} \Rightarrow 1 - p = 0.7\).

\(p = 0.3\), \(n = \frac{6}{0.3} = 20\).

  • “At least 1” = \(1 - P(X = 0)\). Never add up 1, 2, 3, … by hand.
  • Discrete inequalities: \(P(X < 4) = P(X \le 3)\), and \(P(X > 4) = 1 - P(X \le 4)\). Off-by-one errors are the most common binomial mistake.
  • Without replacement from a small population isn't binomial (the probability changes each trial).
  • Conditional binomial: the numerator is the intersection. \(P(X = 2 \mid X \ge 1) = \frac{P(X = 2)}{P(X \ge 1)}\) because \(X = 2\) is inside \(X \ge 1\).

Practice

\(X \sim \text{Bi}(5, 0.2)\). \(P(X \ge 1)\) is closest to

  1. 0.328
  2. 0.672
  3. 0.410
  4. 0.2

\(1 - 0.8^5 = 1 - 0.32768 = 0.67232\).

\(X \sim \text{Bi}(20, 0.3)\). The standard deviation of \(X\) is

  1. 4.2
  2. 6
  3. 2.45
  4. \(\sqrt{4.2}\)

\(\operatorname{Var} = 20 \times 0.3 \times 0.7 = 4.2\), so sd \(= \sqrt{4.2} \approx 2.05\). (C is \(\sqrt6\), a wrong formula.)

\(X \sim \text{Bi}(12, 0.4)\). \(P(X < 4)\) equals

  1. \(P(X \le 3)\)
  2. \(1 - P(X \le 4)\)
  3. \(P(X \le 4)\)
  4. \(1 - P(X \ge 3)\)

For a discrete variable, “less than 4” means 0, 1, 2 or 3.

The smallest number of independent trials, each with success probability 0.2, needed for \(P(\text{at least one success}) > 0.99\) is

  1. 20
  2. 5
  3. 21
  4. 50

\(0.8^n < 0.01 \Rightarrow n > \frac{\log_e 0.01}{\log_e 0.8} \approx 20.64\), so \(n = 21\).

The most likely value (mode) of \(X \sim \text{Bi}(20, 0.3)\) is

  1. 5
  2. 6
  3. 7
  4. 10

\(P(X = 6) \approx 0.192\) is the largest (\(P(X = 5) \approx 0.179\), \(P(X = 7) \approx 0.164\)). The mode is near the mean \(np = 6\).

\(X \sim \text{Bi}(5, 0.4)\). \(P(X = 2 \mid X \ge 1)\) is closest to

  1. 0.3456
  2. 0.9222
  3. 0.3187
  4. 0.3747

\(\frac{P(X = 2)}{1 - P(X = 0)} = \frac{0.3456}{1 - 0.07776} \approx 0.3747\). A forgets to divide.

A fair coin is tossed \(n\) times. The probability of exactly one head is \(\frac{3}{32}\). The value of \(n\) is

  1. 6
  2. 5
  3. 8
  4. 3

\(n\left(\frac12\right)^n = \frac{3}{32}\). Test: \(n = 6\): \(\frac{6}{64} = \frac{3}{32}\) ✓.

A multiple-choice test has 8 questions, each with 4 options. A student guesses every answer. Find the probability that they get at least 2 correct.

\(X \sim \text{Bi}(8, 0.25)\). \(P(X \ge 2) = 1 - P(X = 0) - P(X = 1) = 1 - 0.75^8 - 8(0.25)(0.75)^7 \approx 0.6329\).

Show that \(P(X = 2)\) for \(X \sim \text{Bi}(4, p)\) is \(6p^2(1 - p)^2\), and find its value when \(p = \frac12\).

\(\binom42 = 6\), so \(P(X = 2) = 6p^2(1 - p)^2\). At \(p = \frac12\): \(6 \times \frac{1}{16} = \frac38\).

For \(X \sim \text{Bi}(4, p)\), find the value of \(p\) that maximises \(P(X = 2)\).

Let \(g(p) = 6p^2(1 - p)^2\). \(g'(p) = 12p(1 - p)^2 - 12p^2(1 - p) = 12p(1 - p)(1 - 2p)\). [2]

For \(0 < p < 1\): \(g' = 0 \Rightarrow p = \frac12\) (a maximum, \(g = \frac38\)). [1]

Seeds germinate independently with probability 0.85. (a) In a tray of 20 seeds, find the probability that at least 18 germinate. (b) A gardener buys 5 trays. Find the probability that at least 2 trays have at least 18 seeds germinating.

(a) \(X \sim \text{Bi}(20, 0.85)\): \(P(X \ge 18) \approx 0.4049\). [2]

(b) \(Y \sim \text{Bi}(5, 0.4049)\): \(P(Y \ge 2) = 1 - P(Y = 0) - P(Y = 1) \approx 1 - 0.0746 - 0.2539 \approx 0.6714\). [2] Two binomial distributions, one nested in the other: a VCAA favourite.

Three cards are drawn from a standard deck without replacement, and \(X\) counts the hearts. Explain why \(X\) is not binomial.

The trials aren't independent and \(p\) changes: \(P(\text{heart}) = \frac{13}{52}\) on the first draw, but \(\frac{12}{51}\) on the second if the first was a heart. (With replacement it would be \(\text{Bi}(3, \frac14)\).)

Summary

  • Fixed \(n\), two outcomes, constant \(p\), independent: \(\text{Bi}(n, p)\).
  • \(E = np\), \(\operatorname{Var} = np(1 - p)\).
  • “At least one”: \(1 - (1 - p)^n\). For the smallest \(n\), use logs and round up.
  • Check every inequality sign: \(<\) vs \(\le\) matters for discrete variables.
