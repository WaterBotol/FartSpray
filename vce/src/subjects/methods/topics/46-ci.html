The interval

Approximate confidence interval for \(p\)
\[ \left(\hat p - z\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1 - \hat p)}{n}},\ \ \hat p + z\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1 - \hat p)}{n}}\right) \]

\(z = 1.96\) for 95% (\(\approx 2\) tech-free), \(z \approx 2.576\) for 99%, \(z \approx 1.645\) for 90%. The margin of error is \(M = z\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1 - \hat p)}{n}}\), half the width.

If we took many samples and built a 95% interval from each, about 95% of those intervals would contain the true \(p\). The true \(p\) is fixed: any single interval either contains it or doesn't.

In a random sample of 200 students, 60 walk to school. Find an approximate 95% confidence interval for the proportion of all students who walk, to 4 decimal places.

A confidence interval is the sample estimate plus or minus a margin of error. The margin is \(z\) times the standard error, where \(z = 1.96\) for 95% confidence.

\(\hat p = \frac{60}{200} = 0.3\). Standard error \(= \sqrt{\frac{0.3 \times 0.7}{200}} \approx 0.03240\).

\(M = 1.96 \times 0.03240 \approx 0.0635\).

In a sample of 400 voters, 248 support a proposal. Find an approximate 99% confidence interval for \(p\).

Higher confidence needs a bigger \(z\), which widens the interval. The trade-off is precision: you are more confident, but the interval says less.

\(\hat p = 0.62\); SE \(= \sqrt{\frac{0.62 \times 0.38}{400}} \approx 0.02427\); \(M = 2.5758 \times 0.02427 \approx 0.0625\).

\((0.5575,\ 0.6825)\). The 95% interval is \((0.5724, 0.6676)\). More confidence needs a wider net.

An approximate 95% confidence interval for \(p\) is \((0.52, 0.64)\). Find \(\hat p\), the margin of error and the sample size.

An interval is symmetric about \(\hat p\), so \(\hat p\) is the midpoint and the margin is half the width. Put both into the margin formula and solve for \(n\), rounding up.

\(\hat p\) is the midpoint: \(\frac{0.52 + 0.64}{2} = 0.58\). \(M\) is half the width: \(0.06\).

\(0.06 = 1.96\sqrt{\frac{0.58 \times 0.42}{n}} \Rightarrow n = \frac{1.96^2 \times 0.2436}{0.06^2} \approx 259.9\).

\(\hat p = 0.58\), \(M = 0.06\), \(n = 260\).

A pollster wants a 95% margin of error of at most 3 percentage points and has no idea of \(p\). How many people should be surveyed?

With no estimate of \(p\), use \(\hat p = 0.5\): it makes \(\hat p(1 - \hat p)\) as large as possible, so the sample is big enough whatever the true \(p\). Always round the sample size up.

Use \(\hat p = 0.5\) (which gives the largest possible margin): \(1.96\sqrt{\frac{0.25}{n}} \le 0.03 \Rightarrow n \ge \frac{1.96^2 \times 0.25}{0.03^2} \approx 1067.1\).

\(n = 1068\) (round up). This is why national polls often survey about 1000 people.

Using an interval to judge a claim

If a claimed value of \(p\) lies inside the interval, the data are consistent with the claim. If it's outside, the claim is doubtful (at that confidence level). An interval never proves the claim.

  • Don't say “there's a 95% probability that \(p\) is in (0.24, 0.36)”. VCAA wants the “95% of such intervals” interpretation.
  • Higher confidence → wider interval; larger \(n\) → narrower. To halve the width, quadruple \(n\).
  • Round sample sizes up, even from 1067.1.
  • The interval is about the population, not about future samples.

Practice

In a sample of 100, \(\hat p = 0.5\). Using \(z \approx 2\), an approximate 95% confidence interval is

  1. \((0.45, 0.55)\)
  2. \((0.4, 0.6)\)
  3. \((0.3, 0.7)\)
  4. \((0.48, 0.52)\)

SE \(= \sqrt{\frac{0.25}{100}} = 0.05\); \(M = 2 \times 0.05 = 0.1\).

The midpoint: \(\frac{0.32 + 0.40}{2} = 0.36\). The margin is 0.04.

Find the smallest sample size for a 99% margin of error of at most 0.05, given a pilot study suggests \(p \approx 0.2\).

\(2.5758\sqrt{\frac{0.16}{n}} \le 0.05 \Rightarrow n \ge \frac{2.5758^2 \times 0.16}{0.0025}\). [2]

\(n \ge 424.6\), so \(n = 425\). [1]

A random sample of 250 households found 90 with solar panels. (a) Find an approximate 95% CI for \(p\). (b) A council claims 40% of households have solar panels. Comment. (c) How many more households would need to be surveyed (with the same \(\hat p\)) to halve the margin of error?

(a) \(\hat p = 0.36\); SE \(= \sqrt{\frac{0.36 \times 0.64}{250}} \approx 0.03036\); \(M \approx 0.0595\): \((0.3005, 0.4195)\). [2]

(b) 0.40 lies inside the interval, so the sample is consistent with the claim (but doesn't prove it). [1]

(c) Halving \(M\) needs \(4 \times 250 = 1000\) households: 750 more. [1]

100 different researchers each build a 95% confidence interval for the same \(p\) from their own random samples. How many intervals would you expect to miss \(p\)? Could it be more?

About 5 (5% of 100). It could easily be more or fewer: the number that miss is itself random (roughly \(\text{Bi}(100, 0.05)\)). Try the simulation above.

Summary

  • \(\hat p \pm z\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1 - \hat p)}{n}}\) with \(z = 1.96\) (95%).
  • Midpoint = \(\hat p\); half-width = margin of error.
  • Sample size: rearrange for \(n\), use \(\hat p = 0.5\) if unknown, round up.
  • Interpret as “95% of such intervals contain \(p\)”.
