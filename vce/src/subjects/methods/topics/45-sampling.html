Populations, samples and bias

A parameter describes a population (e.g. the true proportion \(p\) of voters who support a policy). A statistic is calculated from a sample (e.g. the sample proportion \(\hat p\)). Different samples give different \(\hat p\) values, so \(\hat p\) is a random variable.

  • Simple random sample: every group of \(n\) members is equally likely to be chosen.
  • Bias is a systematic tendency to over- or under-estimate. Examples: surveying only people at a gym about exercise habits (selection bias); voluntary online polls (self-selection bias); leading questions.
  • A bigger sample reduces random variability, but it doesn't fix bias.

The distribution of \(\hat p\)

Sample proportion
\[ \hat P = \frac{X}{n},\ X \sim \text{Bi}(n, p) \qquad E(\hat P) = p \qquad \operatorname{sd}(\hat P) = \sqrt{\frac{p(1 - p)}{n}} \]

For large \(n\) (a common guide is \(np \ge 10\) and \(n(1 - p) \ge 10\)), \(\hat P\) is approximately \(\text{N}\!\left(p, \frac{p(1 - p)}{n}\right)\).

Quadrupling the sample size halves the standard deviation of \(\hat P\) (because of the \(\sqrt{n}\)). Diminishing returns: going from \(n = 100\) to \(n = 400\) helps as much as going from 400 to 1600.

30% of a town's households own a dog. Random samples of 100 households are taken. Find the mean and standard deviation of \(\hat P\).

\(E(\hat P) = p = 0.3\).

\(\operatorname{sd}(\hat P) = \sqrt{\frac{0.3 \times 0.7}{100}} \approx 0.0458\).

With \(p = 0.3\) and \(n = 100\), find \(P(\hat P > 0.35)\) (a) exactly, using the binomial distribution, and (b) using the normal approximation.

(a) \(\hat P > 0.35 \iff X > 35 \iff X \ge 36\). With \(X \sim \text{Bi}(100, 0.3)\): \(P(X \ge 36) \approx 0.1161\).

(b) \(\hat P \approx \text{N}(0.3, 0.0458^2)\): \(P(\hat P > 0.35) = P(Z > 1.091) \approx 0.1376\).

(a) 0.1161 (exact); (b) 0.1376 (approximate). Read which one the question asks for. For the exact version, the strict “\(>\)” matters: \(X \ge 36\), not \(X \ge 35\).

A fair coin is tossed 4 times and \(\hat P\) is the proportion of heads. Find \(P(\hat P \ge 0.75)\).

\(\hat P \ge 0.75 \iff X \ge 3\) with \(X \sim \text{Bi}(4, \frac12)\).

\(P(X = 3) + P(X = 4) = \frac{4}{16} + \frac{1}{16}\).

\(\frac{5}{16}\). Small samples: convert to a count and use the binomial.

  • \(p\) vs \(\hat p\): the formula for \(\operatorname{sd}(\hat P)\) uses the population \(p\) when it's known.
  • Convert proportions to counts carefully: \(\hat P \le 0.2\) with \(n = 50\) is \(X \le 10\).
  • Bigger samples don't remove bias. A huge biased sample is still biased.
  • sd of \(\hat P\) depends on \(\sqrt{n}\), not \(n\).

Practice

For samples of size 400 from a population with \(p = 0.5\), the standard deviation of \(\hat P\) is

  1. 0.05
  2. 0.00125
  3. 0.025
  4. 0.25

\(\sqrt{\frac{0.25}{400}} = \frac{0.5}{20} = 0.025\).

To halve the standard deviation of \(\hat P\), the sample size must be

  1. multiplied by 4
  2. doubled
  3. halved
  4. multiplied by \(\sqrt2\)

\(\operatorname{sd} \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt n}\): \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{4n}} = \frac12 \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt n}\).

Which is most likely to produce a biased estimate of the proportion of Year 12 students who drink coffee daily?

  1. A random sample of 50 Year 12 students from the school roll
  2. A random sample of 200 Year 12 students from the state
  3. A random sample of 5 classes, surveying every student in them
  4. Surveying students in the queue at the school café

People in a café queue are more likely than average to drink coffee: selection bias.

\(p = 0.2\) and \(n = 50\). Using the binomial distribution, \(P(\hat P \le 0.1)\) is closest to

  1. 0.0185
  2. 0.0480
  3. 0.0788
  4. 0.1

\(\hat P \le 0.1 \iff X \le 5\) with \(X \sim \text{Bi}(50, 0.2)\): \(P(X \le 5) \approx 0.0480\).

A statistic is

  1. a fixed number describing the whole population
  2. always equal to the parameter
  3. a value calculated from a sample, which varies between samples
  4. the standard deviation of the population

Parameters describe populations (fixed, usually unknown); statistics come from samples (random).

In a large population, 60% of people prefer brand A. For random samples of size 150, find the mean and standard deviation of \(\hat P\), to 4 decimal places.

\(E(\hat P) = 0.6\); \(\operatorname{sd}(\hat P) = \sqrt{\frac{0.6 \times 0.4}{150}} = \sqrt{0.0016} = 0.04\).

\(p = 0.6\), \(n = 150\). Using the normal approximation, find the probability that the sample proportion is within 0.05 of the population proportion.

\(P(0.55 < \hat P < 0.65)\) with \(\operatorname{sd} = 0.04\): \(z = \pm1.25\). [1]

\(P(-1.25 < Z < 1.25) \approx 0.7887\). [2]

A survey of \(n\) people is planned for a population with \(p = 0.4\). Find the smallest \(n\) for which \(\operatorname{sd}(\hat P) \le 0.02\).

\(\sqrt{\frac{0.24}{n}} \le 0.02 \Rightarrow \frac{0.24}{n} \le 0.0004\). [1]

\(n \ge 600\). [1] So \(n = 600\). [1]

A news website runs a poll: 12 000 readers vote and 78% say taxes are too high. Someone claims the huge sample makes the result very accurate. Respond.

It's a self-selected sample, not a random one. People with strong opinions (and readers of that site) are more likely to vote, so it's biased. A large sample reduces random error, not bias.

Summary

  • \(E(\hat P) = p\), \(\operatorname{sd}(\hat P) = \sqrt{\frac{p(1 - p)}{n}}\).
  • Large \(n\): approximately normal. Exact: convert to counts and use \(\text{Bi}(n, p)\).
  • Random sampling controls bias; sample size controls variability.
