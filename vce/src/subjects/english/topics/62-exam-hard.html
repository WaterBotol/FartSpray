English Language · Practice exam 3 (harder)

  • Reading time: 15 minutes. Writing time: 2 hours.
  • Section A: short-answer questions on Text 1 (15 marks). Section B: analytical commentary on Text 2 (30 marks). Section C: one essay from a choice of three (30 marks).
  • These texts are more complex than a typical VCAA exam. Both mix registers or languages, and the essay topics need a nuanced contention. 50/75 is a strong score.

Section A – Short-answer questions (15 marks)

Read Text 1 and answer all questions. Refer to line numbers where relevant.

Transcription key: (.) short pause · [ ] overlapping speech · = latching · ↑ rising intonation · CAPITALS louder speech · :: lengthened sound · (italic text in brackets) English gloss of Greek

Text 1. Three generations of a Greek-Australian family are finishing dinner at the grandmother’s house: Yiayia (grandmother), Eleni (her daughter) and Nick (Eleni’s 16-year-old son).

  1. Yiayia: Niko (.) ela (.) fae (come on, eat) (.) you’re too skinny
  2. Nick: Yiayia I’ve had like three plates=
  3. Eleni: =he’s had three plates Mama
  4. Yiayia: three plates (.) pff (.) your pappou (grandfather) ate FIVE
  5. Nick: (laughs) yeah and look how that turned out
  6. Eleni: Niko::
  7. Nick: what↑ I’m joking (.) I’m joking
  8. Yiayia: ti leei↑ (what’s he saying)
  9. Eleni: tipota (nothing) Mama (.) he says the food is beautiful
  10. Nick: I did not [say that]
  11. Eleni: [you did now]
  12. Nick: (laughs) okay okay (.) Yiayia (.) einai poli oraio (it’s really nice) (.) it’s really good
  13. Yiayia: bravo agapi mou (my love) (.) see (.) he speaks Greek when he wants something
  14. Eleni: he wants the car on Saturday
  15. Nick: MUM
  16. Yiayia: (laughs) the car↑ (.) in my day we walked to the village
  17. Nick: yeah but the village didn’t have a Westfield
  18. Eleni: (laughs) he’s got an answer for everything this one
  19. Yiayia: like his mother

Questions 1–6 refer to Text 1.

Identify two instances of code-switching in Text 1 and explain the function of one.

  • Two instances with line references, e.g. Yiayia’s “ela (.) fae” (line 1), Eleni’s “tipota Mama” (line 9), Nick’s “einai poli oraio” (line 12), “bravo agapi mou” (line 13)
  • Function of one explained, e.g. Nick switches to Greek in line 12 to accommodate to (converge with) his grandmother and please her; Eleni switches to Greek in line 9 to address her mother directly and mediate

Nick’s “einai poli oraio” (line 12) and Eleni’s “tipota Mama” (line 9). Nick’s switch is a strategic convergence towards his grandmother: complimenting her food in her first language shows respect and affection, and, as she notes, helps him get what he wants.

In line 9, Eleni tells Yiayia that Nick “says the food is beautiful”. Explain the pragmatic function of this reformulation.

  • It replaces Nick’s joke about pappou (line 5) with a compliment, a face-saving move that protects Yiayia (and the memory of pappou) from offence
  • It positions Eleni as the family mediator/translator between generations, and creates humour for Nick, who hears the “mistranslation”

Eleni deliberately “mistranslates”, swapping Nick’s cheeky joke about his grandfather for a compliment. This protects Yiayia’s face and keeps the peace, casting Eleni as the bilingual mediator between generations. It also teases Nick, who protests in line 10.

Explain how meaning is conveyed through prosody in line 6.

  • The lengthened vowel in “Niko::” (with no further words) signals disapproval/a warning
  • It is an implicit directive (to stop the joke), with the vocative name and intonation carrying the meaning instead of an explicit reprimand

Eleni says only his name, with a lengthened final vowel. The prosody alone carries the reprimand, an indirect directive to stop, which Nick immediately understands (“I’m joking”, line 7).

Explain what the terms of address in Text 1 show about the relationships between the speakers.

  • Examples: “Mama” (lines 3, 9), “Yiayia” (lines 2, 12), “Niko” (lines 1, 6), “agapi mou” (line 13), “MUM” (line 15)
  • They mark kinship roles across three generations; the Greek kin terms and vocative “Niko” express intimacy and cultural identity, while Nick uses English “Mum” with his mother, showing different language choices in different relationships

Greek kinship terms (“Mama”, “Yiayia”) and the Greek vocative “Niko” mark close family ties and a shared heritage, and Yiayia’s endearment “agapi mou” shows affection. Nick calls his mother “MUM” in English, which reflects the different language each relationship is conducted in.

Compare the overlap in lines 10–11 with the latching in lines 2–3.

  • Lines 10–11: a competitive, playful overlap, with Nick contradicting and Eleni talking over him to insist (teasing)
  • Lines 2–3: Eleni latches onto Nick’s turn to support him, repeating his words for Yiayia (a cooperative alignment)

In lines 2–3 Eleni latches on to support Nick, repeating his claim to Yiayia (cooperative). In lines 10–11 the overlap is competitive but playful: Nick denies the compliment and Eleni talks over him to insist, keeping the teasing going.

Discuss how the participants use language to negotiate family roles and identities in Text 1.

  • Relevant roles and identities discussed: Yiayia as caring authority/keeper of heritage; Eleni as bilingual mediator; Nick as an Australian teenager of Greek heritage
  • Supported by features with line references, e.g. Yiayia’s imperatives (line 1) and evaluations; Eleni’s translation and teasing (lines 9, 14); Nick’s strategic code-switch (line 12) and Yiayia’s metalinguistic comment (line 13); the contrast between the village and Westfield (lines 16–17)
  • Accurate metalanguage (e.g. code-switching, convergence, accommodation, face, identity)

Yiayia performs the traditional role of carer and authority through imperatives in Greek (“ela (.) fae”, line 1) and her evaluation of Nick’s eating (line 4). Eleni moves between both languages as mediator, softening Nick’s joke (line 9), while also joining in the teasing (line 14). Nick’s identity is bicultural: his casual English (“like three plates”, line 2) and his reference to Westfield (line 17) mark him as an Australian teenager, yet he converges to Greek strategically (line 12). Yiayia’s metalinguistic comment “he speaks Greek when he wants something” (line 13) shows everyone is aware that language choice is a way of negotiating relationships.

The strongest answers recognised that identity is performed and negotiated here, not simply displayed, and used Yiayia’s comment in line 13 as evidence.

Section B – Analytical commentary (30 marks)

Read Text 2 and write an analytical commentary.

Text 2. This summary appears when new users sign up to Pixelfort, a fictional game-building platform popular with teenagers. It is displayed in the app before the user taps “I agree”.

  1. Our Terms, Explained (the short version)
  2. Hey! Before you jump in, we want you to know what you’re agreeing to.
  3. We’ve written this summary so you don’t have to wade through pages of legalese, but it doesn’t replace our full Terms of Service, which still apply.
  4. 1. You need to be 13 or older.
  5. If you’re under 18, you’ll need a parent or guardian to read this with you and agree on your behalf.
  6. 2. Your stuff is yours (mostly).
  7. You own the levels, skins and artwork you create.
  8. However, by uploading them, you grant Pixelfort a worldwide, royalty-free licence to use, display and adapt your content across our services.
  9. In plain terms: we can show your creations to other players and feature them in promotions, without paying you.
  10. 3. Play nice.
  11. Harassment, hate speech and cheating aren’t tolerated. Full stop.
  12. Accounts that break these rules may be suspended or permanently terminated, at our discretion.
  13. 4. About in-game purchases.
  14. Gems are a virtual currency. They have no real-world value and can’t be exchanged for cash.
  15. All purchases are final, except where the law says otherwise.
  16. 5. Your data.
  17. We collect information about how you play so we can improve the game and show you relevant offers.
  18. You can change your privacy settings any time in the app. 🔒
  19. 6. Things change.
  20. We may update these terms from time to time. If we make a big change, we’ll let you know. If you keep playing, you’re agreeing to the new version.
  21. Questions? We’re always happy to chat. Hit the Help button in the app.
  22. Now go build something amazing! 🚀

Write an analytical commentary on the language features of Text 2. In your response you should comment on the contextual factors affecting the text’s content and form, the social purpose and register of the text, and the stylistic and discourse features of the text. Refer to the line numbers of the text.

  • Context: written online text in an app (mode), from a company to young users and their parents; legal field; significant power imbalance, with users needing to tap “I agree” to proceed
  • Social purpose and functions: to inform users of their rights and obligations (a legal/transactional function), to reassure and encourage acceptance, to build a friendly brand relationship, and to protect the company’s interests
  • Register identified as a deliberate hybrid (conversational framing of formal legal content), justified with examples from both registers
  • Lexis and semantics: colloquial lexis (“Hey!”, “stuff”, “Play nice”, “Full stop”, “wade through”, “legalese”) versus legal lexis (“worldwide, royalty-free licence”, “terminated”, “at our discretion”, “on your behalf”); hedging/understatement “(mostly)” (line 6) minimising a significant transfer of rights; the euphemism “relevant offers” (line 17) for targeted advertising
  • Syntax: imperatives (“Play nice”, “Hit the Help button”, “go build”); contractions and fragments (“Full stop.”, “Questions?”); the shift to a long legal sentence in line 8; agentless passive “may be suspended or permanently terminated” (line 12); conditionals, including the implied-consent clause “If you keep playing, you’re agreeing” (line 20)
  • Discourse: numbered headings with informal titles for navigation and cohesion; synthetic personalisation through direct address “you” and “we”; the metadiscourse “In plain terms:” (line 9) reformulating legal language; “However” (line 8) as a pivot from reassurance to obligation; emoji as paralinguistic cues (lines 18, 22); a friendly opening and closing framing the legal core
  • Pragmatics and power: rapport-building (positive politeness) masking an imbalance; modality contrasts, with “may” preserving company discretion (lines 12, 20) and “can’t/need to” constraining users (lines 5, 14); the tension between transparency (line 9) and minimisation (lines 6, 17)
  • Accurate, sophisticated metalanguage with quotations and line numbers
  • Insightful, well-structured commentary with fluent expression

Model introduction. Displayed in an app before new users tap “I agree”, this written text summarises a gaming platform’s terms of service for a largely teenage audience and their parents. It has a legal and transactional purpose (to inform users of binding obligations and protect the company), but it is framed as a friendly, persuasive conversation designed to reassure users and maintain a positive brand relationship. The resulting register is a deliberate hybrid: colloquial lexis, contractions and emoji frame content that remains formal, legal and weighted heavily in the company’s favour.

Model body paragraph (friendliness masking power). Synthetic personalisation pervades the text. The greeting “Hey!” (line 2), direct address “you” and the self-deprecating label “legalese” (line 3) construct the company as a peer rather than an institution. This positive politeness is most strategic where the content is most demanding. The heading “Your stuff is yours (mostly)” (line 6) uses informal lexis and a parenthetical hedge to minimise what line 8 reveals in unmistakably legal syntax: a long declarative with the dense noun phrase “a worldwide, royalty-free licence” and a triple verb phrase, “to use, display and adapt”. The discourse marker “However” (line 8) signals the pivot from reassurance to obligation, and the metadiscourse “In plain terms:” (line 9) seems to offer transparency, although its reformulation still frames the transfer of rights positively (“show your creations”, “feature them”).

Remaining paragraphs could cover: modality and power, with “may” preserving company discretion (lines 12, 20) while “need to/can’t” constrain users (lines 5, 14); the agentless passive in line 12; the implied-consent conditional (line 20); euphemism (“relevant offers”, line 17; “Things change”, line 19); imperatives and fragments (“Play nice”, “Full stop.”, “Questions?”); numbered headings as cohesive and navigational devices; emoji and the upbeat closing (line 22) returning to the friendly frame.

This text rewarded students who analysed the relationship between the two registers rather than listing informal and formal features separately. The best commentaries noticed that informality is concentrated exactly where the content is least favourable to users.

Section C – Essay (30 marks)

Choose one topic. Your essay should be supported by relevant examples and appropriate metalanguage.

Write an essay on one of the following topics. Refer to the stimulus material and to other relevant examples. Use appropriate metalanguage.

Topic 1. “Euphemism is the most powerful tool in formal public language.” Discuss.
Stimulus: “workforce optimisation” (job cuts) · “passed away” · “correctional facility” · “pre-loved” · “negative patient outcome” · “unauthorised access to customer data”

Topic 2. “Language change is not decay; it is a sign of a healthy community.” Discuss with reference to contemporary Australian English.
Stimulus: New words such as “doomscrolling” · singular “they” in official style guides · the intensifier “literally” · a letter to the editor lamenting “the death of grammar”

Topic 3. “Our identities are not fixed, and neither is our language.” Discuss.
Stimulus: A teenager who speaks differently with friends, with grandparents and in a job interview · an adult migrant whose accent changes over twenty years · online communities with their own vocabulary · people adopting new terms to describe themselves

Write your essay here, naming the topic you have chosen.

  • Addresses the chosen topic directly with a sophisticated, qualified contention sustained throughout
  • Wide-ranging, contemporary Australian examples beyond the stimulus, analysed precisely and insightfully
  • Accurate, integrated metalanguage and concepts (e.g. euphemism/dysphemism, doublespeak, prescriptivism, semantic change, accommodation, performativity)
  • Engages critically with the stimulus and with linguistic scholarship or public debate
  • Cohesive, controlled essay structure and expression

Topic 1 plan. Contention: euphemism is powerful because it shapes perception without appearing to, but its power depends on context, and overuse invites backlash (doublespeak is often exposed). Examples: corporate job-cut euphemisms; institutional euphemisms (“correctional facility”); euphemism for sensitivity and politeness (“passed away”) versus for evasion; data-breach statements; counterweights such as plain-language movements and dysphemism in political attack. Concepts: euphemism/dysphemism, doublespeak, face, nominalisation, agentless passive, connotation.

Topic 2 plan. Contention: largely agree. Change reflects social needs and inclusivity, although concerns about clarity and communication between generations are understandable. Examples: new coinages for new practices; singular “they” and inclusive language in the Australian Government Style Manual; semantic change (“literally” as an intensifier, like “very” historically); Aboriginal English and MAE contributing to Australian English; prescriptivist complaints. Concepts: semantic change (broadening, bleaching), neologism, prescriptivism vs descriptivism, codification.

Topic 3 plan. Contention: identity is performed and multiple, and speakers constantly adjust their language, though some features (e.g. an early-acquired accent) are harder to shift and are read by others as fixed. Examples: style-shifting across contexts; accent change through accommodation over time; online community lexicons; self-identification labels and their spread; code-switching in bilingual families. Concepts: accommodation (convergence/divergence), style-shifting, idiolect, sociolect, community of practice, performativity.

Model introduction (Topic 1). When a company announces “workforce optimisation”, few readers picture employees packing their desks, and that gap between words and reality is exactly the point. Euphemism is arguably the most powerful tool in formal public language because it reshapes perception quietly, through connotation, nominalisation and agentless constructions, rather than through open argument. Yet its power has limits. Australians are increasingly quick to label evasive language as spin, and plain-language expectations mean that euphemism which is exposed can damage the trust it was meant to protect.

The top essays argued with the topic rather than simply illustrating it, for example by showing where euphemism fails, or when change does cause communication problems.

Assessor’s report

General comments

This paper was set above the VCAA standard. Both texts mixed systems: two languages in Text 1, and two registers in Text 2. The essay topics demanded a qualified, argued position. The strongest scripts treated language choices as strategic acts in a relationship, not as features to label.

Section A

  • Code-switching answers needed a function tied to the moment, such as convergence to please Yiayia, mediation, or asserting authority. “To show they are Greek” was too general.
  • Question 2 separated the cohort. Only strong students saw Eleni’s “mistranslation” as a face-saving strategy.
  • In Question 5, many described both overlaps as “interruptions”. Comparing cooperative and competitive overlap was the point.

Section B

  • High-scoring commentaries analysed how the informal frame works on the legal content: where hedging appears, what “In plain terms” reformulates, and how modality distributes power.
  • Weaker responses praised the text as “easy to understand” without noticing what it minimised (“(mostly)”, “relevant offers”).
  • The implied-consent clause in line 20 and the modality contrast between “may” and “can’t” were the most commonly missed features.

Section C

  • Topic 1 essays that distinguished between euphemism for politeness and euphemism for evasion scored well.
  • Topic 2 rewarded examples of semantic change with specific linguistic description, not just lists of new words.
  • Topic 3 essays needed to address both halves of the statement, identity and language, and how each shapes the other.

Advice

Below 40/75: consolidate with the medium paper and focus on explaining function. Above 50: you are ready for anything VCAA sets. Refresh your bank of recent Australian examples before the exam.
