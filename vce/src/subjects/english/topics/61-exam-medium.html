English Language · Practice exam 2 (medium)

  • Reading time: 15 minutes. Writing time: 2 hours.
  • Section A: short-answer questions on Text 1 (15 marks). Section B: analytical commentary on Text 2 (30 marks). Section C: one essay from a choice of three (30 marks).
  • Refer to line numbers and use accurate metalanguage.

Section A – Short-answer questions (15 marks)

Read Text 1 and answer all questions. Refer to line numbers where relevant.

Transcription key: (.) short pause · (1.5) pause in seconds · [ ] overlapping speech · = latching · ↑ rising intonation · CAPITALS louder speech · :: lengthened sound

Text 1. A caller rings a local community radio breakfast program to promote a fundraiser. The segment is broadcast live.

  1. Dave: and we’ve got Sharon on the line from Mornington (.) morning Sharon how are ya
  2. Sharon: morning Dave (.) yeah good thanks (.) love the show
  3. Dave: aw thank you (.) so you’re ringing about the big fundraiser↑
  4. Sharon: yes so (.) the Mornington Magpies (.) that’s the junior footy club (.) we’re having a trivia night on the fourteenth
  5. Dave: love a trivia night
  6. Sharon: well it’s it’s actually our biggest fundraiser of the year (.) last year we raised (.) um (.) nearly eight thousand
  7. Dave: EIGHT thousand↑ [that’s huge]
  8. Sharon: [yeah] and that paid for all the new jumpers and the (.) the goalposts and stuff
  9. Dave: mm
  10. Sharon: so this year we’re hoping to sort of beat that (.) for lights on the oval
  11. Dave: now I’ve gotta ask (.) are you writing the questions yourself↑
  12. Sharon: (laughs) no no (.) we’ve got a (.) one of the dads is a teacher so he’s doing it
  13. Dave: smart move (.) teachers know how to make it hard don’t they
  14. Sharon: oh he’s ruthless (laughs)
  15. Dave: (laughs) and where can people get tickets
  16. Sharon: on the club website or just come down Saturday mornings to the canteen
  17. Dave: beautiful (.) look (.) good luck to the Magpies (.) thanks for calling in Sharon
  18. Sharon: thanks Dave (.) bye
  19. Dave: and if you’ve got a community event coming up (.) give us a bell on the studio line

Questions 1–6 refer to Text 1.

Explain the function of lines 1 and 17 in the structure of the conversation.

  • Line 1 is the opening sequence (introducing the caller to listeners, a greeting and phatic “how are ya”); line 17 begins the closing sequence (evaluation, well-wishes, thanks)
  • Both are managed by Dave, reflecting the host’s role in controlling the start and end of each segment

Line 1 opens the segment: Dave introduces the caller to the listeners and adds a phatic greeting. Line 17 starts the closing sequence with an evaluation (“beautiful”), well-wishes and thanks. The host controls both, as his role requires.

Identify the feature in “it’s it’s” (line 6) and “we’ve got a (.) one of the dads” (line 12), and explain why it occurs here.

  • Repetition and a false start with self-repair
  • It reflects spontaneous, unplanned speech produced in real time (live on air, possibly with some nervousness)

A repetition (line 6) and a false start followed by self-repair (line 12). They show that Sharon is planning her speech as she goes, live on air.

How does Dave control the topic of the conversation? Refer to two lines.

  • Uses questions to introduce or shift topics (lines 3, 11, 15)
  • Uses discourse markers such as “now” (line 11) and “look” (line 17) to signal a topic shift or the move to close, consistent with his institutional role as host

Dave’s questions move the talk along: the fundraiser (line 3), who writes the questions (line 11) and tickets (line 15). The discourse marker “now” (line 11) signals a topic shift, and “look” (line 17) signals the move to close.

Identify the syntactic feature at the end of line 13 and explain its function.

  • Tag question (“don’t they”)
  • It invites agreement and continues the shared joke, building rapport (positive politeness) and prompting Sharon’s response

A tag question. It invites Sharon to agree with his light-hearted claim about teachers, extending the joke and building rapport, which she takes up in line 14.

Explain the function of two prosodic features in line 7.

  • Increased volume on “EIGHT” emphasises surprise/admiration
  • Rising intonation turns the repetition into an echo question expressing enthusiasm and positively evaluating the club’s achievement (supportive, entertaining for listeners)

The louder “EIGHT” and the rising intonation turn Dave’s echo of Sharon’s figure into an expression of surprise and admiration. This positively evaluates the club’s effort and keeps the segment lively for listeners.

Discuss how the language of Text 1 reflects its context as a live community radio segment.

  • Relevant contextual factors: host/caller roles, an overhearing listening audience, a live broadcast, a local community setting, a promotional purpose
  • Linked to language features with line references, e.g. informal Australian lexis (“how are ya”, “give us a bell”, lines 1, 19); explicit clarification for listeners (“that’s the junior footy club”, line 4); practical information (tickets, line 16); humour for entertainment (lines 13–15); an efficient closing
  • Accurate metalanguage (e.g. phatic talk, overhearing audience, adjacency pairs, discourse markers)

The segment is shaped by its live audience. Sharon clarifies for listeners who the Magpies are (line 4) and gives practical ticket details (line 16), serving the promotional purpose. Dave’s informal Australian lexis (“how are ya”, line 1; “give us a bell”, line 19) and his friendly humour (lines 13–15) suit a relaxed community program and build rapport. As host he manages the structure: an opening sequence, topic-shifting questions, and a brisk closing that moves on to the next item for his listeners (line 19).

Top answers linked each feature to a specific aspect of the context. Many ignored the overhearing audience, which explains much of the language.

Section B – Analytical commentary (30 marks)

Read Text 2 and write an analytical commentary.

Text 2. The chief executive of Southwind Telecom (a fictional company) delivered this scripted statement in a video posted on the company’s website and social media channels, and shared by news outlets, after a data breach.

  1. Good evening.
  2. My name is Karen Liu, and I am the Chief Executive Officer of Southwind Telecom.
  3. Last Tuesday, we became aware that an unauthorised third party had accessed a database containing the personal information of some of our customers.
  4. I want to begin by saying something very simply: I am sorry.
  5. You trusted us with your information, and we did not protect it as we should have.
  6. Here is what we know.
  7. The affected database contained names, dates of birth, phone numbers and email addresses.
  8. For a smaller number of customers, it also contained driver licence numbers.
  9. No passwords, bank details or credit card information were accessed.
  10. Every affected customer will be contacted directly by email or text message within the next forty-eight hours.
  11. Please be aware that Southwind will never ask you for your password or banking details by phone, text or email.
  12. If you receive such a request, it is not from us.
  13. Here is what we are doing.
  14. We have secured the system, and we are working closely with the Australian Federal Police and the Office of the Australian Information Commissioner.
  15. We will cover the cost of replacing any driver licence that was exposed.
  16. And we will provide twelve months of free identity-protection services to every affected customer.
  17. We have also engaged independent experts to review every part of our security, and we will publish their findings.
  18. I know that words are not enough.
  19. Trust is earned through actions, and over the coming months, we intend to earn yours back.
  20. If you have questions or concerns, our dedicated support team is available around the clock on the free-call number listed on our website.
  21. Thank you for listening, and again, I am truly sorry.

Write an analytical commentary on the language features of Text 2. In your response you should comment on the contextual factors affecting the text’s content and form, the social purpose and register of the text, and the stylistic and discourse features of the text. Refer to the line numbers of the text.

  • Context: formal, scripted spoken text broadcast as a video (mode); from a CEO to customers, with a wider overhearing audience (media, regulators, the public); produced after a crisis that damaged the relationship
  • Social purpose and functions: to apologise, inform, reassure and advise customers, and to repair the company’s reputation and customers’ trust
  • Register identified as formal yet personal and direct, justified (e.g. title and name, line 2; first-person “I am sorry”, line 4; plain everyday lexis)
  • Lexis and semantics: euphemistic, technical framing of the incident (“an unauthorised third party had accessed”, line 3) contrasted with the plain apology; lexical field of trust and security (“trusted”, “protect”, “secured”, “Trust”); the nominal group “affected customers”; positive lexis of commitment (“dedicated”, “independent”)
  • Syntax: short simple declaratives for clarity and sincerity (lines 4, 6, 13, 18); listing (lines 7, 9); high-modality “will” commitments (lines 10, 15–17); imperative “Please be aware” (line 11); passive voice in lines 9–10 versus active acceptance of responsibility in line 5 (“we did not protect it”)
  • Discourse: the parallel signposts “Here is what we know / Here is what we are doing” (lines 6, 13) structuring the statement; framing repetition of the apology (lines 4, 21); direct address “you” and a mix of inclusive and exclusive “we”; antithesis of “words” and “actions” (lines 18–19); sentence-initial “And” (line 16) for emphasis
  • Pragmatics: face-repair strategies (explicit apology, admission of fault, concrete redress); negation to limit perceived harm (line 9); establishing authority and credibility (line 2; naming the AFP and OAIC, line 14)
  • Accurate metalanguage, with quotations and line numbers throughout
  • Well-structured commentary with fluent, precise expression

Model introduction. Delivered by Southwind’s chief executive in a scripted video after a data breach, this formal spoken text addresses customers whose trust has been damaged, while also speaking to an overhearing audience of journalists, regulators and the wider public. Its purposes are to apologise, inform and reassure, and ultimately to repair the company’s reputation. The register is formal but deliberately personal: plain lexis, first-person apology and direct address try to close the social distance a corporation usually keeps.

Model body paragraph (managing responsibility). The text carefully balances technical distance with personal accountability. The breach is first described through a euphemistic, formal noun phrase, “an unauthorised third party had accessed a database” (line 3), whose Latinate lexis and neutral verb “accessed” (rather than “stole” or “hacked”) soften the incident. The very next line, however, shifts to the plainest possible first-person declarative: “I am sorry” (line 4). Its brevity signals sincerity, and it is followed by an active-voice admission, “we did not protect it as we should have” (line 5), which places the company in the subject position and accepts agency. Passive constructions return where they serve reassurance, not evasion: “No passwords … were accessed” (line 9) and “Every affected customer will be contacted” (line 10) keep the focus on the customers’ situation.

Remaining paragraphs could cover: the parallel signposts “Here is what we know / Here is what we are doing” (lines 6, 13) and the listing (lines 7–9) giving clarity; high-modality “will” commitments (lines 15–17) and the sentence-initial “And” (line 16); the imperative and security advice (lines 11–12); naming the AFP and OAIC for credibility (line 14); the antithesis in lines 18–19; the framing repetition of the apology (lines 4, 21); direct address “you”, and the shift between inclusive and exclusive “we”.

The best responses noticed the tension between the euphemistic opening (line 3) and the plain apology (line 4), and saw how passive voice is used differently at different points. Avoid claiming that the passive “always hides responsibility”. Explain what it does in each place.

Section C – Essay (30 marks)

Choose one topic. Your essay should be supported by relevant examples and appropriate metalanguage.

Write an essay on one of the following topics. Refer to the stimulus material and to other relevant examples. Use appropriate metalanguage.

Topic 1. “Online communication is making informal language acceptable everywhere.” Discuss.
Stimulus: Emails to teachers that begin “hey”; emoji reactions in workplace chat; a government agency’s playful social-media posts; “lol” used to soften criticism in text messages

Topic 2. “The way we speak can both reveal and conceal who we are.” Discuss.
Stimulus: A student who switches between her family’s language at home and Standard Australian English at school; a newsreader who has modified her accent; features of Multicultural Australian English among young people in Sydney and Melbourne; online usernames and personas

Topic 3. “There is no single ‘correct’ Australian English.” To what extent do you agree?
Stimulus: Broad, General and Cultivated accents; Aboriginal English; a letter to the editor complaining about “lazy” pronunciation; dictionaries adding new Australian words each year

Write your essay here, naming the topic you have chosen.

  • Addresses the chosen topic directly with a clear, nuanced contention
  • Several relevant, contemporary Australian examples beyond the stimulus, analysed precisely
  • Accurate, integrated metalanguage and concepts (e.g. code-switching, accommodation, covert/overt prestige, prescriptivism/descriptivism, register)
  • Engages with the stimulus and with linguistic ideas and current debates
  • Cohesive structure and controlled, fluent expression

Topic 1 plan. Contention: online norms are spreading informality into more contexts, but register awareness persists; what is changing is where the boundaries sit, not whether they exist. Examples: casual email openings and teachers’ reactions; emoji and reaction buttons as pragmatic softeners in workplaces; institutions deliberately adopting informal social-media voices (synthetic personalisation) to build rapport; formal contexts where informality is still sanctioned (job applications, court, government reports). Concepts: register, social distance, synthetic personalisation, face, genre conventions.

Topic 2 plan. Contention: language inevitably signals identity, but speakers actively manage what it reveals. Examples: code-switching between a heritage language and SAE; accent modification for professional roles (convergence to overt prestige); MAE as an expression of multicultural youth identity (covert prestige) and stereotyping of its speakers; online personas and pseudonyms concealing age, gender or location. Concepts: code-switching, accommodation (convergence/divergence), overt/covert prestige, ethnolect, idiolect, face.

Topic 3 plan. Contention: largely agree. Linguistically, all varieties are rule-governed, though Standard Australian English retains social and institutional prestige. Examples: the shift in prestige from Cultivated towards General accents; Aboriginal English as a legitimate, systematic dialect; ethnolects; prescriptivist complaints about “lazy” speech versus descriptivist dictionaries recording change. Concepts: prescriptivism, descriptivism, standard vs non-standard, prestige, dialect, codification.

Model introduction (Topic 3). Letters to the editor still complain about “lazy” pronunciation, yet the Australians reading them speak with a range of accents, dialects and ethnolects, each governed by its own consistent rules. Linguistically, there is no single “correct” Australian English. There is a standard variety that holds institutional prestige, and many others that carry identity, community and history. This essay argues that “correctness” is a social judgement rather than a linguistic fact, while acknowledging that Standard Australian English remains the expected variety in formal contexts.

Topic 3 attracted the most responses. The strongest separated linguistic validity from social prestige, instead of claiming either that “anything goes” or that SAE is simply “correct”.

Assessor’s report

General comments

This paper was at VCAA standard. Text 1 required students to connect features to a specific context, a live broadcast with an overhearing audience. Text 2 rewarded a nuanced reading of how a corporate apology manages responsibility. The essay topics required a contention that went beyond agreeing or disagreeing.

Section A

  • Many answers identified features correctly but explained them generically. “Because it is spoken” doesn’t explain why Sharon repeats herself. Being live on air does.
  • In Question 3, the discourse markers “now” and “look” were often missed. They are the host’s tools for managing the conversation.

Section B

  • High-scoring commentaries tracked how the text moves, from technical distance (line 3) to personal accountability (lines 4–5), then clarity (lines 6–12), commitment (lines 13–17) and reconciliation (lines 18–21).
  • Commentaries that claimed every passive “avoids blame” were marked down. In lines 9–10 the passive puts customers, not the company, in focus.
  • Few students discussed the overhearing audience (media, regulators), which explains the naming of the AFP and OAIC.

Section C

  • Balance and nuance distinguished the top essays. Qualified contentions (“largely agree, but…”) handled counter-examples well.
  • Examples from recent Australian public discourse were more persuasive than generic or overseas examples.

Advice

Aim for 55/75 or more before the harder paper. If your Section B was under 20/30, practise writing one well-developed analytical paragraph a day on short public texts: speeches, media releases, apologies.
