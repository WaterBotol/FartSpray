English Language · real exam sections, medium

Every section here is a real VCAA exam section. English papers can’t be mixed question by question, because each section is built around one text, so the sections were ranked instead: for each of Section A, B and C, the three most recent exams (2023, 2024, 2025) were ordered by the state average. This paper uses Section A from 2025, Section B from 2025 and Section C from 2024.

Be warned: English results barely move from year to year (section averages range from about 44% to 58%), so the three English papers are much closer in difficulty than the science and maths ones. The biggest difference is in Section A.

VCAA publishes Sections B and C on a 0–15 scale. The exam paper weights each at 30 marks, so the state averages are doubled here. The texts and exact questions are in the official PDFs (VCAA copyright), so open them with the links.

How the state went on these exact sections: an average of 40 / 75 (53%).

Section A – Short-answer questions (15 marks)

All questions refer to Text 1 in the insert of the 2025 exam. Open the insert and each question with the links.

2025 VCAA exam · Insert, Text 1 Open in the exam PDF (p. 30) ↗

Text 1: an email from the head of a reusable-bottle company to a customer

State average for this section: 6.9 / 15 (46%).

Identify one modal verb between lines 29 and 39 and describe how it supports a purpose of the text (metalanguage). (p. 3 ↗)

  • An accurate modal verb from the line range (e.g. “will”, line 32; “couldn’t”, line 37; quasi-modal “going to”, line 34)
  • Identifies a valid purpose (e.g. build rapport, reduce social distance, attend to face needs)
  • Explains how the modal supports that purpose (e.g. high modality signals a firm commitment)

“We will continue…” (32): high modality presents the company’s commitment as certain, building trust and rapport with the customer. Revise which words are modals, because many picked non-modals.

State average 1.9 / 3 (63%); 48% scored full marks.

Mark distribution: 0: 26% · 1: 4% · 2: 22% · 3: 48%

Identify two examples of information flow between lines 20 and 33, and describe the impact of one (metalanguage). (p. 3 ↗)

  • First accurate example of information flow (front or end focus) from the line range
  • Second accurate example
  • Describes the impact of one (e.g. places key information in prominence to support a purpose, or builds cohesion)

For example, fronted adverbials such as “Alongside [partner]” (20) and “With your incredible support” (27) put the partnership and the customer in front focus, foregrounding shared effort. 67% scored zero, mostly from misidentifying information flow.

State average 0.8 / 3 (27%); 13% scored full marks.

Mark distribution: 0: 67% · 1: 6% · 2: 13% · 3: 13%

Identify one semantic pattern and one syntactic pattern and explain how each reflects a different function of the text (Jakobson). (p. 4 ↗)

  • A semantic pattern identified and labelled as semantic (e.g. metaphor “made waves”, 21, or “at the heart”, 45)
  • Linked to one Jakobson function with explanation
  • A syntactic pattern identified and labelled as syntactic (e.g. listing of new products, lines 10–11)
  • Linked to a different function with explanation

Listing (syntactic) serves the referential function by cataloguing products. A metaphor (semantic) serves the poetic or conative function. Name the subsystem as well as the pattern.

State average 2.2 / 4 (55%); 16% scored full marks.

Mark distribution: 0: 16% · 1: 11% · 2: 34% · 3: 23% · 4: 16%

Using at least three examples of the writer’s language, analyse how register achieves a range of purposes and intents, with reference to cultural context (line numbers). (p. 5 ↗)

  • Characterises the register accurately (a shifting mix of formal and informal)
  • Example 1 analysed and linked to a purpose/intent
  • Example 2 analysed and linked to a purpose/intent
  • Example 3 analysed and linked to a purpose/intent
  • Links to cultural context (values of the company and the customer, e.g. sustainability)

Formal language lends legitimacy and prestige, while informal touches humanise the company and personalise the email. Connect the examples to each other and to the shared value of sustainability.

State average 2.0 / 5 (40%); 0.5% scored full marks.

Mark distribution: 0: 10% · 1: 20% · 2: 38% · 3: 25% · 4: 6% · 5: 0.5%

Section B – Analytical commentary (30 marks)

Write an analytical commentary on Text 2 in the insert of the 2025 exam: function, purpose and intent; situational and cultural context; register, tenor and audience; and relevant language features from at least two subsystems.

2025 VCAA exam · Section B · Question 5 Open in the exam PDF (p. 6) ↗

Text 2: a transcript of an Indigenous surgeon’s award acceptance speech to a live audience

Insert: Text 2 ↗

State average for this section: 16.4 / 30 (55%).

Analytical commentary on Text 2

  • Function(s), purpose(s) and intent(s), situational and cultural context, and register, tenor and audience are analysed accurately and specifically for this text
  • Salient language features from at least two subsystems are analysed with quoted examples and line numbers, each explicitly linked to function, context or register
  • Precise, accurate metalanguage throughout
  • A coherent, sustained commentary (not a feature list), with clear, controlled expression

Rich in identity features: the surgeon’s down-to-earth humility, occupational identity, Indigenous identity, wider Australian identity and family identity. Choose the most salient features and link each to function, purpose, context, register and audience, rather than listing features by subsystem.

Section C – Essay (30 marks)

Write one essay on one of the three topics. Open the topic in the official exam for the exact wording and stimulus material.

2024 VCAA exam · Section C (choose one of Questions 7, 8, 9) Open in the exam PDF (p. 14) ↗

Choose one topic.

  1. Question 7. Language defines Australian identity, and its use keeps changing: discuss. exact wording and stimulus (p. 14) ↗
  2. Question 8. Our choice of variety of Australian English can both support and undermine our purposes in interactions: discuss. exact wording and stimulus (p. 16) ↗
  3. Question 9. To what extent do register and politeness go hand in hand with social standing in contemporary Australia? exact wording and stimulus (p. 18) ↗

State average for this section: 16.8 / 30 (56%).

Essay (name the topic you chose)

  • A clear, sustained and nuanced contention that answers the exact wording of the topic (including “to what extent” where asked)
  • Relevant, contemporary Australian examples discussed in depth, drawing on at least two subsystems of language, with thoughtful use of the stimulus
  • Accurate linguistic concepts and metalanguage
  • Cohesive essay structure and fluent, controlled expression

Briefly contextualise each example. Essays with weak introductions rarely recovered, while clear, planned contentions scored well. Topic choice across the state: Q7 59%, Q8 29%, Q9 11%.

Assessor’s report

How Victoria went on this paper

These are the real published results for each section in the year it was set, from the VCAA examiners’ reports. A student at the state average in every section would score 40 / 75 (53%): Section A 46%, Section B 55%, Section C 56%.

Short answers: where marks were lost

  • Q2 (Identify two examples of information flow between lines 20 and 33, and describe the impact of one (metalanguage).): 27% of available marks
  • Q4 (Using at least three examples of the writer’s language, analyse how register achieves a range of purposes and intents, with reference to cultural context (line numbers).): 40% of available marks
  • Q3 (Identify one semantic pattern and one syntactic pattern and explain how each reflects a different function of the text (Jakobson).): 55% of available marks
  • Q1 (Identify one modal verb between lines 29 and 39 and describe how it supports a purpose of the text (metalanguage).): 63% of available marks

What the examiners kept saying

  • Short answers: quote precisely, give line numbers, and do every part the question asks. Five-mark questions usually need several features and their links to context or identity.
  • Commentary: choose the most salient features, and link each to function, context or register. Summary and subsystem-by-subsystem listing were marked down.
  • Essay: unpack every key word of the topic in the introduction, use contemporary Australian examples, and don’t pad with misattributed quotations.
