A dialect, not “broken English”

Aboriginal English is a group of dialects of English spoken by many Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people. It is rule-governed, with systematic features across every subsystem, and it varies by region, from forms close to Standard Australian English to forms closer to Kriol. It carries Aboriginal cultural knowledge and is a strong marker of identity.

Traditional languages: more than 250 distinct languages (with many dialects) were spoken at the time of colonisation; only a small number are still learned by children today, and many communities are actively reviving their languages. Kriol: a separate creole language spoken widely across northern Australia. Aboriginal English: dialects of English. Don't use these terms interchangeably.

Features

SubsystemExamples (vary by region and speaker)
PhonologyVariable /h/ (dropped or added); /θ/ and /ð/ realised as /t/ and /d/ (“tink”, “dat”); some varieties merge sounds such as /p/ and /b/
Lexicon and semanticsdeadly (excellent), mob (family or community group), yarn (talk, story), gammon (joking, pretend), shame (embarrassment at being singled out), solid (good, genuine); extended kinship terms (aunty, uncle as terms of respect, cousin-brother); Country meaning ancestral land and connection
Morphology and syntaxPlural or past marking may be omitted when context makes it clear (“two dog”, “he go yesterday”); copula (be) may be absent (“she real deadly”); tags like eh or unna; im as a general third-person pronoun in some varieties
Discourse and pragmaticsYarning (relaxed, relational talk); indirectness, with information often shared through stories rather than direct questions; comfortable silence; avoiding singling people out

Identity, code-switching and attitudes

  • Many speakers code-switch between Aboriginal English (with family and community, expressing belonging) and Standard Australian English (in formal or institutional settings).
  • Words such as deadly have spread into general Australian English (the Deadly Awards celebrated Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander achievement from 1995 to 2013), a sign of growing public visibility.
  • Education: bidialectal approaches value Aboriginal English as a home dialect while adding Standard Australian English, rather than “correcting” students' identity.
  • Law: research (notably by linguist Diana Eades) shows that pragmatic differences, such as answering “yes” to questions to avoid conflict (gratuitous concurrence), or silence being misread as guilt, can disadvantage Aboriginal witnesses in court. This has led to interpreter use and guidelines for questioning.
  • Language revival: communities are reclaiming traditional languages through schools, dictionaries, place names (dual naming) and media. The Acknowledgement of Country and Welcome to Country often include language.
  • Describe; don't judge. Avoid “incorrect”, “slang” or “broken” for Aboriginal English features.
  • Don't generalise: there are many Aboriginal Englishes and many communities.
  • Aboriginal English isn't Kriol and isn't a traditional language.

Explain how Aboriginal English expresses Aboriginal identity, with two examples.

It encodes cultural concepts and relationships that matter in Aboriginal communities: kinship terms such as aunty and uncle show respect and extended family structures, and mob signals belonging to a family or community group. Using Aboriginal English with community members signals solidarity and connection to culture and Country.

Discuss how attitudes to Aboriginal English and Indigenous languages have changed, with examples.

Historically, Aboriginal languages were suppressed (e.g. on missions and in schools), and Aboriginal English was stigmatised as “bad English”. [1] Linguistic research established it as a rule-governed dialect, leading to bidialectal education and changes in legal practice (awareness of gratuitous concurrence, use of interpreters). [2] Today, language revival programs, dual place naming and the spread of words like deadly show greater recognition, though Standard Australian English still carries more overt prestige in institutions. Summary

  • Aboriginal English: rule-governed dialects of English and a key marker of identity.
  • Distinct from Kriol and from traditional languages.
  • Features across subsystems; pragmatic differences matter in education and law.
  • Attitudes have shifted from suppression towards recognition and revival.
