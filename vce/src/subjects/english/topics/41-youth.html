Why young people innovate

Adolescence is when people build identities separate from their parents, so youth language is a hotspot for innovation. Slang marks in-group membership, carries covert prestige, creates solidarity, excludes adults and other out-groups, and is simply playful and creative. Social media now spreads new words globally within weeks.

Oxford: rizz (2023), brain rot (2024), rage bait (2025). Dictionary.com chose 67 for 2025, a term with almost no fixed meaning, used mainly by Gen Alpha as an in-group joke. Collins chose brat (2024). These show dictionaries descriptively recording youth-driven change.

Digital conventions

  • Emoji and emoticons add tone and pragmatic meaning; their meanings shift by generation (💀 = “I'm dead (laughing)”; 😭 = laughing or overwhelmed; 🙂 read as passive-aggressive by younger users).
  • Tone indicators (/j joking, /s sarcastic, /srs serious) make pragmatic intent explicit in text.
  • Punctuation and case: all-lowercase for a casual, low-effort style; a full stop can read as cold or angry; lol and haha soften statements rather than signal laughter.
  • Non-standard spelling for voice and prosody: soooo, omg, ur, CAPS for volume.

Life cycle and attitudes

  • Rapid turnover: once parents, brands or politicians adopt a term, it loses covert prestige and becomes “cringe”. Lit and on fleek went this way.
  • Moral panic and the complaint tradition: headlines claim texting and slang are ruining literacy. Research (e.g. David Crystal's Txtng: The Gr8 Db8, 2008) found texting abbreviations were less common than claimed and that texters need good literacy to play with language.
  • Appropriation debate: many “Gen Z slang” words come from African American English and queer ballroom culture (slay, tea, shade). Using them without awareness of origin is criticised, and the “internet slang” label can erase their source communities.
  • Age-grading: speakers use most slang during adolescence and drop it in adulthood, so not every youth word enters the standard. Some do: cool, OK and awesome were once slang.
  • Don't just list slang: name the word-formation process and the function (solidarity, identity, exclusion).
  • Slang ≠ jargon. Jargon is specialist and occupational; slang is informal and group-based.
  • Double-check meanings: using a term wrongly in an essay undermines your credibility.
  • Be descriptive about “brain rot” and moral panics: discuss the attitudes, don't adopt them.

Identify two word-formation processes in youth slang and explain one social function of each.

Clipping (sus, delulu): creates short, casual words that suit fast digital communication and signal a relaxed in-group tone. [1.5] Semantic shift (slay, cooked): gives familiar words new meanings that only the in-group understands, creating solidarity and excluding outsiders. [1.5]

“Social media has changed the way young people use language.” Outline two points and evidence.

Speed and reach: TikTok and other platforms spread words globally within weeks (rizz, Oxford 2023; brain rot, Oxford 2024; 67, Dictionary.com 2025), accelerating turnover and the “cringe” cycle once adults adopt terms. [2]

New pragmatic conventions: emoji, tone indicators, lowercase and softening lol show young people creating detailed ways to signal tone in writing, and borrowing from AAVE and gaming shows cross-community contact, sometimes raising appropriation concerns. [2]

Summary

  • Youth language builds identity, solidarity and covert prestige, and excludes out-groups.
  • Processes: clipping, semantic shift, initialisms, borrowing, affixation, compounding.
  • Digital conventions carry pragmatic meaning; turnover is rapid.
  • Attitudes: moral panic vs descriptive evidence (age-grading, past slang becoming standard).
