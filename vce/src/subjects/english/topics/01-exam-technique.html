The three sections

SectionTaskSuggested time
A: Short answerQuestions on one text (often a transcript); identify, explain and analyse featuresabout 25 min
B: Analytical commentaryAn extended analysis of one text: context, purposes, register and featuresabout 50 min
C: EssayChoose one of several topics, each with stimulus; argue with metalanguage and contemporary examplesabout 45 min

Times are a guide for a 2-hour writing period; check current mark allocations on the VCAA website and adjust.

Using reading time

  • Read the essay topics first and choose one, so your brain can gather examples while you work on A and B.
  • Read the context lines for both texts: who, where, audience, mode, purpose.
  • Mentally annotate the commentary text: find three functions to organise your paragraphs.

Command words

WordWhat earns the marks
IdentifyThe precise term plus a quote and line number. No explanation needed.
DescribeWhat the feature is and how it appears in the text.
ExplainFeature plus its function or effect in this context.
AnalyseSeveral features, how they work together, and links to purpose, register and tenor.
DiscussConsider different aspects or views, with evidence, reaching a judgement.

Quoting and referencing

  • Quote the shortest part that shows the feature: “arvo (line 9)”, not the whole line.
  • Always give line numbers; for multiple lines use a range (lines 4–6).
  • Keep transcript symbols when they matter (“FOUR hundred↑”, line 4) because prosody is evidence.

The formula for analysis

Every analytical sentence

Metalanguage term + quote (line) + function in this context + link to purpose, tenor or register

Example: “The hypocorism arvo (line 9) keeps Tom's request casual, reflecting the equal, familiar tenor between housemates.”

Errors examiners' reports mention every year

  • Naming a feature without explaining its function in context.
  • Generic effects: “engages the audience”, “makes it flow”, “adds emphasis” with no link to purpose.
  • Mislabelling: calling any short word “slang”, any formal word “jargon”, or any repetition “anaphora”.
  • Confusing anaphoric reference with anaphora, or elision with ellipsis.
  • Treating the passive as always evasive, or formal texts as having no informal features.
  • Prescriptive judgements (“bad grammar”, “incorrect”) instead of descriptive terms (“non-standard”).
  • Essays that list examples without analysing their language, or that use inaccurate or invented examples.
  • Retelling the stimulus instead of using it as a launch pad.
