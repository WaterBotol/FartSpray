English Language · Practice exam 1 (easier)

  • Reading time: 15 minutes. Writing time: 2 hours.
  • Section A: short-answer questions on Text 1 (15 marks, about 25 minutes). Section B: analytical commentary on Text 2 (30 marks, about 45 minutes). Section C: one essay from a choice of three (30 marks, about 50 minutes).
  • Refer to line numbers. Use accurate metalanguage from the five subsystems: phonetics and phonology, morphology and lexicology, syntax, semantics, discourse and pragmatics.

Section A – Short-answer questions (15 marks)

Read Text 1 and answer all questions. Refer to line numbers where relevant.

Transcription key: (.) short pause · (1.5) pause in seconds · [ ] overlapping speech · = latching (no gap between turns) · ↑ rising intonation · CAPITALS louder speech · :: lengthened sound

Text 1. Mia and Tom, two Year 11 students, are chatting at lunchtime.

  1. Mia: so are you coming Saturday↑
  2. Tom: yeah (.) probably (.) depends if Mum lets me take the car
  3. Mia: oh my god you got your Ps↑
  4. Tom: yep (.) last Thursday [first go]
  5. Mia: [no WAY] that’s so good
  6. Tom: haha yeah I was kinda stressing but the guy was pretty chill
  7. Mia: so you can drive us=
  8. Tom: =wait wait who’s us
  9. Mia: me and Jess and um (.) maybe Priya
  10. Tom: (laughs) okay so I’m the taxi now
  11. Mia: you’re the taxi (.) we’ll shout you Maccas after
  12. Tom: deal (.) what time’s it start
  13. Mia: like seven-ish↑ but no one rocks up till eight
  14. Tom: yeah nah I’ll get you at eight then
  15. Mia: legend (.) oh and bring your speaker
  16. Tom: the big one↑
  17. Mia: yeah the big one (.) Jess’s is cooked
  18. Tom: sweet (.) I’ll text you tomorrow arvo

Questions 1–6 refer to Text 1.

Identify two features of Text 1 that show it is spontaneous spoken discourse. Give a line reference for each.

  • One valid feature with a line reference, e.g. micropauses (.) (line 2), filler “um” (line 9), overlapping speech (lines 4–5)
  • A second valid feature with a line reference, e.g. latching (lines 7–8), laughter (line 10), false start or repetition “wait wait” (line 8)

Micropauses, e.g. “yeah (.) probably (.)” (line 2), and the filler “um” (line 9), both of which show on-the-spot planning.

Lines 4–5 contain overlapping speech. Explain its function here.

  • Mia’s “no WAY” overlaps Tom’s turn: a cooperative (supportive) overlap, not a hostile interruption
  • It shows her enthusiasm and excitement about his news, building rapport (with the loud stress on “WAY”)

Mia’s “[no WAY]” overlaps Tom’s “[first go]”. This is a cooperative overlap that shows her excitement and support. It builds rapport rather than competing for the floor, and the increased volume on “WAY” adds emphasis.

Identify the word-formation process in “Maccas” (line 11) and “arvo” (line 18), and explain what their use suggests about the speakers’ relationship.

  • Hypocorism (shortening plus a suffix such as -s or -o), a feature of Australian English
  • Informal, casual lexis signals familiarity, solidarity and shared (Australian, peer-group) identity

Both are hypocorisms, formed by shortening and adding a suffix (McDonald’s → Maccas, afternoon → arvo). They are typical of informal Australian English and signal familiarity and a shared identity between the friends.

Identify the feature marked by “=” in lines 7–8 and explain its function.

  • Latching: Tom’s turn follows Mia’s with no gap
  • It shows his quick reaction/surprise and a fast pace, as he checks the request before accepting it (and shows engagement)

Latching: Tom begins immediately as Mia finishes. It conveys his surprise at being volunteered as the driver and lets him seek clarification (“who’s us”) before committing, while keeping the exchange fast and playful.

Explain the function of “yeah nah” in line 14.

  • A discourse particle typical of Australian English: “yeah” acknowledges or agrees with Mia’s point that no one arrives till eight
  • “nah” rejects the seven o’clock plan, so Tom politely declines the earlier time while showing he has listened (softening the disagreement)

“Yeah” acknowledges Mia’s comment that no one arrives until eight. “Nah” rejects the earlier time. The combination lets Tom change the plan politely while showing he has taken her point on board.

Discuss how Mia and Tom use language to maintain a positive relationship in this conversation. Refer to at least two language features, with line numbers.

  • Discusses at least two relevant features accurately, e.g. compliments/praise (“that’s so good” line 5, “legend” line 15); humour and laughter (line 10); reciprocity (“we’ll shout you Maccas” line 11); informal and slang lexis (“chill”, “cooked”); hedges (“probably”, “kinda”, “seven-ish”)
  • Explains the function of each (e.g. positive face/solidarity, softening a request, sharing a joke to reduce imposition), with line references
  • Uses accurate metalanguage (e.g. positive face, mitigation, solidarity, adjacency pair)

Mia protects Tom’s positive face with praise (“that’s so good”, line 5; “legend”, line 15), which builds warmth. When she asks him to drive, a significant imposition, she offsets it with a reciprocal offer (“we’ll shout you Maccas after”, line 11), and Tom turns the imposition into humour with laughter and “so I’m the taxi now” (line 10). Shared informal Australian lexis (“chill”, “cooked”, “arvo”) and quick, cooperative adjacency pairs (“bring your speaker” / “the big one↑”) reinforce their solidarity.

The best answers explained why each feature helps the relationship. Feature-spotting alone reached at most 2 marks.

Section B – Analytical commentary (30 marks)

Read Text 2 and write an analytical commentary.

Text 2. This article appeared in the fortnightly email newsletter of a Victorian secondary school.

  1. From the Principal: Towards a Phone-Free Learning Day
  2. Dear families,
  3. Over the past term, our staff, students and School Council have been discussing a question that affects every household in our community: how can we help young people learn well in a world that never stops buzzing?
  4. After extensive consultation, I am writing to let you know that from the start of Term 3, Wattle Park College will become a phone-free school between 8.45 am and 3.20 pm.
  5. Students will be asked to switch off their phones on arrival and store them in their lockers until the final bell.
  6. We recognise that this represents a significant change for many students.
  7. However, the evidence is compelling.
  8. Teachers report fewer distractions in class, and students themselves told us, in our wellbeing survey, that constant notifications make it harder to concentrate and to connect with friends face to face.
  9. Our aim is not to punish, but to protect: to give every student six hours each day in which their attention belongs to their learning and to the people around them.
  10. We also understand that families need to be able to contact their children.
  11. Should an urgent situation arise, please phone the General Office, and a message will be passed on immediately.
  12. Students with a documented medical or wellbeing need will, of course, be supported through an individual plan.
  13. I would like to thank our Year 10 Student Representative Council, whose thoughtful presentation to School Council shaped several aspects of this policy.
  14. Their proposal that lunchtime clubs be expanded, so that students have more to do rather than less, has been adopted in full.
  15. Change is rarely easy, and we expect there will be an adjustment period.
  16. We ask for your support in talking with your child about why this matters, and in modelling healthy phone habits at home.
  17. Together, I am confident we can make our school a place where students are fully present.
  18. Warm regards,
  19. Dr Helen Ashford, Principal

Write an analytical commentary on the language features of Text 2. In your response you should comment on the contextual factors affecting the text’s content and form, the social purpose and register of the text, and the stylistic and discourse features of the text. Refer to the line numbers of the text.

  • Context: written email newsletter (mode) from the principal to families (participants: institutional authority addressing a community whose cooperation is needed); field: a new school policy
  • Social purpose and functions: to inform families of the policy, persuade them of its benefits, and maintain a cooperative relationship/secure their support
  • Register identified as formal but warm and accessible, justified with examples (e.g. “Dear families” line 2, “Warm regards” line 18; formal lexis “consultation” line 4)
  • Lexis and semantics: lexical field of learning/wellbeing (“learn”, “concentrate”, “attention”, “wellbeing” lines 3, 8, 9); positive connotation (“protect”, “thoughtful”); “phone-free” (line 1, 4) as a positively framed alternative to “phone ban”
  • Syntax: complex sentences with subordination to pack in justification (line 8); a short declarative for emphasis (line 7); agentless passive to soften directives (“will be asked”, line 5; “will be passed on”, line 11); formal inverted conditional “Should an urgent situation arise” (line 11); antithesis “not to punish, but to protect” (line 9)
  • Discourse and cohesion: inclusive first-person plural (“we”, “our”, “Together” lines 3, 6, 17) to build solidarity; conjunctive adverbs “However” (line 7) and “also” (line 10); clear structure (question → announcement → justification → concession → thanks → call to action); rhetorical question (line 3)
  • Pragmatics and politeness: acknowledging difficulty and objections (lines 6, 10, 15) to protect negative face; mitigated request “We ask for your support” (line 16); praise of students (lines 13–14) as a positive-face strategy
  • Accurate metalanguage throughout, with precise quotations and line numbers
  • Well-organised commentary (overview, then paragraphs by function or subsystem) with fluent expression

Model introduction. This formal written text, a newsletter article sent by email from a school principal to families, announces a new phone-free policy. Its purpose is to inform, but equally to persuade parents that the change is beneficial and to secure their cooperation. Because the principal holds institutional authority but depends on the goodwill of families, the register is formal yet warm, and inclusive pronouns and politeness strategies soften what is essentially a directive.

Model body paragraph (persuasion through syntax). The principal justifies the policy through carefully structured syntax. The short declarative “However, the evidence is compelling” (line 7) follows the concession in line 6, signalling a turn in the argument and giving the claim emphasis through its brevity. It is followed by a long compound-complex sentence (line 8) that packs in two sources of evidence, teachers and students themselves, lending the policy credibility. The antithesis “not to punish, but to protect” (line 9) reframes a restriction as care, a positive semantic shift that is reinforced by the colon introducing an elaboration. Agentless passives such as “Students will be asked” (line 5) and “a message will be passed on” (line 11) foreground the procedure rather than the authority imposing it, softening the directive.

Remaining paragraphs could cover: inclusive “we/our/Together” (lines 3, 6, 17) and the rhetorical question (line 3) building community; politeness (acknowledging difficulty, lines 6 and 15; thanking students, lines 13–14; mitigated request, line 16); formal lexis and nominalisation (“consultation”, “adjustment period”); cohesion through conjunctive adverbs and the lexical field of attention and wellbeing; the clear problem–solution–call-to-action structure; the formal inverted conditional “Should … arise” (line 11).

Strong commentaries linked every feature to the context and purpose. Weaker ones listed features by subsystem (“there is a passive in line 5”) without saying what it does. Always answer: what is it, where is it, and why is it used here?

Section C – Essay (30 marks)

Choose one topic. Your essay should be supported by relevant examples and appropriate metalanguage.

Write an essay on one of the following topics. In your response, refer to the stimulus material and to other relevant examples. Use appropriate metalanguage.

Topic 1. “Australians use slang to show who they are.” Discuss.
Stimulus: arvo · servo · reckon · no worries · heaps good · cozzie livs (cost of living) · “yeah nah”

Topic 2. “Formal language includes some people and excludes others.” Discuss.
Stimulus: A bank’s terms state: “The Account Holder indemnifies the Bank against any loss arising from unauthorised transactions not attributable to the Bank’s negligence.” Its plain-English summary reads: “If someone uses your card and it wasn’t our fault, you may have to cover the cost.”

Topic 3. “Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander languages, and Aboriginal English, are central to Australian identity.” Discuss.
Stimulus: Place names such as Uluru, Parramatta and Wagga Wagga · dual naming of landmarks · Acknowledgements of Country at public events · borrowed words such as “kangaroo” and “yakka”

Write your essay here, naming the topic you have chosen.

  • Addresses the chosen topic directly with a clear, sustained contention (not just a list of examples)
  • Uses several relevant, contemporary Australian examples from beyond the stimulus, discussed precisely
  • Accurate, well-integrated metalanguage and linguistic concepts (e.g. covert prestige, solidarity, jargon, euphemism, hypocorism, ethnolect)
  • Engages thoughtfully with the stimulus material and with ideas from linguists, commentators or current debate
  • Cohesive essay structure (introduction, topic sentences, conclusion) and controlled, fluent expression

Topic 1 plan. Contention: slang is a key marker of Australian identity, but also of narrower group identities, and it is used strategically. Examples: hypocorisms (arvo, servo, Maccas; recent coinages like “cozzie livs”) signalling national identity and informality; youth slang (“cooked”, “lowkey”, “rizz”) marking in-group membership and separating young people from adults; “yeah nah” as a distinctively Australian discourse particle; politicians’ strategic use of slang (e.g. “fair go”) to build solidarity with voters, which shows slang as performance. Concepts: covert prestige, solidarity, in-group/out-group, lexical change.

Topic 2 plan. Contention: formal language includes those who share its codes and excludes those who don’t, but plain-language movements show it can be made inclusive. Examples: legal jargon and nominalisation in the stimulus (“indemnifies”, “not attributable”) versus plain English; medical jargon; the Australian Government Style Manual’s plain-language guidance; Acknowledgements of Country as formal language that includes. Concepts: jargon, nominalisation, passive voice, social distance, gatekeeping, clarity.

Topic 3 plan. Contention: First Nations languages are embedded in Australian English and public life, and their growing visibility reflects shifting attitudes, though revitalisation is ongoing. Examples: borrowed lexis and place names; dual naming; Acknowledgements and Welcomes to Country; Aboriginal English as a legitimate dialect with its own features (e.g. “deadly”, “mob”) and its role in expressing identity; language revitalisation programs. Concepts: borrowing, dialect, prestige, reclamation, identity.

Model introduction (Topic 1). Few things mark a speaker as Australian as quickly as “arvo” or “yeah nah”. Slang is one of the clearest ways Australians signal who they are, but the identities it expresses are layered. Hypocorisms and distinctive discourse particles mark a broad national identity. Youth and online slang mark membership of narrower groups, often precisely by excluding outsiders. Because slang carries covert prestige, speakers also deploy it strategically, as politicians and advertisers do when they want to sound like “one of us”.

Essays that stayed close to the topic wording and developed three or four well-chosen examples in depth outscored those that listed many examples briefly. Always give the contention in the introduction.

Assessor’s report

General comments

This paper followed the VCAA structure with accessible texts and topics. The casual chat in Text 1 was rich in familiar informal features, the newsletter in Text 2 showed a clear persuasive purpose, and the essay topics invited well-known examples. The difference between average and strong responses was almost always analysis, not identification.

Section A

  • Name the feature precisely, give its line, and explain its function in this context. “Overlapping speech shows they are friends” is too vague. “A cooperative overlap that shows Mia’s excitement” earns full marks.
  • Question 6 rewarded two well-explained features. Five brief features did not score higher.

Section B

  • Open with the context (mode, participants, relationship, field) and the purpose, then use them in every paragraph.
  • Organise by function (e.g. “building community”, “softening directives”) rather than mechanically by subsystem. Functional commentaries were more analytical.
  • Common gaps were the agentless passive (lines 5, 11), the antithesis in line 9 and the politeness strategies in lines 6, 15 and 16.

Section C

  • A clear contention, three or four developed examples, and accurate metalanguage are the core. Examples from the last few years were more convincing than dated ones.
  • Refer to the stimulus briefly, but don’t build the essay around it.

Advice

Aim for 55/75 or more before the medium paper. If your commentary was the weakest section, practise the “feature → example → function → link to context” sentence pattern on short texts.
