English Language · real exam sections, easier

Every section here is a real VCAA exam section. English papers can’t be mixed question by question, because each section is built around one text, so the sections were ranked instead: for each of Section A, B and C, the three most recent exams (2023, 2024, 2025) were ordered by the state average. This paper uses Section A from 2024, Section B from 2024 and Section C from 2025.

Be warned: English results barely move from year to year (section averages range from about 44% to 58%), so the three English papers are much closer in difficulty than the science and maths ones. The biggest difference is in Section A.

VCAA publishes Sections B and C on a 0–15 scale. The exam paper weights each at 30 marks, so the state averages are doubled here. The texts and exact questions are in the official PDFs (VCAA copyright), so open them with the links.

How the state went on these exact sections: an average of 41 / 73 (56%).

Section A – Short-answer questions (13 marks)

All questions refer to Text 1 in the insert of the 2024 exam. Open the insert and each question with the links.

2024 VCAA exam · Insert, Text 1 Open in the exam PDF (p. 30) ↗

Text 1: a transcript of a podcast interview between a host and a sportsperson/disability advocate

State average for this section: 6.7 / 13 (52%).

Using an example, explain how the host establishes the guest’s contributions to society (metalanguage, line numbers). (p. 3 ↗)

  • An accurate, quoted example with line numbers and metalanguage (e.g. the declarative listing of domains, lines 21–24)
  • Explains how it establishes the guest’s contributions: the range of domains he has worked in, as framed by the host

The listing of the domains the guest has moved through (sport, politics, healthcare, representation) in the host’s declarative shows the breadth of his contribution. It has to be the host’s language, not the guest describing himself.

State average 1.3 / 2 (65%); 46% scored full marks.

Mark distribution: 0: 20% · 1: 34% · 2: 46%

Identify one politeness strategy in the interaction and justify how it influences the tenor (metalanguage, line numbers). (p. 4 ↗)

  • Accurately identifies a positive or negative politeness strategy with an example and line number (e.g. thanking/complimenting, line 2)
  • Explains how it shapes the tenor (e.g. warm, friendly yet professional, attending to face needs)

Thanking the guest for joining (line 2) is positive politeness: it meets his positive face needs and sets a warm, respectful tenor. Name the strategy precisely and state the tenor it creates.

State average 1.3 / 2 (65%); 44% scored full marks.

Mark distribution: 0: 13% · 1: 43% · 2: 44%

Explain how changes in function across the text reflect aspects of the situational context (Jakobson’s functions, metalanguage, line numbers). (p. 4 ↗)

  • Identifies a shift between two Jakobson functions (e.g. phatic to referential) with quoted evidence
  • Links the shift to a named element of the situational context (setting, field, tenor, mode or audience)
  • Uses metalanguage to show how the shift is made, not just that it happens
  • Develops a second shift or explains the first in depth

For example, the opening is phatic (greetings, thanks), then turns referential as the host sets up the guest’s background for the podcast audience. Tie each shift to field, audience or mode.

State average 2.2 / 4 (55%); 12% scored full marks.

Mark distribution: 0: 10% · 1: 16% · 2: 33% · 3: 29% · 4: 12%

Analyse how the participants’ turn-taking strategies construct their individual identities and group membership (metalanguage, line numbers). (p. 5 ↗)

  • A turn-taking strategy analysed with evidence (e.g. question–answer adjacency pairs to pass the floor)
  • A second, different strategy analysed (e.g. rising intonation, fillers or fast tempo to hold or transfer the floor)
  • The host’s identity (e.g. podcaster, floor manager, fan) linked to his turn-taking
  • The guest’s identity (e.g. sports figure, advocate, entrepreneur) linked to his turn-taking
  • Group membership in the wider community (e.g. shared fandom, community of innovators) explained

Keep turn-taking separate from topic management. Strong answers used prosody (intonation, tempo) to show how the floor is held or ceded, and covered both speakers’ identities and group membership.

State average 1.9 / 5 (38%); 0.9% scored full marks.

Mark distribution: 0: 13% · 1: 23% · 2: 33% · 3: 23% · 4: 6% · 5: 0.9%

Section B – Analytical commentary (30 marks)

Write an analytical commentary on Text 2 in the insert of the 2024 exam: function, purpose and intent; situational and cultural context; register, tenor and audience; and relevant language features from at least two subsystems.

2024 VCAA exam · Section B · Question 6 Open in the exam PDF (p. 6) ↗

Text 2: a web page (with an embedded spoken monologue) promoting a reconciliation program for schools

Insert: Text 2 ↗

State average for this section: 16.8 / 30 (56%).

Analytical commentary on Text 2

  • Function(s), purpose(s) and intent(s), situational and cultural context, and register, tenor and audience are analysed accurately and specifically for this text
  • Salient language features from at least two subsystems are analysed with quoted examples and line numbers, each explicitly linked to function, context or register
  • Precise, accurate metalanguage throughout
  • A coherent, sustained commentary (not a feature list), with clear, controlled expression

Formal yet approachable register. Referential and conative functions (informing educators and urging them to engage), with emotive and poetic touches in the spoken monologue. Salient features: imperatives (“Join”, “Read”), hyperlinked noun phrases, listing of stakeholders, inclusive “we/together”, the Indigenous borrowing of the program name, and prosody (pauses) in the monologue. The multimodal text tripped many students up.

Assessor’s report

How Victoria went on this paper

These are the real published results for each section in the year it was set, from the VCAA examiners’ reports. A student at the state average in every section would score 41 / 73 (56%): Section A 52%, Section B 56%, Section C 58%.

Short answers: where marks were lost

  • Q5 (Analyse how the participants’ turn-taking strategies construct their individual identities and group membership (metalanguage, line numbers).): 38% of available marks
  • Q4 (Explain how changes in function across the text reflect aspects of the situational context (Jakobson’s functions, metalanguage, line numbers).): 55% of available marks
  • Q2 (Using an example, explain how the host establishes the guest’s contributions to society (metalanguage, line numbers).): 65% of available marks
  • Q3 (Identify one politeness strategy in the interaction and justify how it influences the tenor (metalanguage, line numbers).): 65% of available marks

What the examiners kept saying

  • Short answers: quote precisely, give line numbers, and do every part the question asks. Five-mark questions usually need several features and their links to context or identity.
  • Commentary: choose the most salient features, and link each to function, context or register. Summary and subsystem-by-subsystem listing were marked down.
  • Essay: unpack every key word of the topic in the introduction, use contemporary Australian examples, and don’t pad with misattributed quotations.
