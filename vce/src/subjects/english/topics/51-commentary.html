Section B is an analytical commentary on one text (30 marks, about 50 minutes). The task wording typically asks you to comment on the contextual factors affecting the text's content and form, its social purposes and register, and its stylistic and discourse features. Check the current exam specifications on the VCAA website for exact wording.

The method

  1. Read the context line first (who, where, when, audience, mode). Jot field, tenor, mode, setting and purposes.
  2. Annotate the text for features across the subsystems, with line numbers.
  3. Group features by function (e.g. “establishing authority”, “building rapport”, “persuading”), not by subsystem. Aim for 3 body paragraphs.
  4. Write each paragraph: function topic sentence → feature (metalanguage) → quote + line → effect in this context → link to purpose, register or tenor. Two or three features per paragraph.
  5. Keep the introduction short (context, purposes, register) and skip a long conclusion.
  • Accurate, varied metalanguage from several subsystems (not just lexis).
  • Every feature linked to a function in this text, not a generic effect.
  • Recognising mixed register: formal texts often use strategic informality, and informal texts can have formal moments.
  • Short, exact quotes with line numbers.

Text 2: a formal speech

This speech was delivered by the mayor of Riverbend (a fictional regional Victorian council) at the opening of a new community library in 2025. The audience of about 200 included local residents, council staff, builders and local media. Transcript key: (laughter), (applause) audience responses; — pause for emphasis.

  1. Good morning, everyone, and thank you for braving the weather. (laughter)
  2. I acknowledge the Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung people, the Traditional Owners of the land on which we gather, and pay my respects to their Elders, past and present.
  3. Distinguished guests, councillors, library staff and — most importantly — the people of Riverbend: welcome.
  4. Six years ago, this site was a car park.
  5. Today, it is a library.
  6. And tomorrow, I hope, it will be the heart of our community.
  7. When Council first proposed this project, not everyone was convinced.
  8. Some of you told me — quite firmly — that libraries were a thing of the past. (laughter)
  9. Well, I'm pleased to report that more than four thousand of you signed up for library cards before we'd even opened the doors.
  10. That's not a thing of the past. That's a queue out the front.
  11. This building is not just about books, although there are plenty of those.
  12. It's a place where a teenager can find a quiet corner to study, where a new arrival can practise English with a volunteer, where a grandparent can learn to video-call the grandkids.
  13. It's a place that asks nothing of you but curiosity.
  14. Of course, projects like this don't just happen.
  15. They are the result of years of planning, consultation and, yes, the occasional heated community meeting.
  16. I want to thank our library staff, whose expertise has shaped every shelf and every program.
  17. I want to thank the builders, who worked through two winters to get us here.
  18. And I want to thank you — the ratepayers, readers and dreamers of Riverbend — for backing this vision.
  19. So, without further ado, it is my great pleasure to declare the Riverbend Community Library officially open.
  20. Go on — borrow something. (applause)

Model commentary

Write an analytical commentary on the language features of Text 2.

An analytical commentary opens with the context (field, mode, setting, participants, purpose, register). Each body paragraph is built around a function, not a feature, and uses metalanguage, quoted examples with line numbers, and an explanation of how each feature serves that function in this context.

Introduction. Text 2 is a planned spoken monologue delivered by the mayor of Riverbend at the opening of a community library. The field of civic celebration and the public setting, with residents, staff and media present, call for a largely formal register. However, the mayor's aim to build community pride shapes a warm tenor, so formal ceremonial language is balanced with strategic informality. The speech has several social purposes: to perform the official opening, to thank those involved, to justify a project that faced opposition, and to position the library as “the heart of our community” (line 6).

Paragraph 1: ceremony and authority. As an official civic event, the speech follows ceremonial conventions that establish the mayor's institutional authority. The Acknowledgement of Country (line 2) is a formulaic, formal speech act expected at Australian public events; its elevated syntax (“the land on which we gather”) and respectful lexis (“Elders, past and present”) recognise the Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung people as Traditional Owners and show the council's respect for cultural protocol. The list of vocatives in line 3 (“Distinguished guests, councillors, library staff”) orders the audience by status, reflecting formal politeness norms, although the parenthetical “most importantly — the people of Riverbend”, emphasised by pauses, reverses that hierarchy to flatter the community. The speech peaks with a performative speech act: “it is my great pleasure to declare the Riverbend Community Library officially open” (line 19). The formulaic without further ado, the Latinate verb declare and the adverb officially mark this as the ritual moment that actually opens the building, a function only the mayor's role can perform.

Paragraph 2: persuasion and justification. Because the project was contested, the mayor uses rhetorical devices to justify it. The temporal tricolon in lines 4–6 (“Six years ago… Today… And tomorrow”), built from short parallel declaratives, traces a story of progress and ends on the metaphor “the heart of our community”, elevating the library's importance; the hedge “I hope” keeps the claim modest. The mayor then addresses opponents through concession (“not everyone was convinced”, line 7), reporting their view that libraries were “a thing of the past” (line 8), before rebutting it with a precise statistic (“more than four thousand of you”, line 9). The repetition of “a thing of the past” in line 10, followed by the antithesis “That's a queue out the front”, turns the critics' own phrase against them in a memorable, humorous way. Line 12 uses a tricolon of parallel relative clauses (“where a teenager… where a new arrival… where a grandparent…”) with lexis spanning different ages and backgrounds, constructing the library as inclusive of the whole community and further justifying the spending.

Paragraph 3: rapport and inclusion. Alongside its formality, the speech uses informal features to reduce social distance and create solidarity with residents. Humour is used from the first line (“thank you for braving the weather”), and the audience laughter recorded in lines 1, 8 and 10 shows it succeeding in building positive face. Contractions (I'm, That's, don't), the discourse marker Well (line 9) and the interjected yes in “and, yes, the occasional heated community meeting” (line 15) create a conversational tone, as though the mayor is sharing a joke with neighbours rather than lecturing them. Inclusive pronouns and possessive determiners (“we gather”, “our community”, “our library staff”) position the mayor as one of the community. The anaphora “I want to thank” (lines 16–18) builds to the audience itself, with alliteration in “ratepayers, readers and dreamers” moving from a financial role to an aspirational one. The speech ends with the colloquial imperative “Go on — borrow something” (line 20), whose informal phrasal verb and direct address turn the ceremony into an invitation and prompt the applause.

Cohesion (woven through). The speech is highly cohesive for a spoken text, as its planned mode allows. Anaphoric reference (“That's”, line 10, referring to the four thousand sign-ups; “They”, line 15, referring to “projects like this”) and the parallel structures noted above link ideas clearly for listeners, while lexical cohesion through the semantic field of libraries (books, shelf, library cards, readers, borrow) keeps the field in focus. Together, these features let the mayor fulfil the ceremonial purpose of the speech while persuading and uniting the community.

  • Feature-spotting without function: “Line 10 has a contraction” earns little. Say what it does here.
  • Only analysing lexis. Include syntax, discourse, prosody and semantics too.
  • Generic effects (“this engages the reader”). Name the purpose it serves in this context.
  • Long quotes. Quote the smallest bit that shows the feature.
  • Calling a planned speech “spontaneous”, or treating a formal text as uniformly formal.

Text 3: plan it yourself

A group chat between four Year 12 friends, the night before a Methods SAC. Emoji are described in brackets.

  1. Priya: guys the SAC is TOMORROW???
  2. Liam: ya
  3. Liam: i'm so cooked
  4. Hana: nah ur fine u literally got 90 on the last one
  5. Priya: ok but has anyone done q7 on the practice sheet [skull emoji]
  6. Jack: the chain rule one? u just do derivative of the outside times inside
  7. Jack: actually wait no outside derivative w inside left alone, THEN times derivative of inside
  8. Hana: jack carrying the whole gc fr
  9. Liam: jack for PM
  10. Priya: ty ty [crying-laughing emoji] ok going to bed gl everyone
  11. Hana: gl!! u got this [heart emoji]

Plan an analytical commentary on Text 3: context and purposes, register, and three function-based paragraphs with features and line references.

Context: field, school assessment and maths; tenor, close peers of equal status; mode, written but interactive and synchronous digital (a “spoken-like” written text); purposes: to share anxiety, get help, reassure and maintain friendship. Register: highly informal.

P1, sharing stress and seeking help: CAPS and multiple question marks for prosodic emphasis (“TOMORROW???”, line 1); youth slang with semantic shift cooked (line 3); skull emoji as a pragmatic marker of exaggerated despair (line 5); interrogative request for help (line 5); ellipsis and minimal response ya (line 2).

P2, collaborative problem-solving: Jack's explanation uses the field-specific jargon chain rule, derivative (lines 6–7), showing shared school knowledge; his self-repair “actually wait no…” (line 7) mimics spoken correction; CAPS on THEN as emphatic stress; elliptical clauses (“the chain rule one?”).

P3, solidarity and face work: Hana reassures Liam with counter-evidence (“u literally got 90”, line 4), supporting positive face; praise and humour: initialism fr, clipped gc (group chat) and the hyperbolic “jack for PM” (lines 8–9); politeness through the reduplicated initialism ty ty and emoji (line 10); closing sequence with gl, exclamation marks and heart emoji (lines 10–11). Non-standard spelling (ur, u, w) and all-lowercase reflect speed and a casual, in-group style.

Summary

  • Context → purposes → register in a brief introduction.
  • Paragraphs by function; features from several subsystems with line references.
  • Every feature gets an effect in this context; mixed registers are marks waiting to happen.
