Cohesion: the links you can point to

DeviceExplanationExample
Anaphoric referenceRefers back to something already mentioned“The report was released. It criticised…”
Cataphoric referenceRefers forward“Here's the thing: we're out of time.”
Exophoric referenceRefers to something outside the text (shared context)“Pass me that.”
SubstitutionReplaces a word or phrase with another (one, do, so)“I need a pen. Have you got one?”
EllipsisOmits recoverable words“She can swim, and I can [swim] too.”
Conjunction / connectivesShow relationships: addition, contrast, cause, timehowever, therefore, meanwhile, so
Lexical cohesionRepetition, synonyms, antonyms, collocation, semantic fieldsclimate, emissions, warming, carbon
Information flowGiven → new ordering; end focus places key information last“The committee met on Tuesday. Its decision surprised everyone.”

Coherence: the text makes sense as a whole

  • Logical ordering (chronological, problem–solution, general to specific).
  • Formatting and layout: headings, paragraphs, bullet points, bold type.
  • Consistency of tense, style, register and point of view.
  • Inference and shared knowledge: readers fill gaps using context (especially in informal texts).
  • Cohesive devices support coherence, but a text can be cohesive and still incoherent.

Register = field + tenor + mode

Field (subject matter) shapes lexis; tenor (relationship) shapes formality, pronouns and politeness; mode (channel and planning) shapes structure. Informal conversation relies heavily on exophoric reference and inference; formal writing uses explicit connectives, lexical repetition and formatting.

“The new bike lanes opened in March. Since then, cycling trips have doubled. However, some local traders say they have lost business. They want the council to reconsider.” Identify three cohesive devices and their effect.

Temporal connective Since then links the second sentence back to March (anaphoric), creating a clear sequence.

Contrastive connective However signals a counter-view, structuring a balanced argument.

Anaphoric pronoun They refers back to local traders, avoiding repetition. Lexical cohesion (bike lanes, cycling trips) keeps the field clear. Together these create a coherent, easy-to-follow news text.

  • Anaphoric ≠ anaphora. Anaphoric reference is cohesion; anaphora is rhetorical repetition at the start of clauses.
  • Exophoric reference isn't cohesion within the text; it depends on the situation, which is typical of informal speech.
  • Name the direction (anaphoric/cataphoric) and quote both the reference and its referent.

  2. substitution
  3. nominalisation supporting information flow (given information packaged as a noun at the start of the next sentence)
  4. ellipsis

New information becomes given, and the nominal carries the discussion forward.

Distinguish between cohesion and coherence.

Cohesion is the use of specific linguistic devices that link parts of a text (reference, connectives, lexical repetition). Coherence is the overall sense and logical flow of a text, supported by cohesion but also by ordering, formatting, consistency and the reader's inferences.

Explain why informal conversation can be highly coherent even with few explicit cohesive devices.

Participants share situational context and background knowledge, so they rely on exophoric reference (“that”, “there”) and inference. [2] Ellipsis, adjacency pairs and prosody signal links between turns without explicit connectives, and topic management keeps the talk on track. [2]

Summary

  • Cohesion: reference, substitution, ellipsis, conjunction, lexical cohesion, information flow.
  • Coherence: logical order, formatting, consistency, inference.
  • Register depends on field, tenor and mode.
