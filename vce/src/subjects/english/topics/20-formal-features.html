When language is formal

Formal registers appear when the tenor is distant (strangers, hierarchy, a public audience), the field is serious or specialised (law, government, academia, medicine), and the mode is planned (written documents, speeches, ceremonies). Cultural expectations about respect and authority also shape formality (e.g. courtrooms, parliament, the National Apology).

Features by subsystem

SubsystemTypical formal features
Phonology (spoken)Careful articulation, fewer reductions, steady tempo, deliberate pauses for emphasis, controlled intonation
Morphology/lexicologyNo contractions; Latinate lexis (commence, endeavour, facilitate); technical terms and jargon; full forms rather than clippings; formal titles
SyntaxStandard grammar; complex and compound-complex sentences; nominalisation; passive voice; lists and parallelism; long noun phrases
SemanticsPrecise denotation; euphemism; semantic fields of the specialist area; modal verbs showing obligation or tentativeness (must, may)
DiscoursePlanned structure (headings, numbered clauses); explicit cohesion (furthermore, consequently); impersonal or institutional voice (the University, we as institution); formal openings and closings

(2) During this period, access to affected properties may be temporarily restricted. (3) Council apologises for any inconvenience caused and appreciates the community's patience while these essential works are undertaken. (4) Enquiries should be directed to Customer Service on 1300 000 000.

Practice

“Applications will be assessed on merit.” The verb phrase is

  1. active voice
  2. an imperative
  3. agentless passive voice
  4. a nominalisation

Who assesses isn't stated.

Replacing “We decided to close the pool” with “The decision was made to close the pool” uses

  1. nominalisation and passive voice
  2. ellipsis and contraction
  3. clipping and blending
  4. discourse particles

Decided → the decision, and was made removes the agent.

Which is most formal?

  1. “Can't make it, sorry!”
  2. “I won't be able to come, unfortunately.”
  3. “Sorry, I'm not gonna be there.”
  4. “Regrettably, I am unable to attend.”

Latinate lexis (regrettably, attend), no contractions, full clause.

Lexical density (the proportion of content words) is typically higher in

  1. casual conversation
  2. formal written texts
  3. text messages
  4. informal podcasts

Nominalisation and long noun phrases pack more content words per clause.

In the council notice, the main effect of “Council” (rather than “we”) in sentence 3 is to

  1. create intimacy with residents
  2. show uncertainty
  3. present an impersonal institutional voice that diffuses individual responsibility
  4. signal informality

The institution, not a person, apologises.

Rewrite this informal sentence formally, then name two features you changed: “Heaps of kids didn't hand in their stuff on time, so we're giving 'em till Friday.”

E.g. “A significant number of students did not submit their assessments by the due date; consequently, an extension until Friday has been granted.” Changes: colloquial lexis replaced with Latinate/standard lexis (heaps → a significant number; stuff → assessments); contractions removed; passive voice and a formal connective (consequently) added.

Analyse how syntax contributes to the formality of the council notice.

Agentless passives (“Residents are advised”, “works… are undertaken”, “Enquiries should be directed”) create an impersonal, authoritative register and foreground procedures over people. [2] Complex sentences with subordinate clauses (“while these essential works are undertaken”, sentence 3) and nominalised noun phrases (“works to upgrade the stormwater infrastructure”) increase lexical density and precision, typical of planned written bureaucratic discourse. Summary

  • Formality follows distant tenor, serious field and planned mode.
  • Key features: Latinate lexis, jargon, nominalisation, passive, complex syntax, explicit cohesion.
  • Analyse effect: authority, precision, impersonality, and sometimes obscurity.
