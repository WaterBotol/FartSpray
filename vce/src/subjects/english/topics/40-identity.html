Identity is something we do

Linguists treat identity as performed and negotiated through language choices, not fixed. Each of us has a linguistic repertoire (all the varieties and styles we can use) and we select from it depending on who we're talking to, where, and who we want to be seen as.

TermMeaning
IdiolectAn individual's unique way of speaking: favourite words, catchphrases, pronunciation habits
SociolectA variety associated with a social group (class, occupation, age)
Dialect / ethnolectRegional / ethnic varieties (see Australian English and ethnolects)
In-group / out-groupThose who share a group's language (and belong) vs those who don't
Solidarity / social distanceCloseness and equality vs formality and hierarchy, signalled by language

Code-switching and style shifting

Code-switching is moving between languages or varieties; style shifting is moving along the formality scale within one variety. Both express different parts of identity: a student might use slang with friends, a heritage language with grandparents and SAE in a job interview. Reasons include matching the audience, showing solidarity, excluding outsiders, quoting someone, or expressing a concept that one code handles better.

Communication Accommodation Theory

Developed by social psychologist Howard Giles, CAT explains how speakers adjust their speech in relation to others.

StrategyWhat happensEffect
ConvergenceShifting towards the other speaker's accent, lexis, rate or formalitySeeks approval and solidarity; reduces social distance
DivergenceEmphasising differences (broadening an accent, using in-group slang)Asserts distinct identity; increases social distance
MaintenanceKeeping one's usual style regardlessSignals independence or indifference
Over-accommodationConverging too far (e.g. talking down to an elderly person, “elderspeak”)Can seem patronising

Allan Bell's related idea: speakers design their style for their audience, including people who are only overhearing. Radio presenters, politicians and influencers shift styles for different audiences.

Naming and address

  • Address terms map relationships: Dr Nguyen, Ms Smith, Sarah, mate, babe, bro. Asymmetric use (a teacher calls a student Jack, the student says Mr Brown) signals hierarchy.
  • Nicknames and hypocorisms (Robbo, Shaz, ScoMo, Albo) create familiarity and group belonging. Australian politicians often adopt them to seem approachable.
  • Self-naming: choosing pronouns, names and labels (e.g. identity-first “autistic person” vs person-first “person with autism”) asserts self-definition.

Gender and language

  • Robin Lakoff (1975) claimed “women's language” used more hedges, tag questions and polite forms, reflecting lower social power. Later research found these features depend more on role and power than on gender alone (e.g. tag questions used by powerful speakers to facilitate talk).
  • Deborah Tannen (1990) described “rapport talk” vs “report talk”, a difference model; critics say it can reinforce stereotypes.
  • Modern views stress that people perform gender identity through language in different ways, and that non-binary people use language (e.g. singular they) to express identity.
  • Australian example: Julia Gillard's 2012 “misogyny speech” in Parliament named and challenged sexist language used about her.

Online identities

Usernames, bios, emoji, hashtags, lowercase styling and in-group memes let people build curated personas. Communities (gaming, fandoms, TikTok subcultures) develop their own jargon, and switching accounts (a public profile vs a private “finsta” or close-friends list) is a digital form of style shifting.

  • Convergence isn't always good; divergence isn't always rude. Explain the motive in context.
  • Code-switching is between languages or varieties; style shifting is within one variety.
  • Avoid gender stereotypes: present Lakoff critically and link features to power and context.
  • Identity is multiple: one person has many identities (age, gender, ethnicity, occupation, place).

Summary

  • Identity is performed through choices from a linguistic repertoire.
  • Code-switching and style shifting express different identities.
  • CAT: convergence (solidarity), divergence (distinctiveness), maintenance.
  • Address terms, naming and gendered language reveal power and relationships.
