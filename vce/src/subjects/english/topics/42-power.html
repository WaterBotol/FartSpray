Language includes and excludes

  • Jargon: specialist vocabulary gives precision and solidarity among insiders (medicine, law, AFL commentary, gaming) but can exclude and intimidate outsiders.
  • Secret and in-group codes: slang, private nicknames and memes define who belongs.
  • Power in conversation: controlling topics, interrupting, holding the floor and using asymmetric address terms (see spoken discourse and identity).
  • Naming: whoever names something frames how people think about it.

Taboo, euphemism and dysphemism

Australian linguists Keith Allan and Kate Burridge (Forbidden Words, 2006) describe three ways to talk about a sensitive topic:

TypeDefinitionExamples (death, job loss, toilet)
EuphemismSweeter-sounding, softer term that avoids offence or hides unpleasantnesspassed away; let go; bathroom
OrthophemismNeutral, direct, literal termdied; dismissed; toilet
DysphemismHarsh, blunt or offensive term, often used for humour, insult or solidaritycarked it; sacked; the dunny

A euphemism gradually takes on the negative connotations of what it refers to, so it gets replaced by a newer one. Terms for disability and mental illness show this cycle clearly. It's a strong essay point on why “PC language” keeps changing.

Swearing: functions, not just offence

  • Catharsis (stubbing a toe), aggression (insults), solidarity and humour (mates using taboo terms affectionately), emphasis.
  • Australian English is known for relaxed attitudes to some swear words, but context still decides: what's fine at the footy may lead to a fine or dismissal elsewhere.
  • Attitudes differ by age, gender, culture and setting; broadcast standards and workplace codes regulate taboo language.

Inclusive language and “political correctness”

Genderfireman, chairman, policemanfirefighter, chair(person), police officerAvoids implying the role belongs to men
Pronounsgeneric he; “he or she”singular theyIncludes everyone; used for centuries and chosen by Merriam-Webster as its 2019 Word of the Year

  • Supporters: inclusive language reflects and encourages respect, reduces harm and recognises groups' right to name themselves.
  • Critics: call it “political correctness gone mad” or “woke”, arguing it restricts free speech, is imposed from above or is just a label change (the euphemism treadmill). “PC” and “woke” are themselves often used as dysphemisms.
  • Analyse both sides; the VCE rewards discussion of attitudes with evidence.

Contested naming in Australia

  • Dual naming: Ayers Rock was dual-named “Ayers Rock / Uluru” in 1993; the order was reversed to “Uluru / Ayers Rock” in 2002. Fraser Island was officially renamed K'gari in 2023.
  • 26 January: Australia Day, Invasion Day and Survival Day frame the same date from different perspectives. The choice of name signals identity and political stance.
  • Acknowledgement of Country and Welcome to Country (the latter given by Traditional Owners) are ritual uses of language that recognise sovereignty and connection to land.
  • Euphemism vs doublespeak: euphemism softens (often kindly); doublespeak deliberately deceives (see formal functions).
  • Dysphemism isn't always hostile: among mates it can build solidarity.
  • Stay balanced on PC debates: present evidence for multiple views before your contention.
  • Singular they isn't new: it appears in Chaucer and Shakespeare. Great counter-evidence to “it's ungrammatical”.

“Inclusive language is just political correctness.” Outline two points for an essay responding to this view.

Against the view: inclusive terms (firefighter, singular they, specific First Nations names) reflect real social change and groups' right to self-name; dual naming (Uluru, K'gari) shows language recognising identity and history. [2]

Acknowledging the view: the euphemism treadmill means some replacements don't change underlying attitudes, and “PC” criticism reflects genuine concerns about imposed usage; but the fact that “PC” and “woke” function as dysphemisms shows the debate is about power and identity, not just words. [2]
