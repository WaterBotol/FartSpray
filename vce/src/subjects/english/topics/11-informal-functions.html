Face and politeness

  • Positive face: the desire to be liked, approved of and included. Protected by compliments, in-group terms, nicknames and friendly teasing.
  • Negative face: the desire to be unimposed upon and free to act. Protected by hedges, indirect requests and apologies (“sorry, would you mind…”).
  • A face-threatening act (request, refusal, criticism) can be softened by politeness strategies or done bluntly (“bald on record”) between close friends, where bluntness itself can signal intimacy.

Non-standard or informal varieties can carry covert prestige: status within a group, even though the wider society values standard forms (overt prestige). Broad Australian features, slang or swearing can signal toughness, authenticity or belonging.

Australian informality and identity

Informality is strongly linked to Australian national identity: egalitarianism (“tall poppy syndrome”), mateship and a dislike of pretension. Hypocorisms (arvo, smoko, ambo, Albo for PM Anthony Albanese), friendly insults and understatement are all used to reduce social distance, even in public life. Politicians use informal language strategically to appear relatable (“I'm just a normal bloke”).

Explain the difference between positive and negative face, with an example of language that protects each.

Positive face is the desire to be liked and included, protected by compliments or inclusive language (“you legend, thanks heaps”). Negative face is the desire not to be imposed on, protected by hedged, indirect requests (“If you get a chance, could you maybe…”).

Informality reflects egalitarian values and mateship: hypocorisms and nicknames are used even for prime ministers (“Albo”), and words of the year such as cozzie livs (Macquarie 2023) show Australians' love of playful clipping. [2]

But identity is diverse and not always informal: formal registers carry authority in ceremonies and politics (the 2008 National Apology), and ethnolects and Aboriginal English also express Australian identities. A nuanced essay acknowledges multiple Australian identities. [2]

Summary

  • Informal language builds solidarity, identity, inclusion/exclusion, humour, and manages face.
  • Positive face: approval. Negative face: freedom from imposition.
  • Covert prestige explains why non-standard forms persist.
