Section C is an essay (30 marks, about 45 minutes). You choose one of several topics, each with stimulus material. Markers look for a clear, sustained contention, accurate metalanguage, relevant contemporary examples (ideally Australian and recent), and understanding of different views. Check the current exam specifications on the VCAA website for exact details.

Unpack the prompt

  1. Find the key terms and define them in your introduction (e.g. “identity”, “Standard Australian English”, “informal language”).
  2. Find the assumption: “Young people are the main drivers of language change” contains a claim you can qualify.
  3. Choose a position: agree, disagree, or (usually strongest) agree to an extent, with a clear reason.
  4. Plan three body paragraphs, each with a different argument, two or three examples, and metalanguage.

Paragraph structure

PartWhat to write
Topic sentenceAn argument that answers the prompt (not a fact or an example)
ExplanationThe linguistic concept behind it, with metalanguage (e.g. covert prestige, euphemism, convergence)
ExamplesTwo or three specific, accurate examples with dates and sources; analyse the language in each
ComplicationA counter-view or limitation, to show sophistication
LinkReturn to the prompt and your contention
  • Never invent examples or statistics. A vague real example beats a precise fake one, and markers notice.
  • Analyse, don't just name: “the nominalised label ‘Online Compliance Intervention’ obscured…” beats “Robodebt used formal language”.
  • Use the stimulus but go beyond it. Markers reward examples you bring yourself.
  • Mix scale: national events, media, and everyday observations (your own school, family, part-time job) all count.

Model introduction and paragraph

Write an introduction and one body paragraph.

An essay needs a clear contention that responds to the prompt, a body paragraph structure of topic sentence, contemporary Australian examples, metalanguage and linguistic analysis, and links back to the prompt. Draw on a range of evidence (media, public debate, scholarship) rather than one example.

Introduction. Identity, our sense of who we are and where we belong, is not fixed but constantly performed and negotiated through language. Australians draw on a repertoire of varieties, from Standard Australian English to ethnolects, youth slang and Aboriginal English, choosing features that align them with some groups and distance them from others. Yet identity is also shaped by others: the labels that institutions, media and politicians apply can include or exclude whole communities. While the language we choose is central to how we express identity, the language used about us, especially by those with power, can constrain or legitimise that identity.

Body paragraph. The labels applied by powerful groups can shape how communities are seen, but communities increasingly reclaim the right to name themselves. For decades, place names honouring colonial figures overwrote Aboriginal names, erasing connection to Country from official language. The dual naming of “Uluru / Ayers Rock”, with the Anangu name placed first since 2002, and the renaming of Fraser Island as K'gari in 2023 show institutions adopting First Nations terms and so legitimising those identities in public discourse. Similarly, the contested naming of 26 January as “Australia Day”, “Invasion Day” or “Survival Day” shows that the same date can be framed to foreground national celebration or the impact of colonisation, and a speaker's choice of name signals their stance and identity. Communities can also reclaim labels used against them: Greek- and Italian-Australian comedians turned the slur “wog” into an in-group marker in productions such as Acropolis Now (1989–1992), giving the term covert prestige among speakers who used it about themselves. Nevertheless, reclamation has limits, since the word remains offensive when used by outsiders, showing that the power of a label depends on who uses it and in what context. Thus, while imposed language can marginalise, individuals and groups actively contest and reshape it to assert their own identities.

Practice prompts with plans

1. “Language can be used to clarify or to conceal.” Discuss, with reference to formal language in Australian public life.

Contention: formal language is designed to clarify, but the same features (nominalisation, passive voice, jargon) are readily used to conceal, particularly where power is at stake.

P1, clarity: plain-English legislation and the Australian Government Style Manual; public health slogans such as “Stay home, stay safe” (imperatives, repetition, rhyme) during COVID-19.

P2, concealment: Robodebt's “Online Compliance Intervention” (bureaucratic nominalisation) vs the Royal Commission's plain verdict (2023); “on-water matters” (2013–14); corporate euphemisms such as restructure.

P3, formal language to acknowledge: the 2008 National Apology's plain “we say sorry” within a formal speech; the Acknowledgement of Country as a ritual that recognises rather than conceals.

2. “Young people are the main drivers of language change.” To what extent do you agree?

Contention: young people drive much lexical innovation, especially online, but technology, institutions, migration and major events drive change across all ages.

P1, youth innovation: rizz (Oxford 2023), brain rot (Oxford 2024), 67 (Dictionary.com 2025); clipping, semantic shift, initialisms; covert prestige and rapid turnover.

P2, events and institutions: COVID-19 vocabulary (iso, ANDC 2020; strollout, 2021); politics (teal, 2022; Voice, ANDC 2023); inclusive language driven by workplaces and style guides (firefighter, singular they).

P3, contact and technology: ethnolects and multiethnolects (yalla, full); AI vocabulary (hallucinate, Cambridge 2023); age-grading means many youth terms don't last.

3. “Australian English is as much about attitude as it is about vocabulary.” Discuss.

Contention: distinctive vocabulary matters, but Australian English is also defined by pragmatic norms and attitudes such as egalitarianism, understatement and irreverence.

P1, vocabulary: hypocorisms (arvo, cozzie livs, Macquarie 2023); Aboriginal loanwords; regionalisms (bathers/togs).

P2, attitude in pragmatics: yeah nah as a softener (Burridge and Florey's research); mate and nicknames (Albo) flattening hierarchy; dysphemistic humour; understatement.

P3, attitudes to Australian English itself: from cultural cringe to codification (Macquarie Dictionary, 1981); the attempt to include “mateship” in a 1999 constitutional preamble and the backlash; debates about Americanisation.

4. “Standard Australian English should be valued above all other varieties.” Discuss.

Contention: SAE is valuable as a shared code for formal contexts, but ranking it “above” other varieties confuses social prestige with linguistic worth and disadvantages many speakers.

P1, value of a standard: codification, access to education, law and government; plain English movements.

P2, all varieties are rule-governed: Aboriginal English (bidialectal education; gratuitous concurrence in court); youse filling a pronoun gap; ethnolects expressing bicultural identity.

P3, attitudes and power: prescriptivism and the complaint tradition; overt vs covert prestige; accent bias in hiring.

5. “Informal language is essential in building and maintaining relationships.” Discuss.

Contention: informal language is central to solidarity and rapport, though its value depends on shared context, and it can also exclude.

P1, solidarity: hypocorisms, in-group slang, mock impoliteness among friends; mate; convergence (CAT).

P2, strategic informality in public: politicians' nicknames (Albo, ScoMo); Dan Andrews' “get on the beers” (2020); brands using casual register on social media.

P3, limits: informality can exclude outsiders (in-group slang, jargon), misfire when tenor is misjudged (adults using youth slang), or undermine credibility in formal settings.

6. “The language used to describe people matters.” Discuss.

Contention: labels shape perception and power, so changes in naming reflect, and can drive, social change, even though critics see some changes as superficial.

P1, inclusive terms: gender-neutral job titles; singular they (Merriam-Webster 2019); person-first vs identity-first disability language.

P2, First Nations naming: specific nation names; dual place names (Uluru, K'gari); 26 January naming; Acknowledgement of and Welcome to Country, and debate about them.

P3, the counter-view: euphemism treadmill; “PC” and “woke” as dysphemisms in debate; the idea that attitudes matter more than words.

Keep a running list from news, social media, TV and your own life: a new word, a politician's slogan, a label controversy, a workplace policy. For each, note the date, the source and one metalanguage term. Ten well-understood examples beat forty half-remembered ones.

  • Refer to the stimulus, explicitly or implicitly, and label it so the assessor sees the link: “… as the inclusive language guide (Stimulus A) argues …”. Very good essays that skipped the stimulus lost marks.
  • Unpack every key word in both the topic and the question (e.g. both “social separation” and “social equality”). Top essays showed how the same language can do both for different audiences.
  • Keep evidence current. There's no date cut-off, but examples should be socially and culturally relevant at the time you're writing. Recent slang, a live debate about inclusive language, or a current public controversy beats a stale memorised example. Older examples are fine if usage hasn't changed.
  • Adapt, don't recite. Pre-prepared paragraphs that sit awkwardly beside the point were marked down. Pick examples that illuminate the specific question.

Summary

  • Unpack the prompt, define key terms, and take a qualified position.
  • Paragraphs: argument → concept → analysed examples → complication → link.
  • Accurate, recent, Australian examples with metalanguage; never fabricate.
