What “standard” means

Standard Australian English (SAE) is the variety used in formal writing, education, government, law and the media. It is defined mainly by grammar, spelling and vocabulary, not accent: you can speak SAE with a broad, general or ethnocultural accent. Linguists see it as one dialect among many that gained prestige for social and historical reasons, not because it is more logical.

Non-standard forms are systematic and follow rules; they are inappropriate in some contexts, not “errors” in any absolute sense. “Youse” is a neat example: it fixes a real gap that SAE fills clumsily with “you guys” or “you all”.

Prescriptivism vs descriptivism

Prescriptive

  • Says how language should be used
  • Treats change as decline
  • Enforces rules: “never split an infinitive”, “less for amounts, fewer for countables”, “literally must mean literally”
  • Found in style guides, letters to the editor, pedantic social media posts

Descriptive

  • Records how language is used
  • Treats change as natural
  • Dictionaries now record literally as an intensifier and singular they
  • The approach of linguists and of VCE English Language

The complaint tradition is the centuries-old habit of lamenting that language is being ruined (by the young, by Americans, by technology). Many “rules” were invented by 18th-century grammarians modelling English on Latin, such as the ban on split infinitives or ending a sentence with a preposition.

Prestige

Overt prestigeCovert prestige
Status from society at large and institutions; attached to SAE and formal styles. Shows education and authority.Status within a group, often attached to non-standard forms (broad accent, slang, ethnolect features). Shows toughness, authenticity, solidarity, “not trying too hard”.
  • Linguistic insecurity: speakers who feel their variety is inferior may hypercorrect (“between you and I”).
  • Gatekeeping: control of SAE affects access to education, jobs and legal processes, so the standard can both empower (shared code) and exclude.
  • Plain English: movements push governments, banks and courts to write clearly, balancing standardness with accessibility.
  • SAE isn't an accent; it's grammar, lexis and spelling.
  • Don't be prescriptive in your own answers: write “non-standard”, not “incorrect” or “bad grammar”.
  • But do discuss prescriptive attitudes as evidence: they're part of the Unit 4 content.
  • Prestige is social, not linguistic: no variety is inherently better.

Which is not a stage of standardisation?

  1. selection
  2. codification
  3. elaboration

Convergence is an accommodation strategy in conversation, not a stage of standardisation.

Distinguish between overt and covert prestige, with an example of each.

Overt prestige is status granted by society and institutions, e.g. SAE and formal lexis in a job application. Covert prestige is status within a particular group, often for non-standard forms, e.g. a broad accent or slang among mates signalling toughness or solidarity.

“Standard Australian English is necessary, but it is also a tool of exclusion.” Outline two points for an essay responding to this statement.

Necessary: a codified shared standard enables clear communication across the nation in law, education and government (dictionaries, the Style Manual, plain-English legislation), giving everyone access to one code. [2]

Exclusion: because SAE carries overt prestige, speakers of non-standard varieties, ethnolects or Aboriginal English can be judged as less capable in schools, job interviews and courts; prescriptive attitudes and the complaint tradition reinforce this gatekeeping. [2]

Summary

  • SAE: the codified variety of formal contexts; defined by grammar, lexis and spelling, not accent.
  • Standardisation: selection, codification, elaboration, acceptance.
  • Prescriptive (should) vs descriptive (is); the complaint tradition.
  • Overt prestige (institutions) vs covert prestige (groups); hypercorrection shows insecurity.
