Where Australian English came from

  • From 1788, settlers and convicts brought many British and Irish dialects. In the first generations of children born in the colony, these levelled into a new, fairly uniform variety.
  • Borrowings from Aboriginal languages named unfamiliar plants, animals and places: kangaroo (Guugu Yimithirr), koala, boomerang, billabong, yakka (hard work), and thousands of place names.
  • Words from British dialects took on new Australian meanings (paddock, creek, bush), and new compounds arose (outback, sheep station).
  • Later influences: American English (film, TV, the internet), post-war migration, and multicultural contact.

Research shows the categories are a continuum, not boxes. Cultivated has declined sharply, and newer ethnocultural accents have joined the mix (see ethnolects).

Changing attitudes

  • Cultural cringe (mid-20th century): Australian English was widely seen as inferior to British English; broadcasters were expected to sound British.
  • Growing pride from the 1970s: the Macquarie Dictionary (1981) and the Australian National Dictionary (1988) codified Australian English as a legitimate national variety; the ABC now uses a range of Australian accents.
  • Current debates: Americanisation (awesome, dude, spelling changes in digital media), concern over “loss” of Australian slang, and recognition of Aboriginal English and multicultural varieties as Australian.
  • There's no single Australian English: there are regional, social and ethnocultural varieties.
  • Broad isn't “bad” and cultivated isn't “correct”. Describe the attitudes, don't adopt them.
  • Accent is pronunciation only; dialect includes lexicon and grammar as well.

Most Australians speak with which accent?

  2. broad
  3. cultivated
  4. rhotic

General is the majority accent. Australian English is non-rhotic.

  1. covert prestige
  2. dialect levelling
  3. code-switching
  4. cultural cringe

Cultural cringe: a sense that local culture is second-rate.

Cozzie livs (Macquarie Word of the Year 2023) best illustrates

  1. borrowing from an Aboriginal language
  2. the Australian fondness for hypocorisms, applied to a contemporary issue
  3. American influence
  4. a formal register

“Cost of living” clipped and suffixed playfully.

Publication of the Macquarie Dictionary (1981) is significant because it

  1. introduced the cultivated accent
  2. banned Americanisms
  3. codified Australian English as a legitimate variety, reflecting growing national linguistic confidence
  4. replaced British spelling with American spelling

Codification signals legitimacy and pride.

Describe the broad, general and cultivated accents and the attitudes traditionally associated with each.

Broad: most distinctively Australian vowels; associated with rural, working-class, larrikin identity (covert prestige, authenticity). General: majority, mainstream accent; seen as neutral. Cultivated: closest to British RP; associated with education and status (overt prestige), now declining.

“Australian English is losing its distinctiveness.” Outline evidence for and against this claim.

For: American media and online culture spread words like dude, awesome and gotten; some traditional slang (strewth, bonzer) is now rare among young people. [2]

Against: Australians keep coining distinctive words, often hypocorisms and blends (iso, strollout, cozzie livs); Australian vowels and yeah nah remain strong; ethnolects and Aboriginal English add new distinctively Australian varieties. [2]

Summary

  • AusE arose from dialect levelling after 1788, with Aboriginal loanwords and new meanings.
  • Hallmarks: hypocorisms, non-rhoticity, distinctive vowels, yeah nah.
  • Accents form a continuum (broad, general, cultivated); attitudes moved from cringe to pride.
