What you have to do

In Unit 4 you design and carry out your own investigation into cellular processes and/or biological change, then communicate it in a scientific poster. It draws on the key science skills page and on Unit 3 and 4 content. Check your school's task sheet for its exact requirements.

  • Logbook: a dated record of planning, risk and ethics considerations, raw data, observations and changes to the method, kept as you go. It authenticates your work.
  • Poster: VCAA provides a poster template with a word limit (around 600 words); check your school's version for the exact requirements.
  • Ethics and safety are part of the design, not an afterthought: school rules normally exclude culturing pathogens, using human body fluids and experiments that harm vertebrate animals.

Designing a biological experiment

  • Controls: include a control group or set-up for comparison (e.g. boiled enzyme, no enzyme, leaf discs kept in the dark).
  • Sample size and replication: living material varies, so use several replicates (e.g. 10 leaf discs per trial, three trials per condition) and average.
  • Controlled variables: temperature, pH, concentration, volume, source and age of the tissue, time.
  • Range and intervals: at least five values of the independent variable across a sensible range, to show a trend or an optimum.
  • Qualitative vs quantitative data: measure numbers wherever possible (volume of gas, time to float, absorbance) rather than descriptions.

Your teacher must approve the question, your risk assessment and any ethical issues before you start.

Plan an investigation into how light intensity affects the rate of photosynthesis using spinach leaf discs in sodium hydrogen carbonate solution. Include the hypothesis, variables, control, method outline, data analysis, one ethical or safety consideration and the main sources of error.

Hypothesis: “If light intensity increases, then the rate of photosynthesis will increase until another factor becomes limiting, because more light energy drives the light-dependent stage, producing O₂ that makes the discs float sooner.”

Variables: IV = light intensity (lamp at 5 distances, or a light meter reading). DV = time for 5 of 10 discs to float (ET₅₀), or the number floating after 10 min. Controlled: NaHCO₃ concentration and volume, temperature (heat shield of water between lamp and discs), disc size, leaf source and age, number of discs.

Control: a set of discs in the dark, which shouldn't float (showing that floating is caused by photosynthesis).

Method outline: cut discs with a hole punch, remove the air by vacuum in a syringe, place 10 discs per cup, record floating every minute; three trials per light intensity. Rate = 1/ET₅₀.

Analysis: plot mean rate (1/ET₅₀) against light intensity with the range of the trials shown; describe the rise and any plateau, and explain the plateau as another limiting factor (CO₂ or temperature).

Safety and ethics: lamps get hot (heat shield, don't touch); use the sharp hole punch carefully; plant material raises no animal ethics issues, but dispose of it correctly.

Errors: variation between leaves and discs (random: more discs and trials); heating from the lamp changing temperature (systematic: a heat shield or LED lamp); judging exactly when a disc “floats” (random: a clear criterion). This structure maps directly onto the poster sections.

Writing a strong discussion

  1. Describe the trend with data values (and any optimum or plateau).
  2. Explain it with biology, using precise terms.
  3. Compare with expected results or published data.
  4. Evaluate the data: variability between replicates (precision), specific random and systematic errors and the direction of their effects, outliers.
  5. Validity and limitations: were relevant variables controlled? Was the sample size large enough? For a model or simulation, how well does it represent the real process?
  6. Improvements and extensions that target the errors you identified, plus a follow-up question.
  • A correlation between two variables doesn't prove one causes the other unless other variables were controlled.
  • A bead or card simulation of natural selection is a model: say what it represents well (selection, change in frequency) and what it leaves out (mutation, inheritance mechanisms, many generations).

Students model natural selection by “hunting” coloured paper dots on a patterned background for several rounds, “breeding” the survivors each round. Identify two strengths and two limitations of this model.

Strengths (any two): shows that variation must exist before selection; shows the frequency of better-camouflaged phenotypes increasing over generations; shows selection acts on phenotypes; allows quick collection of quantitative data. [2]

Limitations (any two): no genetic mechanism or new mutations; offspring are identical copies of parents; few generations; the “predators” are humans who may not behave like real predators; no random events (drift) or gene flow unless added. [2]

Summary

  • Design with a control, enough replicates and clearly controlled variables.
  • Logbook authenticates; poster communicates (title, communication statement, introduction, methodology, results, discussion, conclusion, references).
  • Discussions quantify trends, evaluate specific errors and variability, and state limitations (including of models).
