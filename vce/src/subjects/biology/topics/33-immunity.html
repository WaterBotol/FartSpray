How vaccines work

A vaccine contains antigens (or instructions to make them) from a pathogen in a form that can't cause disease. It triggers a primary immune response, producing memory B and T cells. On real exposure, a rapid secondary response clears the pathogen before illness develops.

Boosters re-expose the immune system to the antigen, producing a secondary response with even more memory cells and antibody, extending protection (e.g. tetanus boosters every 10 years).

Herd immunity

When a high enough proportion of a population is immune, the pathogen can't spread effectively, so people who can't be vaccinated (newborns, immunocompromised people) are protected indirectly.

Herd immunity threshold
\[ \text{threshold} = 1 - \frac{1}{R_0} \]

\(R_0\) (basic reproduction number) = average number of people infected by one case in a fully susceptible population. Measles (\(R_0 \approx 12\text{–}18\)) needs about 92–95% immunity.

A disease has \(R_0 = 4\). What proportion of the population must be immune for herd immunity? If the vaccine is 90% effective, what vaccination coverage is needed?

If each infected person infects \(R_0\) others, the disease stops spreading once fewer than \(\frac{1}{R_0}\) of their contacts are susceptible. So more than \(1 - \frac{1}{R_0}\) of the population must be immune. A vaccine less than 100% effective means more people must be vaccinated to reach that level.

Threshold \(= 1 - \frac14 = 0.75\), i.e. 75% immune.

Coverage \(= \frac{0.75}{0.90} \approx 83\%\). 75% immune; about 83% vaccinated.

  • Vaccination is artificial active immunity, not passive: your body makes the antibodies.
  • Passive immunity provides no memory cells.
  • Antivenom works immediately because it's passive; a vaccine would be too slow after a snakebite.
  • Herd immunity doesn't mean everyone is immune; it means transmission chains break.

Explain how a vaccine protects a person from a future infection.

The vaccine contains antigens from the pathogen (in a form that can't cause disease), triggering a primary adaptive response. [1] Clonal selection and expansion produce memory B and T cells specific to that antigen. [1] On later exposure, memory cells produce a rapid, strong secondary response that destroys the pathogen before symptoms develop. [1]

Vaccination rates for measles in a community fall from 95% to 85%. Explain why an outbreak becomes likely, and who is most at risk.

Measles has a very high \(R_0\) (about 12–18), so its herd immunity threshold is about 92–95%. [1] At 85% coverage there are enough susceptible people for chains of transmission to continue. [1] Most at risk: babies too young to be vaccinated and immunocompromised people, who relied on herd protection. [1]

Compare natural active and artificial passive immunity, with an example of each.

Natural active: the body makes its own antibodies and memory cells after infection (e.g. recovering from chickenpox); slow onset, long-lasting. [1] Artificial passive: ready-made antibodies are injected (e.g. antivenom); immediate, short-lived, no memory cells. [1] Both give protection, but only active immunity gives long-term memory. [1]

A student says mRNA vaccines change your DNA. Explain why this is incorrect.

The mRNA stays in the cytoplasm, where ribosomes translate it into the antigen protein; it doesn't enter the nucleus and there's no reverse transcriptase to convert it to DNA. The mRNA is broken down within days.

Summary

  • Active: own antibodies and memory (slow, lasting). Passive: received antibodies (fast, temporary).
  • Natural vs artificial depends on the source.
  • Vaccines trigger a primary response so the real infection meets a secondary response.
  • Herd immunity threshold \(= 1 - \frac{1}{R_0}\).
