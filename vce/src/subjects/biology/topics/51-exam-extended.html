VCE Biology Section B rewards precise terminology and linked explanations: “because… therefore…”. Experimental design questions expect named variables, a control and a reason for each choice. Attempt fully, then compare with the solutions line by line.

Question 1: gene expression and DNA tools (12 marks)

Human growth hormone (hGH) is a protein secreted by pituitary cells.

a. Describe how the hGH gene is transcribed and the pre-mRNA processed. (3)

b. Describe the path of hGH from its synthesis to its release from the cell. (3)

c. hGH is now made in bacteria. Explain why the gene is obtained as cDNA and outline how a recombinant plasmid is made. (4)

d. PCR is used to check that the hGH gene is present in transformed bacteria. After 25 cycles, how many copies could be made from 10 starting copies? (1)

e. Explain how gel electrophoresis could confirm the size of the PCR product. (1)

a. RNA polymerase binds the promoter and moves along the template strand, building complementary pre-mRNA. [1] Introns are spliced out and exons joined. [1] A 5′ cap and a poly-A tail are added. [1]

b. Translated by ribosomes on the rough ER; the polypeptide folds in the RER. [1] Transported in a vesicle to the Golgi, where it's modified and packaged into secretory vesicles. [1] Vesicles fuse with the plasma membrane and release hGH by exocytosis. [1]

c. Bacteria can't remove introns; cDNA, made from mature mRNA by reverse transcriptase, has no introns. [1] The plasmid and cDNA are cut with the same restriction enzyme to produce complementary sticky ends. [1] They're mixed so the sticky ends base-pair. [1] DNA ligase joins the sugar–phosphate backbones. [1]

d. \(10 \times 2^{25} \approx 3.4 \times 10^8\) copies. [1]

e. Run the PCR product beside a DNA ladder; the band's distance compared with the ladder bands shows whether it's the expected size. [1]

Question 2: biochemical pathways and experimental design (12 marks)

A student investigates the effect of light intensity on the rate of photosynthesis in spinach leaf discs by timing how long the discs take to float in a bicarbonate solution.

a. Write an aim and a hypothesis. (2)

b. Identify the independent and dependent variables and two controlled variables. (3)

c. Explain why the discs float as photosynthesis proceeds. (2)

d. At high light intensities, the rate stops increasing. Suggest and explain why. (2)

e. Explain why the Calvin cycle stops soon after the light is switched off. (2)

f. Identify one source of random error and one way to reduce it. (1)

a. Aim: to investigate the effect of light intensity on the rate of photosynthesis in spinach leaf discs. [1] Hypothesis: if light intensity increases, then the rate of photosynthesis will increase (discs will float sooner) up to a point, because more light energy drives the light-dependent stage. [1]

b. IV: light intensity (e.g. lamp distance). DV: time for discs to float (or number of discs floating after a set time). [1] Controlled: bicarbonate (CO₂) concentration, temperature, disc size and number, leaf type/age. [2]

c. The light-dependent stage splits water, releasing O₂, [1] which accumulates in the air spaces of the disc and makes it buoyant. [1]

d. Another factor is limiting, e.g. CO₂ concentration (bicarbonate) or temperature, [1] so extra light can't increase the rate further (or the light-dependent machinery is saturated). [1]

e. The Calvin cycle needs ATP and NADPH from the light-dependent stage. [1] Without light, these aren't regenerated and quickly run out, so CO₂ fixation and sugar production stop. [1]

f. E.g. variation between leaf discs (thickness, damage), reduced by using many discs per trial and repeating trials, then averaging. [1]

Question 3: immunity and disease (12 marks)

A new respiratory virus spreads from bats to humans.

a. What term describes a disease transmitted from animals to humans, and give one human activity that increases the risk. (2)

b. Describe how the innate immune system responds when the virus first infects the airway. (3)

c. Explain how the adaptive immune system eliminates the virus, including both free virus and infected cells. (4)

d. A vaccine is developed. Explain why a person who is vaccinated is protected even though they've never had the disease. (2)

e. Explain why the vaccine may need updating in future years. (1)

a. Zoonosis (zoonotic disease). [1] E.g. deforestation, wildlife trade, farming close to wildlife habitats. [1]

b. Infected cells release interferons, warning neighbouring cells and activating NK cells. [1] NK cells kill infected cells. [1] Macrophages and dendritic cells engulf virus particles; mast cells trigger inflammation, bringing more phagocytes. [1]

c. Dendritic cells present viral antigen on MHC II to T helper cells in lymph nodes, which release cytokines. [1] Specific B cells undergo clonal expansion into plasma cells producing antibodies that neutralise free virus and mark it for phagocytosis. [1] Cytotoxic T cells recognise viral antigen on MHC I of infected cells and kill them by apoptosis. [1] Memory B and T cells remain. [1]

d. The vaccine contains viral antigen (or mRNA for it), stimulating a primary response that produces memory cells. [1] On real exposure, memory cells mount a rapid, strong secondary response that clears the virus before disease develops. [1]

e. Antigenic drift (mutations in surface proteins) may mean memory cells no longer recognise new variants. [1]

Question 4: evolution and data analysis (12 marks)

A population of snails lives on a mainland. A sea-level rise isolates a small group on an island. Over thousands of years, the island snails develop thicker shells, a response to crab predators found only on the island.

a. Name the process by which the island population was established and explain its immediate effect on genetic diversity. (2)

b. Explain how thicker shells became common on the island. (4)

c. The two populations can no longer interbreed. Name the type of speciation and outline the steps. (3)

d. The number of DNA differences in a mitochondrial gene between the island snails and the mainland snails is 18; between the mainland snails and a third species, 42. What can be concluded? (2)

e. Explain why the fossil record of these snails might be relatively complete compared with that of worms. (1)

a. Founder effect (a form of genetic drift). [1] The small group carries only a subset of the mainland's alleles, reducing diversity and possibly changing allele frequencies by chance. [1]

b. Variation in shell thickness existed (due to different alleles). [1] Crab predation was a selection pressure: thin-shelled snails were eaten more often. [1] Thicker-shelled snails survived and reproduced more, passing on their alleles. [1] Over many generations, thick-shell alleles increased in frequency. [1]

c. Allopatric speciation. [1] The sea created a geographical barrier with no gene flow; different selection pressures (crabs) and drift changed the island gene pool; [1] eventually reproductive isolation developed. [1]

d. The island and mainland snails share a more recent common ancestor with each other than either does with the third species [1], consistent with the island population's recent origin from the mainland (molecular clock). [1]

e. Snails have hard shells (mineralised), which fossilise more readily than soft-bodied worms. [1]
