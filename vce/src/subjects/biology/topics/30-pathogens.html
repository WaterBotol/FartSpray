Antigens: self and non-self

An antigen is any molecule (usually a protein or glycoprotein on a surface) that the immune system can recognise and that can trigger an immune response.

  • Self antigens are the body's own markers. The key ones are MHC (major histocompatibility complex) proteins. MHC class I is on all nucleated cells; MHC class II is only on antigen-presenting cells (dendritic cells, macrophages, B cells). Normally the immune system tolerates self antigens.
  • Non-self antigens are foreign: on pathogens, transplanted tissue or toxins.
  • Allergens are harmless non-self antigens (pollen, dust mite proteins, peanut proteins) that trigger an inappropriate immune response in some people: mast cells release histamine, causing an allergic reaction.

The first line of defence

First-line defences are non-specific barriers that stop pathogens entering the body.

TypeAnimals (humans)Plants
PhysicalIntact skin; mucous membranes and mucus; cilia sweeping mucus out of airways; coughing and sneezing; flushing by urine and tearsWaxy cuticle; cellulose cell walls; bark; closing stomata; thorns and hairs
ChemicalLysozyme in tears and saliva (breaks bacterial cell walls); stomach acid; acidic skin secretionsAntimicrobial chemicals; toxins; enzymes that break down fungal cell walls
Microbiological (microbiota)Normal flora on skin and in gut and vagina outcompete pathogens for space and nutrientsBeneficial microbes on and around roots and leaves

Broad-spectrum antibiotics can kill the normal microbiota as well as the pathogen, removing competition and allowing opportunistic infections (e.g. thrush, or C. difficile in the gut).

  • Antibiotics don't work on viruses (no cell wall, ribosomes or metabolism of their own).
  • Viruses aren't cells, and prions contain no DNA or RNA.
  • MHC I is on almost all body cells; MHC II only on antigen-presenting cells. Red blood cells have no nucleus and no MHC I.
  • An allergen isn't a pathogen: it's harmless, but the response to it causes harm.

Distinguish between self and non-self antigens.

Self antigens are molecules (e.g. MHC markers) on the body's own cells that the immune system normally doesn't attack. Non-self antigens are foreign molecules (e.g. on pathogens) that trigger an immune response.

Explain why prions are described as pathogens even though they aren't living and contain no genetic material.

A pathogen is any agent that causes disease. [1] Prions are misfolded proteins that cause normal copies of the same protein to misfold, [1] accumulating in brain tissue and causing damage and disease, so they transmit and cause disease without nucleic acid. [1]

Describe three first-line defences of the human respiratory tract and how each prevents infection.

Mucus traps inhaled particles and pathogens. [1] Cilia beat to sweep mucus up and out of the airways to be swallowed or coughed out. [1] Coughing and sneezing forcibly expel irritants and pathogens. [1] (Also: lysozyme and antimicrobial chemicals in secretions.)

A patient with a cold asks for antibiotics. Explain why a doctor refuses.

Colds are caused by viruses, which are non-cellular and lack the structures antibiotics target (cell walls, bacterial ribosomes). Antibiotics would have no effect on the virus and would contribute to antibiotic resistance in bacteria.

Summary

  • Antigens: self (MHC I everywhere; MHC II on APCs) vs non-self; allergens are harmless triggers.
  • Cellular pathogens: bacteria, fungi, protozoa, worms. Non-cellular: viruses, prions.
  • First line: physical, chemical and microbiota barriers in animals and plants.
