Amino acids and polypeptides

There are 20 common amino acids, each with an amino group, a carboxyl group and a variable R group (side chain) attached to a central carbon. R groups can be non-polar (hydrophobic), polar, acidic (−) or basic (+). Amino acids join by condensation to form peptide bonds, making a polypeptide.

Four levels of structure

LevelDescriptionBonds / interactions
PrimaryThe sequence of amino acids (determined by the gene)Peptide bonds
SecondaryLocal folding into α-helices, β-pleated sheets or random coilsHydrogen bonds between backbone groups
TertiaryThe overall 3D shape of one polypeptideInteractions between R groups: hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, disulfide bridges, hydrophobic interactions
QuaternaryTwo or more polypeptides combined into one functional protein (e.g. haemoglobin: 4 chains; antibodies: 4 chains)The same interactions as tertiary, between chains

A protein's function depends on its 3D shape, which depends on its primary structure. Changing one amino acid (e.g. sickle-cell haemoglobin: glutamic acid → valine) can change folding and function. Denaturation (heat, pH) disrupts secondary and tertiary structure without breaking peptide bonds.

The proteome

The proteome is the complete set of proteins expressed by a cell, tissue or organism at a given time. It's larger and more variable than the genome, because of alternative splicing and modifications after translation, and because different genes are expressed in different cells and conditions.

The protein secretory pathway

  1. Ribosomes on the rough endoplasmic reticulum (RER) translate the mRNA; the growing polypeptide enters the RER lumen, where it folds (and may be modified).
  2. The protein is packaged into a transport vesicle that buds off the RER and fuses with the Golgi apparatus.
  3. The Golgi modifies (e.g. adds carbohydrate groups), sorts and packages proteins into secretory vesicles.
  4. Secretory vesicles move to the plasma membrane and fuse with it, releasing the protein outside the cell by exocytosis.

Proteins that stay in the cytosol are made on free ribosomes. Secreted proteins include insulin, digestive enzymes and antibodies.

Pancreatic cells are given radioactive amino acids for a few minutes. Over the next hour, radioactivity appears in turn in several organelles and finally outside the cell. List the order and explain it.

Proteins for export follow the secretory pathway. They're made on ribosomes attached to the rough endoplasmic reticulum, carried in vesicles to the Golgi apparatus to be modified and packaged, then released from the cell by exocytosis.

The amino acids are built into proteins on ribosomes on the RER first, so radioactivity appears in the RER.

Then in transport vesicles and the Golgi apparatus (modification and packaging), then in secretory vesicles.

RER → transport vesicles → Golgi apparatus → secretory vesicles → outside the cell (exocytosis). This is the classic pulse-chase experiment that revealed the pathway.

  • Disulfide bridges are covalent but are tertiary (or quaternary) structure, because they link R groups.
  • Secondary structure H-bonds are between backbone atoms, not R groups.
  • Denaturation ≠ digestion: peptide bonds stay intact when a protein is denatured.
  • The Golgi doesn't make proteins; it modifies and packages them.
  • A single polypeptide has no quaternary structure.

A mutation replaces a cysteine involved in a disulfide bridge with serine. Explain how this could reduce the protein's activity.

Serine can't form a disulfide bridge, so this covalent link between two parts of the chain is lost. [1] The tertiary structure becomes less stable or folds differently. [1] If the protein is an enzyme, its active site shape may change, reducing substrate binding and activity. [1]

The insulin gene is transcribed in the nucleus; mature mRNA leaves through a nuclear pore. [1] Ribosomes on the RER translate the mRNA; the polypeptide enters the RER and folds. [1] It's carried in a transport vesicle to the Golgi, which modifies (processes) and packages it into secretory vesicles. [1] Secretory vesicles fuse with the plasma membrane and release insulin by exocytosis. [1]

A student says the Golgi apparatus “makes proteins for export”. Correct the statement.

Proteins are synthesised by ribosomes on the RER. The Golgi receives them in vesicles and modifies, sorts and packages them for export; it doesn't synthesise the polypeptide chain.

Summary

  • Primary (sequence) → secondary (backbone H-bonds) → tertiary (R-group interactions) → quaternary (multiple chains).
  • The proteome is dynamic and larger than the number of genes.
  • Secretion: RER → vesicle → Golgi → secretory vesicle → exocytosis.
