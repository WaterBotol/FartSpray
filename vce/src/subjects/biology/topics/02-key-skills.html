Investigation methodologies

VCAA can ask you to name the methodology used, or to justify why it suits an investigation.

MethodologyWhat it isWhen it's the right choice
Case studyIn-depth study of a real situation or event (past or current), analysing causes and consequences and making recommendationsMonitoring and learning from real outbreaks (e.g. WHO tracking swine flu), when experiments would be unsafe or unethical
Classification and identificationSorting organisms or samples into groups, or identifying them, using features or DNAIdentifying species from eDNA or morphology; classifying a new plant
Controlled experimentChanging one independent variable, keeping others constant, with a control groupTesting cause and effect in a lab, e.g. light intensity vs rate of photosynthesis
Correlational studyMeasuring two variables to see if they're related, without manipulating eitherComparing groups you can't assign (e.g. people with and without a disease); shows association, not cause

ModellingUsing a physical, conceptual or mathematical modelRepresenting processes too small, large, slow or complex to observe directly
Product, process or system developmentDesigning and testing a solution to a problemDesigning a better vaccine delivery system or eDNA sampling kit
SimulationImitating a real process, often with software, when the real thing is impracticalModelling how an allele frequency changes over many generations

Opinion, anecdote and evidence

  • Opinion: a personal view or belief, not necessarily based on data (“I think this vaccine is unsafe”).
  • Anecdote: a personal story about one event or observation (“My cousin got sick after the vaccine”). It's subjective and can't be generalised.
  • Evidence: data collected systematically and objectively, ideally repeatable and reproducible (a large clinical trial with a control group).
  • Scientific ideas are testable and can be falsified by evidence; non-scientific ideas can't be tested this way.

Models and their limitations

Scientists use models (diagrams, flowcharts, physical models, simulations) to organise and explain phenomena, such as the model of protein synthesis or the lock-and-key/induced-fit model of enzymes. Be ready to give a limitation of the model itself (not of the process it describes) and a way around it:

  • It's simplified: e.g. protein synthesis shown as a neat linear sequence, leaving out gene regulation, alternative splicing, protein folding or differences between prokaryotes and eukaryotes. Fix: use several models or more detailed ones.
  • It may not fit new discoveries. Fix: refine or replace the model as evidence builds.
  • A simulation assumes ideal conditions and gives a general trend, missing real-world variation. Fix: compare with experimental data.

Quality of data

TermMeaning
AccuracyHow close a measurement is to the true value
PrecisionHow close repeated measurements are to each other
RepeatabilitySame results when the same person repeats the method with the same equipment
ReproducibilitySame results when others repeat it (different people, equipment, locations)
ValidityWhether the experiment actually tests the hypothesis (controlled variables, suitable method)
Random errorUnpredictable variation; reduced by repeating and averaging (affects precision)
Systematic errorA consistent bias (e.g. uncalibrated equipment); not fixed by repeats (affects accuracy)
Personal errorA mistake (e.g. misreading); these results should be discarded and redone, not averaged in
OutlierA result far from the others; investigate, and repeat if possible

Presenting and analysing data

  • Line graphs for continuous independent variables; bar charts for categories. IV on the x-axis, with labels and units.
  • Describe trends with specific data values; identify patterns, anomalies and limitations.
  • Correlation isn't causation: a link between two variables may be due to a third factor.
  • Conclusions must refer back to the hypothesis (supported or not) and state limitations.

Bioethics

Exam questions on gene editing, GMOs, vaccination, cloning, reproductive technologies or animal research often ask you to “discuss the ethical issues”. VCAA expects you to use named approaches and concepts, applied to the specific scenario.

Ethical approachThe question it asks
Consequences-basedWhich option produces the best overall outcomes (most benefit, least harm) for those affected?
Duty/rule-basedWhich option follows our duties and moral rules (e.g. “do not harm”, “obtain consent”), whatever the outcome?
Virtues-basedWhat would a person of good character (honest, compassionate, responsible) do?
Ethical conceptMeaning
IntegrityHonesty and transparency in conducting and reporting research
JusticeFair distribution of benefits, risks and access (e.g. who can afford a new therapy?)
BeneficenceMaximising benefits and doing good
Non-maleficenceAvoiding or minimising harm
RespectFor the autonomy, rights and dignity of people (e.g. informed consent) and for the welfare of other living things

Social, economic, legal and political factors

Bioethics questions also ask how these factors influence decisions. Link each one to the scenario:

  • Social: public acceptance or fear (e.g. consumers avoiding products labelled GM), cultural beliefs, education needed, equity of access.
  • Economic: who saves or makes money. Vaccinating costs less than treating disease; fewer hospitalisations and less time off work save money; new crops need research and marketing costs.
  • Legal: regulations, labelling laws, patents, approval processes, consent requirements.
  • Political: government funding and priorities, public health programs (e.g. which vaccines the National Immunisation Program funds), international agreements.

In 2018 a researcher announced the birth of twins whose embryos had been edited with CRISPR-Cas9 to disable the CCR5 gene, aiming to make them resistant to HIV. The work was widely condemned and the researcher was jailed. Discuss two ethical issues, referring to ethical concepts or approaches.

A bioethics answer names an ethical concept or principle, applies it to the specific facts of the case, and ideally contrasts approaches. Consequences-based thinking weighs outcomes (benefits against harms); duty-based thinking asks whether the action follows rules and obligations regardless of outcome. Integrity, justice, beneficence, non-maleficence and respect are the principles VCAA names.

Non-maleficence: CRISPR can cause off-target edits and mosaicism, and the long-term effects of disabling CCR5 were unknown, so the children were exposed to possible harm with little medical need (safer ways of preventing HIV transmission already existed).

Respect and consent: the edits are heritable, so future generations are affected without being able to consent; the parents' consent was reportedly not properly informed.

A consequences-based argument might weigh possible benefits (HIV resistance) against risks; a duty-based argument would say researchers have a duty not to impose unnecessary risk and to follow agreed rules, which this work broke (also a failure of integrity). Two issues, each named with a concept and applied to the case, would earn full marks.

A company develops a genetically modified drought-tolerant wheat and patents the seed, which farmers must buy each season. Discuss one argument for and one against its use, referring to ethical concepts.

For (beneficence): higher, more reliable yields in drought could improve food security and farmers' incomes as the climate changes. [2]

Against (justice): patented seed that must be bought each season may be unaffordable for poorer farmers, concentrating benefits with the company and wealthier farmers; possible ecological risks (gene flow to wild relatives) also raise non-maleficence concerns. [2]

Researchers compared the number of eosinophils in intestinal tissue from eight people with Crohn's disease and eight people without it. The scientific investigation methodology of this study is

  1. a case study
  2. a correlational study
  3. a controlled experiment
  4. classification and identification

Nothing was manipulated: the researchers measured two groups they couldn't assign, looking for an association between the disease and eosinophil numbers. That's correlational, not a controlled experiment.

Unit 4 investigation poster: checklist

The full guide, with investigation ideas and a worked plan, is on the investigation and poster page.

  • Title (question), introduction with relevant biological background, aim, hypothesis.
  • Methodology summary including variables, controls and safety/ethics.
  • Results: clear tables and graphs, with units and uncertainty where relevant.
  • Discussion: interpret trends, link to biology, evaluate errors and limitations, suggest improvements.
  • Conclusion: answer the question; state whether the hypothesis is supported.
  • References and acknowledgements; stay within the word limit.
