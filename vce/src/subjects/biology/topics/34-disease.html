Why new diseases emerge

  • Zoonotic spillover: pathogens jump from animals to humans. Examples: SARS-CoV-2 (COVID-19, identified in 2019), HIV (from primates), Hendra virus (from flying foxes via horses, in Australia). Deforestation, wildlife trade, farming and urban growth increase human–animal contact.
  • Antigenic drift: small, gradual mutations in viral surface antigens (e.g. influenza haemagglutinin). Memory cells recognise the virus less well, which is why flu vaccines are updated each year.
  • Antigenic shift: a sudden, major change when two different strains (e.g. from birds and humans) infect the same host (often pigs) and swap gene segments. Nobody is immune to the new strain, so it can cause a pandemic.
  • Re-emerging diseases: falling vaccination rates (measles), antibiotic resistance (tuberculosis), climate change expanding vector ranges (mosquito-borne diseases).

Pandemics are driven by: a new pathogen (no existing immunity), efficient person-to-person transmission (droplets, aerosols), transmission before symptoms, and global travel.

Case study: European arrival and Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples

When Europeans arrived in Australia from 1788, they brought pathogens that Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples had never been exposed to, including smallpox, measles, influenza, tuberculosis and sexually transmitted infections.

  • No prior exposure: with no memory cells for these pathogens and no herd immunity, infections spread rapidly and were often severe. A smallpox epidemic around Sydney in 1789 killed a large proportion of the Aboriginal people of the region, and further epidemics followed along trade routes and with expanding colonisation.
  • Social and environmental factors: dispossession of land cut access to traditional food and water sources, and forced relocation onto missions and reserves brought crowding, poor sanitation and malnutrition, all of which increase transmission and severity of disease.
  • Lasting impacts: these events contributed to large population losses and to health inequities that continue today.
  • Community leadership: in 2020, Aboriginal community-controlled health organisations led early, culturally informed COVID-19 responses that helped keep infection rates in many communities very low, showing the value of community knowledge in controlling spread.

Identifying and controlling spread

  • Identify: surveillance and testing (e.g. PCR tests, genome sequencing to track variants), epidemiology (who, where, when, how).
  • Control: isolation of cases and quarantine of contacts; contact tracing; hygiene (handwashing), masks and ventilation; social distancing and travel restrictions; vector control (e.g. mosquito nets, removing breeding sites); vaccination; treatment (antivirals, antibiotics for bacteria).

Immunotherapy and monoclonal antibodies

Immunotherapy uses or modifies the immune system to treat disease. Monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) are identical antibodies, all specific to one antigen, made by a single clone of cells.

Making monoclonal antibodies (hybridoma technology)

  1. A mouse is injected with the target antigen; it produces B cells (plasma cells) making antibodies against it.
  2. B cells are collected from its spleen and fused with myeloma (cancerous B) cells, which divide indefinitely.
  3. The resulting hybridoma cells make antibody and divide indefinitely. They're screened to find those making the desired antibody.
  4. The chosen hybridoma is cloned and cultured to produce large amounts of the monoclonal antibody. (Modern therapeutic mAbs are “humanised” to reduce immune reactions.)

Monoclonal antibody therapy is artificial passive immunity: it works quickly but doesn't produce memory, so repeated doses are needed.

  • Drift vs shift: drift is gradual mutation; shift is sudden reassortment of genes between strains.
  • Pandemic is about spread, not how deadly a disease is.
  • Quarantine is for exposed people who might be infected; isolation is for confirmed cases.
  • Myeloma cells provide immortality; the B cells provide the specific antibody.

Outline three strategies that were used to control the spread of COVID-19 and explain how each works.

Isolation and quarantine: infected and exposed people are separated so they can't infect others. Masks and ventilation: reduce spread of respiratory droplets and aerosols. Vaccination: builds immunity, reducing severe illness and (partly) transmission. (Also contact tracing, testing, distancing, travel restrictions.)

A student says monoclonal antibody treatment gives long-term immunity. Evaluate this.

Incorrect: it's artificial passive immunity. The injected antibodies are broken down over weeks, and the patient's own B cells don't produce memory cells, so the effect fades and repeat doses are needed.

The pathogens (e.g. smallpox, measles, influenza) were new to Australia, so no one had memory B or T cells against them and there was no herd immunity. Summary

  • European arrival introduced pathogens to which Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples had no immunity; dispossession worsened their impact.
  • New diseases: zoonoses, antigenic drift (gradual) and shift (sudden); re-emergence via low vaccination, resistance and climate change.
  • Control: surveillance, isolation, quarantine, tracing, hygiene, vaccination.
  • Monoclonal antibodies: B cell + myeloma → hybridoma → identical antibodies; used for cancer (targeting, checkpoint inhibitors) and autoimmune disease.
