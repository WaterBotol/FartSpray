Biochemical pathways

A biochemical pathway is a series of linked reactions where the product of one step is the substrate of the next, and each step is catalysed by a specific enzyme: A →(enzyme 1)→ B →(enzyme 2)→ C … Photosynthesis and cellular respiration are pathways. Cells control pathways by controlling enzymes (including by feedback inhibition, where the end product inhibits an early enzyme).

How enzymes work

  • Enzymes are (mostly) proteins that lower the activation energy of a reaction. They're not used up and don't change the products.
  • The substrate binds to the active site, a region with a specific shape and chemical properties set by the enzyme's tertiary structure, forming an enzyme–substrate complex.
  • Induced fit model: the active site changes shape slightly as the substrate binds, giving a tighter fit that strains the substrate's bonds. (The older lock-and-key model treats the active site as rigid.)
  • Specificity: each enzyme catalyses one reaction or a small group of similar reactions.

Coenzymes: loaded and unloaded

Coenzymes are non-protein organic molecules that help enzymes by carrying energy, electrons or hydrogen ions between reactions. They cycle between an unloaded and a loaded form.

Factors affecting enzyme activity

FactorEffect
TemperatureRate rises with temperature (more frequent, energetic collisions) up to the optimum (about 37 °C for many human enzymes). Above that, the enzyme denatures: H-bonds and other interactions break, the active site changes shape, and the rate falls steeply (usually irreversibly). Low temperature slows the enzyme but doesn't denature it.
pHEach enzyme has an optimum pH (pepsin ≈ 2, trypsin ≈ 8). Other pH values change the charges on R groups, disrupting ionic and H-bonds and the active site.
Substrate concentrationRate increases until all active sites are occupied (saturation), then plateaus. The enzyme concentration is then limiting.
Enzyme concentrationWith excess substrate, rate is proportional to enzyme concentration.
Competitive inhibitorSimilar shape to the substrate; binds the active site, blocking it. Can be overcome by adding more substrate.
Non-competitive inhibitorBinds elsewhere (an allosteric site), changing the active site's shape. Adding substrate doesn't restore the maximum rate.

An enzyme's rate is measured at increasing substrate concentrations with and without inhibitor X. At low substrate concentration, X greatly reduces the rate; at very high substrate concentration, the rate with X is the same as without it. What type of inhibitor is X? Explain.

At high substrate concentration, substrate molecules outcompete X for the active sites.

So the maximum rate is eventually reached. That only happens if X competes for the active site.

Competitive inhibitor. A non-competitive inhibitor would lower the maximum rate no matter how much substrate was added.

  • Enzymes aren't alive, so they're denatured, not “killed”.
  • ATP is the loaded form; ADP is unloaded.
  • At high temperatures the rate falls because of denaturation, not because particles move slower.
  • Non-competitive inhibitors don't compete for the active site, so more substrate doesn't help.
  • Enzymes don't provide energy; they lower the activation energy.

A describes lock-and-key.

Explain why the rate of an enzyme-catalysed reaction decreases sharply above the optimum temperature.

High temperatures break hydrogen bonds and other weak interactions in the enzyme's tertiary structure. [1] The active site changes shape (denaturation). [1] The substrate can no longer bind (fewer enzyme–substrate complexes form), so the rate decreases. [1]

Distinguish between the loaded and unloaded forms of a coenzyme, using NAD⁺/NADH as an example, and explain why cells need the unloaded form to be regenerated.

NAD⁺ (unloaded) accepts electrons and a hydrogen ion from a reaction to become NADH (loaded). [1] NADH carries them to another reaction (the ETC), where it's unloaded back to NAD⁺. [1] A cell has limited NAD⁺; if it isn't regenerated (e.g. when there's no O₂ for the ETC), glycolysis stops. That's why fermentation regenerates NAD⁺. [1]

A student investigates the effect of pH on catalase from potato. Identify the independent and dependent variables, two controlled variables, and predict the results.

Independent variable: pH (e.g. buffers of pH 3, 5, 7, 9, 11). Dependent variable: rate of reaction (e.g. volume of O₂ produced per minute). [1]

Controlled: temperature, volume and concentration of hydrogen peroxide, mass and surface area of potato, reaction time. [2]

Prediction: rate increases to a maximum near the optimum pH (about 7 for potato catalase), then decreases at more extreme pH as the enzyme's active site is altered. [1]

A student adds more substrate to a reaction containing a non-competitive inhibitor and expects the maximum rate to return to normal. Explain why it won't.

A non-competitive inhibitor binds to a site other than the active site and changes the active site's shape, so affected enzyme molecules can't catalyse at all. Extra substrate can't displace it, so the maximum rate stays lower.

Summary

  • Pathways: multistep, each step with a specific enzyme.
  • Loaded/unloaded coenzymes: ATP/ADP, NADH/NAD⁺, FADH₂/FAD, NADPH/NADP⁺.
  • Temperature and pH have optimums; denaturation changes the active site.
  • Competitive: active site, overcome by substrate. Non-competitive: other site, lowers maximum rate.
