Which region of a eukaryotic gene is not transcribed into pre-mRNA?

  2. introns
  3. the promoter
  4. the 5′ untranslated region (UTR)

The promoter is where RNA polymerase binds. Transcription starts downstream of it, so exons, introns and the UTRs are all transcribed.

  1. 300 amino acids
  2. 301 amino acids
  3. 903 amino acids
  4. 900 amino acids

903 ÷ 3 = 301 codons. The stop codon codes for no amino acid, so there are 300.

Humans have about 20 000 protein-coding genes but make many more different proteins. The main reason is

  1. each gene has many promoters
  2. alternative splicing allows different combinations of exons from one gene
  3. tRNA can carry several different amino acids
  4. introns are translated

Different exons can be retained or removed, producing different mature mRNAs, and so different proteins, from one gene.

Deleting a single nucleotide near the start of a gene’s coding sequence is most likely to

  3. shift the reading frame, changing most of the amino acids that follow

Every codon after the deletion is read in a different frame. This usually produces a nonsense sequence and often an early stop codon.

E. coli uses two mechanisms to reduce transcription of the trp operon when tryptophan is abundant.

  • The active repressor binds the operator, blocking RNA polymerase from transcribing the structural genes

Coenzymes link the stages of cellular respiration.

Explain the role of NAD⁺/NADH in aerobic respiration.

  • NAD⁺ accepts electrons and H⁺ during glycolysis and the Krebs cycle, forming NADH (loaded)
  • NADH carries them to the electron transport chain, where they are used to make ATP, regenerating NAD⁺

During glycolysis and the Krebs cycle, NAD⁺ picks up electrons and H⁺ to become NADH. NADH delivers them to the ETC, where their energy drives ATP synthesis, and NAD⁺ is regenerated for reuse.

Explain why fermentation is needed for glycolysis to continue when oxygen is absent.

  • Glycolysis requires NAD⁺; without O₂ the ETC stops and NADH cannot be unloaded there
  • Fermentation converts pyruvate to lactate (or ethanol + CO₂), oxidising NADH back to NAD⁺ so glycolysis can continue

Glycolysis needs a supply of NAD⁺. Without oxygen the ETC stops, so NADH builds up. Fermentation uses NADH to reduce pyruvate to lactate (or ethanol and CO₂), regenerating NAD⁺ so glycolysis, and its 2 ATP, can continue.

“Fermentation makes ATP” is wrong. Its job is regenerating NAD⁺. The ATP comes from glycolysis.

The frequency of g will increase. Predation is the selection pressure: brown beetles are better camouflaged, so more of them survive to reproduce and pass on g. Over generations g becomes more common.

Selection acts on phenotype. As G becomes rare it is found mostly in heterozygotes, which are green and still selected against, so G keeps declining. But it can persist at low frequency through new mutations or gene flow, and elimination takes many generations.

A hominin fossil is found in a sedimentary layer lying between two volcanic ash layers. The lower ash layer is dated at 2.4 million years and the upper at 2.0 million years. Approximate DNA sequence differences between humans and other apes: chimpanzee 1.2%, gorilla 1.6%, orangutan 3.1%.

Explain why the fossil record is incomplete.

  • Fossilisation requires specific conditions (rapid burial, low oxygen, hard parts), so most organisms never fossilise
  • Many fossils are destroyed (erosion, tectonic activity) or have not been found

Fossils only form under rare conditions, such as rapid burial in sediment without oxygen, and soft-bodied organisms seldom fossilise. Many fossils are later destroyed by erosion or geological activity, or simply haven’t been found.

Estimate the age of the fossil, and name the dating technique used on the ash layers.

  • Between 2.0 and 2.4 million years old
  • Radiometric (absolute) dating, e.g. potassium–argon dating

Between 2.0 and 2.4 million years old. The ash layers are dated radiometrically, for example by potassium–argon dating.

Explain why carbon-14 dating cannot be used on this fossil.

  • C-14 has a short half-life (≈ 5730 years), so it is only useful for material up to about 50 000 years old

C-14’s half-life is about 5730 years, so after roughly 50 000 years too little remains to measure.

Use the DNA data to identify humans’ closest living relative, and justify your answer.

  • Chimpanzee
  • It has the smallest DNA difference (1.2%), so it shares the most recent common ancestor with humans

The chimpanzee. Its DNA differs least from ours (1.2%), meaning fewer mutations have accumulated since we shared a common ancestor, so the split was most recent.

Describe one piece of evidence supporting the “Out of Africa” model of modern human origins.

  • e.g. The oldest Homo sapiens fossils (≈ 300 000 years) are found in Africa
  • e.g. African populations have the greatest mtDNA/genetic diversity, suggesting they are the oldest

The oldest Homo sapiens fossils, about 300 000 years old, are from Africa. African populations also have the greatest genetic diversity, which is expected in the region where a species has existed longest.

Explain why mitochondrial DNA is used to trace human migration.

  • It is inherited only from the mother and does not recombine, so lineages can be traced
  • It mutates at a relatively constant rate, acting as a molecular clock to estimate when populations diverged

mtDNA passes only down the maternal line without recombination, so each lineage can be followed. Mutations accumulate at a fairly steady rate, so differences between populations estimate when they separated.

General comments

This paper matched the demand of a typical VCAA Biology exam. Most marks needed you to apply a process to a scenario (a mutant repressor, a plasmid map, leaf discs) rather than recite it. Students who referred explicitly to the stimulus, by quoting data, naming the enzyme or giving the fragment sizes, consistently scored higher than those who wrote textbook paragraphs.

Question-type patterns

  • Experimental data (Question 5). “Describe and explain” needs the trend with data (describe) and the biology behind it (explain). A plateau always means another factor is limiting, so name one.
  • Restriction mapping (Question 3). Sketch the circle. Circular DNA cut n times gives n fragments.
  • Mechanism questions (Questions 2 and 7). Marks are for each link in the chain. Describing attenuation without the ribosome or the terminator hairpin left marks on the table.
  • Allele frequencies (Question 9). Count alleles, not individuals. Drift = chance; selection = differential survival and reproduction because of a phenotype.

Common errors

  • Saying fermentation “produces ATP”, when its role is regenerating NAD⁺.
  • Confusing transgenic with genetically modified (Question 4c).
  • Writing “the virus adapts” or “the beetles become brown”. Populations evolve, individuals don’t.
  • Giving the age of a fossil between two ash layers as a single number.

Advice

Aim for 75% or more before the harder paper. If your Section B marks lagged behind your Section A score, practise “describe and explain” questions using the key-skills page, and always quote the stimulus.
