Biology · Practice exam 1 (easier)

  • Reading time: 15 minutes. Writing time: 2 hours 30 minutes.
  • Section A: 40 multiple-choice questions (40 marks). Section B: 10 short-answer questions (80 marks).
  • Allow about 1 minute per multiple-choice question and about 1.2 minutes per mark in Section B.
  • Answer in full sentences. Use precise terms: “complementary”, “specific”, “allele frequency”.

Section A – Multiple-choice questions

Answer all questions. Choose the response that is correct or best answers the question. A correct answer scores 1; an incorrect answer scores 0. No marks are deducted for incorrect answers.

The monomers of nucleic acids are

  1. amino acids
  2. nucleotides
  3. monosaccharides
  4. fatty acids

Each nucleotide contains a phosphate, a pentose sugar and a nitrogenous base.

In a DNA molecule, complementary base pairing occurs between

  1. A and G; C and T
  2. A and T; C and G
  3. A and U; C and G
  4. A and C; G and T

Adenine pairs with thymine (2 hydrogen bonds) and cytosine with guanine (3 hydrogen bonds).

Compared with DNA, RNA

  1. contains deoxyribose and thymine
  2. contains ribose and uracil
  3. is always double-stranded
  4. contains no phosphate

RNA has ribose sugar, uracil in place of thymine, and is usually single-stranded.

In eukaryotic cells, transcription takes place in the

  1. cytosol
  2. nucleus
  3. ribosome
  4. Golgi apparatus

DNA stays in the nucleus. The mRNA is made there and exported to the ribosomes.

A tRNA molecule has the anticodon 3′-UAC-5′. The mRNA codon it pairs with is

  1. 5′-ATG-3′
  2. 5′-AUG-3′
  3. 5′-UAC-3′
  4. 5′-TAC-3′

The anticodon is complementary to the codon: U pairs with A, A with U, C with G. RNA contains no thymine.

Introns are removed from pre-mRNA during

  1. translation
  2. RNA processing (splicing)
  3. replication
  4. post-translational modification

In the nucleus, splicing removes introns and joins the exons. A 5′ cap and a poly-A tail are also added.

A regulatory gene

  1. codes for a protein that controls the expression of other genes
  2. codes for a structural protein such as keratin
  3. is never transcribed
  4. is found only in eukaryotes

Regulatory genes code for proteins (for example repressors and transcription factors) that switch other genes on or off.

When tryptophan levels in an E. coli cell are high,

  1. the repressor is inactive and the trp genes are transcribed
  2. tryptophan binds to the repressor, which then binds to the operator and blocks transcription
  3. RNA polymerase binds more strongly to the promoter
  4. the trp genes are translated faster

Tryptophan acts as a corepressor. It activates the repressor, which binds the operator and blocks RNA polymerase.

The sequence of amino acids in a polypeptide is its

  1. primary structure
  2. secondary structure
  3. tertiary structure
  4. quaternary structure

All the higher levels of structure depend on the primary sequence.

The correct sequence in the secretory pathway for a protein hormone is

  1. Golgi → rough ER → vesicle → plasma membrane
  2. ribosome on rough ER → rough ER → vesicle → Golgi → secretory vesicle → plasma membrane
  3. nucleus → smooth ER → lysosome → plasma membrane
  4. ribosome in cytosol → mitochondrion → plasma membrane

The protein is made at the rough ER and folded in its lumen. It is transported in a vesicle to the Golgi for modification and packaging, then released by exocytosis.

The enzyme that joins DNA fragments by forming phosphodiester bonds is

  1. restriction endonuclease
  2. DNA ligase
  3. DNA polymerase
  4. reverse transcriptase

Ligase seals the sugar–phosphate backbone.

In gel electrophoresis, DNA fragments move towards the positive electrode because

  1. DNA is positively charged
  2. the phosphate groups give DNA a negative charge
  3. smaller fragments are attracted to the positive electrode
  4. the gel is positively charged

The phosphate backbone carries negative charges, so all DNA moves towards the anode (+). Smaller fragments move further.

In PCR, the step carried out at about 95 °C

  1. allows primers to bind
  2. separates the two DNA strands (denaturation)
  3. allows Taq polymerase to extend the new strands
  4. joins the fragments together

Heating breaks the hydrogen bonds between the strands. Annealing happens at about 55 °C and extension at about 72 °C.

In CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing, the role of Cas9 is to

  1. find the target sequence by base pairing
  2. cut both strands of the DNA at the site specified by the guide RNA
  3. join the new gene into the chromosome
  4. copy the target gene

The guide RNA finds the target by complementary base pairing. Cas9 is the endonuclease that makes the cut.

A plasmid is

  1. a protein that cuts DNA
  2. a small circular DNA molecule found in bacteria, used as a vector
  3. a type of virus
  4. the DNA in a chloroplast

Plasmids replicate independently of the bacterial chromosome and are easy to cut and rejoin, which makes them useful vectors.

A transgenic organism is one that

  1. has had one of its own genes deleted
  2. contains a gene from a different species
  3. has been cloned
  4. has been selectively bred

All transgenic organisms are GMOs, but not all GMOs are transgenic. A gene can be edited or silenced without adding one from another species.

Enzymes increase the rate of reactions by

  1. increasing the temperature of the cell
  2. lowering the activation energy
  3. increasing the energy released by the reaction
  4. changing the products of the reaction

Enzymes provide an alternative pathway with a lower activation energy. ΔG and the products are unchanged.

Which pair shows the loaded (high-energy) and unloaded forms of a coenzyme, in that order?

  1. ADP, ATP
  2. NADH, NAD⁺
  3. NADP⁺, NADPH
  4. NAD⁺, NADH

NADH is loaded with electrons and H⁺ and becomes NAD⁺ when it unloads. Likewise ATP → ADP + Pi and NADPH → NADP⁺.

A competitive inhibitor

  1. binds to the active site, blocking the substrate
  2. binds to an allosteric site and permanently changes the enzyme
  3. increases the rate of reaction
  4. denatures the enzyme

It resembles the substrate and competes for the active site. Adding more substrate reduces its effect.

The light-dependent stage of photosynthesis takes place in the

  1. stroma
  2. thylakoid membranes (grana)
  3. cytosol
  4. mitochondrial matrix

Chlorophyll and the electron transport proteins are embedded in the thylakoid membranes.

The inputs to the light-independent stage (Calvin cycle) are

  1. H₂O, light and NADP⁺
  2. CO₂, ATP and NADPH
  3. O₂, glucose and ADP
  4. CO₂, H₂O and light

CO₂ is fixed, and ATP and NADPH from the light-dependent stage supply the energy and hydrogen needed to make glucose.

The enzyme rubisco

  1. splits water in the light-dependent stage
  2. catalyses the fixation of CO₂ in the Calvin cycle (and can also bind O₂)
  3. makes ATP in the thylakoid
  4. breaks down glucose

Rubisco fixes CO₂ onto RuBP. It can also bind O₂, which leads to wasteful photorespiration.

C₄ plants reduce photorespiration by

  1. closing their stomata during the day
  2. fixing CO₂ in mesophyll cells and concentrating it in bundle-sheath cells, where the Calvin cycle occurs
  3. carrying out photosynthesis only at night
  4. not using rubisco

C₄ plants separate the steps in space. CAM plants separate them in time, opening their stomata at night.

Glycolysis takes place in the

  1. mitochondrial matrix
  2. cristae
  3. cytosol
  4. nucleus

Glucose is split into two pyruvate in the cytosol, with no oxygen needed.

Most of the ATP produced in aerobic respiration is made in the

  1. glycolysis stage
  2. Krebs cycle
  3. electron transport chain
  4. fermentation stage

The ETC produces about 26 or 28 ATP per glucose. Glycolysis and the Krebs cycle make 2 each.

In yeast, anaerobic fermentation of glucose produces

  1. lactate and 2 ATP
  2. ethanol, CO₂ and 2 ATP
  3. ethanol, O₂ and 30 ATP
  4. CO₂, water and 32 ATP

Alcoholic fermentation regenerates NAD⁺ so glycolysis can continue. The only ATP comes from glycolysis.

Which is a non-cellular pathogen?

  1. a bacterium
  2. a fungus
  3. a virus
  4. a protozoan

Viruses (and prions) are non-cellular. They have no cytoplasm or ribosomes of their own.

A feature shared by all hominins is

  1. a large brain (over 1000 cm³)
  2. habitual bipedalism
  3. the use of fire
  4. a prominent chin

Walking upright on two legs is the defining feature of hominins. Large brains, fire use and chins appear only in later species.

During inflammation, histamine released by mast cells

  1. kills bacteria directly
  2. causes vasodilation and makes capillaries more permeable
  3. produces antibodies
  4. activates B cells

Increased blood flow and leakier capillaries bring phagocytes and complement proteins to the site.

Neutrophils and macrophages are both

  1. antibody-producing cells
  2. phagocytes
  3. T lymphocytes
  4. memory cells

Both engulf and digest pathogens. Macrophages also act as antigen-presenting cells.

Antibodies are produced and secreted by

  1. helper T cells
  2. plasma cells
  3. cytotoxic T cells
  4. macrophages

Activated B cells divide and differentiate into plasma cells, which secrete antibodies, and into memory B cells.

Cytotoxic T cells

  1. secrete antibodies
  2. destroy infected or cancerous body cells that display a specific antigen
  3. engulf bacteria
  4. release histamine

They recognise antigen presented on MHC class I molecules and trigger apoptosis in the infected cell.

MHC class I markers are found on

  1. all nucleated body cells
  2. antigen-presenting cells only
  3. red blood cells only
  4. bacteria only

MHC I marks cells as “self” and displays fragments of proteins made inside the cell. MHC II is found only on antigen-presenting cells.

A vaccine provides

  1. natural active immunity
  2. artificial active immunity
  3. natural passive immunity
  4. artificial passive immunity

Vaccination is artificial exposure, and the person’s own immune system responds and makes memory cells, so the immunity is active.

Antibodies passed from mother to baby in breast milk provide

  1. natural active immunity
  2. artificial active immunity
  3. natural passive immunity
  4. artificial passive immunity

The antibodies are received, not made by the baby (passive), and are transferred naturally.

Monoclonal antibodies used in cancer immunotherapy

  1. are identical antibodies that bind to one specific antigen on target cells
  2. contain a weakened form of the cancer
  3. are antibiotics
  4. stimulate the production of memory cells

Monoclonal antibodies come from a single B-cell clone, so they all bind the same antigen. They can block signals or flag cancer cells for destruction.

Genetic drift has its greatest effect on allele frequencies in

  1. large populations
  2. small populations
  3. populations with high gene flow
  4. populations under strong selection

Chance events change allele frequencies far more in small populations.

The only source of new alleles in a population is

  1. natural selection
  2. mutation
  3. genetic drift
  4. selective breeding

Selection, drift and gene flow change the frequencies of existing alleles. Mutation creates new ones. (Gene flow brings in alleles that are new to that population.)

Allopatric speciation begins with

  1. a mutation in one individual
  2. the geographical isolation of populations
  3. hybridisation of two species
  4. a change in diet

A physical barrier stops gene flow. The separated populations then diverge through mutation, drift and different selection pressures until they are reproductively isolated.

In undisturbed sedimentary rock, fossils in deeper layers are

  1. younger than those in the layers above
  2. older than those in the layers above
  3. the same age as those in the layers above
  4. impossible to compare

The law of superposition. This is relative dating: it gives the order of events, not an age in years.

Section B – Short-answer questions

Answer all questions in the spaces provided. Write in full sentences where appropriate.

Part of the template strand of a gene has the base sequence 3′-TAC GGA TTC ACT-5′.

CodonAUGCCUAAGUGACCA
Amino acidMet (start)ProLysstopPro

Write the mRNA sequence transcribed from this template, labelling the 5′ and 3′ ends.

  • Correct bases: AUG CCU AAG UGA (uracil, not thymine)
  • Correct orientation: 5′ … 3′

5′-AUG CCU AAG UGA-3′

Writing T instead of U, or leaving the ends unlabelled, cost many students a mark.

Give the amino acid sequence coded for.

  • Met–Pro–Lys
  • Translation stops at UGA (no amino acid added)

Met–Pro–Lys, then stop.

Describe the role of tRNA in translation.

  • Each tRNA carries a specific amino acid to the ribosome
  • Its anticodon pairs with the complementary mRNA codon, so the amino acids are added in the correct order

Each tRNA carries one specific amino acid. At the ribosome its anticodon binds to the complementary codon on the mRNA, so amino acids are added in the order the mRNA specifies.

A mutation changes the second codon from CCU to CCA. Explain why this has no effect on the protein.

  • The genetic code is degenerate (redundant): more than one codon codes for the same amino acid
  • CCA also codes for Pro, so the amino acid sequence is unchanged (a silent mutation)

The code is degenerate. CCA and CCU both code for proline, so the protein is unchanged. This is a silent mutation.

Name the bond that forms between adjacent amino acids.

  • Peptide bond

A peptide bond, formed by a condensation reaction.

In E. coli, the trp operon contains five structural genes that code for enzymes needed to make the amino acid tryptophan.

Distinguish between structural genes and regulatory genes.

  • Structural genes code for proteins with a structural or functional role in the cell (e.g. enzymes)
  • Regulatory genes code for proteins (e.g. repressors) that control the expression of other genes

Structural genes code for proteins that do jobs in the cell, such as the trp enzymes. Regulatory genes code for proteins that switch other genes on or off, such as the trp repressor.

Describe what happens at the trp operon when tryptophan is scarce.

  • The repressor is inactive (no tryptophan bound), so it cannot bind the operator
  • RNA polymerase binds the promoter and transcribes the structural genes, so the enzymes are made

With little tryptophan, the repressor stays inactive and cannot bind the operator. RNA polymerase transcribes the structural genes, and the enzymes for making tryptophan are produced.

Explain the advantage to the bacterium of regulating the trp operon in this way.

  • It saves energy and resources by only making the enzymes when tryptophan is needed

The bacterium doesn’t waste energy and amino acids making enzymes when tryptophan is already available.

Name the DNA region to which RNA polymerase binds.

  • Promoter

The promoter.

What is an operon?

  • A cluster of structural genes controlled by a single promoter (and operator), transcribed together

A group of genes under the control of one promoter and operator, which are transcribed together as one mRNA.

Insulin is a protein hormone made by beta cells in the pancreas. It consists of two polypeptide chains held together by disulfide bonds.

State the highest level of protein structure shown by insulin, and justify your answer.

  • Quaternary structure
  • It is made of more than one polypeptide chain

Quaternary, because it consists of two polypeptide chains joined together.

Describe the pathway insulin takes from its synthesis to its release from the cell.

  • Synthesised by ribosomes on the rough ER and enters the ER lumen, where it folds
  • Transported in vesicles to the Golgi apparatus, where it is modified and packaged
  • Secretory vesicles fuse with the plasma membrane and release insulin by exocytosis

Ribosomes on the rough ER make the polypeptide, which enters the ER lumen and folds. Transport vesicles carry it to the Golgi, where it is modified and packaged into secretory vesicles. These fuse with the plasma membrane and release insulin by exocytosis.

Define the term proteome.

  • The complete set of proteins expressed by a cell, tissue or organism at a particular time

All the proteins expressed by a cell or organism at a given time.

Explain why changing a single amino acid could make insulin non-functional.

  • It can change how the chain folds (tertiary structure), so the shape of the protein, and its ability to bind its receptor, changes

A different R group can change the bonds that hold the folded shape together. The 3D shape changes, and insulin may no longer bind to its receptor.

A small blood sample from a crime scene is used to make a DNA profile. The DNA is first amplified by PCR, and then run on a gel alongside samples from three suspects and a DNA ladder. Crime scene: bands at 200 bp and 450 bp. Suspect 1: 200 bp and 300 bp. Suspect 2: 200 bp and 450 bp. Suspect 3: 150 bp and 450 bp.

Outline the three steps of one PCR cycle, including approximate temperatures.

  • Denaturation ≈ 95 °C: hydrogen bonds break and the strands separate
  • Annealing ≈ 55 °C: primers bind to complementary sequences either side of the target
  • Extension ≈ 72 °C: Taq polymerase adds nucleotides from the primers, making new strands

Denaturation (about 95 °C): the strands separate. Annealing (about 55 °C): primers bind to each strand. Extension (about 72 °C): Taq polymerase builds new complementary strands from the primers.

Explain why Taq polymerase is used rather than human DNA polymerase.

  • It is heat-stable: it is not denatured at 95 °C (it comes from a thermophilic bacterium)

It comes from a bacterium that lives in hot springs, so it isn’t denatured during the 95 °C steps.

State the role of primers.

  • Short single-stranded DNA sequences that bind either side of the target region, giving a starting point for DNA polymerase

Primers bind to the ends of the target sequence and give the polymerase a starting point, so only the target region is copied.

Explain how gel electrophoresis separates the DNA fragments.

  • DNA is negatively charged (phosphate groups), so it moves towards the positive electrode
  • Smaller fragments move more easily through the gel matrix, so they travel further

The negatively charged DNA moves towards the positive electrode. The gel acts as a sieve: small fragments pass through faster and travel further than large ones.

Which suspect’s profile matches the crime-scene sample?

  • Suspect 2

Suspect 2: both bands (200 bp and 450 bp) match.

State the purpose of the DNA ladder.

  • It contains fragments of known sizes, used to estimate the sizes of the sample fragments

It contains fragments of known lengths, so the sizes of the sample bands can be estimated by comparison.

Human insulin is produced by inserting the human insulin gene into bacterial plasmids. The plasmid carries a gene for resistance to the antibiotic ampicillin.

Explain why the same restriction enzyme is used to cut both the plasmid and the DNA containing the insulin gene.

  • The enzyme cuts at a specific recognition sequence
  • It produces complementary sticky ends on both, so the insulin gene can base-pair with the cut plasmid

A restriction enzyme cuts at one specific sequence, so using the same enzyme leaves complementary sticky ends on the plasmid and the gene. These pair up, and the gene slots into the plasmid.

State the role of DNA ligase in this process.

  • It joins the sugar–phosphate backbones of the gene and plasmid (forms phosphodiester bonds)

Ligase seals the sugar–phosphate backbone, joining the gene permanently into the plasmid.

Explain how the ampicillin resistance gene is used to identify bacteria that have taken up a plasmid.

  • The bacteria are grown on a medium containing ampicillin
  • Only bacteria that took up the plasmid (with its resistance gene) survive and form colonies

The bacteria are spread on agar containing ampicillin. Only those carrying the plasmid can survive and grow, so every colony contains the plasmid.

Name one method used to get plasmids into bacterial cells.

  • Heat shock (with CaCl₂) or electroporation

Heat shock or electroporation.

CRISPR-Cas9 can also be used to modify organisms. State the role of the guide RNA.

  • It is complementary to the target DNA sequence and directs Cas9 to cut at that site

The guide RNA base-pairs with the target DNA sequence and takes Cas9 to exactly that site.

Give one social or ethical concern about genetically modified crops.

  • Any valid concern, e.g. gene transfer to wild relatives, reduced biodiversity, corporate control of seed, labelling/consumer choice

For example, the modified genes could spread to wild relatives, or a few companies could control the seed supply.

Photosynthesis occurs in chloroplasts. A student measures the rate of photosynthesis of pondweed at different light intensities at 20 °C. The rate increases as light intensity increases, then levels off.

Name the location of the light-dependent stage and the light-independent stage within the chloroplast.

  • Light-dependent: thylakoid membranes (grana)
  • Light-independent: stroma

Light-dependent stage: thylakoid membranes. Light-independent stage (Calvin cycle): stroma.

State the inputs and outputs of the light-dependent stage.

  • Inputs: H₂O, light energy, NADP⁺, ADP + Pi
  • Outputs: O₂, NADPH, ATP

Inputs: water, light, NADP⁺, ADP and Pi. Outputs: oxygen, NADPH and ATP.

Explain why the rate levels off at high light intensity.

  • Light is no longer the limiting factor
  • Another factor, e.g. CO₂ concentration or temperature, is now limiting the rate

Light is no longer limiting. Another factor, such as CO₂ concentration or temperature, now limits the rate of the Calvin cycle.

The student repeats the experiment at 50 °C and finds a much lower rate. Explain.

  • Enzymes of photosynthesis (e.g. rubisco) are denatured at high temperature
  • Their active sites change shape, so the reactions are catalysed more slowly (or not at all)

At 50 °C, enzymes such as rubisco begin to denature. Their active sites change shape, so fewer enzyme–substrate complexes form and the rate falls.

State the role of rubisco.

  • It catalyses the fixation of CO₂ (joining CO₂ to RuBP) in the Calvin cycle

Rubisco catalyses the first step of the Calvin cycle, fixing CO₂.

Aerobic cellular respiration releases energy from glucose in three stages.

For each stage (glycolysis, the Krebs cycle and the electron transport chain), state its location in the cell and the ATP yield per glucose.

  • Glycolysis: cytosol, 2 ATP (net)
  • Krebs cycle: mitochondrial matrix, 2 ATP
  • Electron transport chain: inner mitochondrial membrane (cristae), about 26 or 28 ATP

Glycolysis: cytosol, 2 ATP. Krebs cycle: mitochondrial matrix, 2 ATP. ETC: inner membrane (cristae), about 26–28 ATP.

State the role of oxygen in aerobic respiration.

  • Final electron acceptor in the ETC (combines with electrons and H⁺ to form water)

Oxygen is the final electron acceptor at the end of the ETC, forming water.

Name the products of anaerobic fermentation in human muscle.

  • Lactate (lactic acid) and 2 ATP

Lactate and 2 ATP (from glycolysis).

Explain why anaerobic fermentation produces much less ATP than aerobic respiration.

  • Only glycolysis produces ATP; the Krebs cycle and ETC do not operate without oxygen
  • Glucose is only partly broken down: most of its energy remains in lactate or ethanol

Without oxygen, the ETC and Krebs cycle stop, so glycolysis is the only source of ATP. Most of glucose’s energy stays locked in lactate or ethanol.

Give one use of yeast fermentation in biotechnology.

  • e.g. making bioethanol fuel, brewing, baking (CO₂ raises bread)

Producing bioethanol from plant biomass (also brewing and baking).

A person cuts their finger on a rusty nail, and bacteria enter the wound.

Describe two barriers that normally prevent bacteria from entering the body.

  • One physical barrier, e.g. intact skin, mucous membranes, cilia
  • One chemical or microbiological barrier, e.g. lysozyme in tears/sweat, stomach acid, normal flora competing

Intact skin is a physical barrier. Lysozyme in sweat and tears breaks down bacterial cell walls (chemical). Other acceptable answers include mucus, stomach acid and normal flora.

Describe the inflammatory response at the wound.

  • Mast cells release histamine
  • Vasodilation and increased capillary permeability: more blood flow, redness, swelling (fluid and complement enter the tissue)
  • Phagocytes (neutrophils, macrophages) are attracted to the site and engulf the bacteria

Damaged tissue and mast cells release histamine. Nearby blood vessels dilate and become leakier, so more blood, fluid and complement reach the site (heat, redness, swelling). Chemical signals attract neutrophils and macrophages, which engulf the bacteria.

Explain how dendritic cells link the innate and adaptive immune responses.

  • They engulf pathogens and present antigen fragments on MHC class II
  • They migrate to a lymph node and present the antigen to helper T cells, activating the adaptive response

Dendritic cells engulf the bacteria and display antigen fragments on MHC II. They travel to a lymph node, where they present the antigen to the specific helper T cells, starting the adaptive response.

State one role of complement proteins.

  • e.g. forming membrane attack complexes that lyse bacteria, opsonisation, or attracting phagocytes

Complement proteins can form pores that burst bacterial cells. They also coat bacteria for phagocytosis and attract phagocytes.

State the role of helper T cells.

  • They release cytokines that activate specific B cells and cytotoxic T cells

Activated helper T cells release cytokines, which stimulate the clonal expansion of the specific B cells and cytotoxic T cells.

A graph of antibody levels shows the responses to a first injection of a vaccine and a later exposure to the real pathogen.

Explain how a vaccine provides long-term protection.

  • The vaccine contains antigens (or instructions to make them) that trigger a primary immune response without causing disease
  • Memory B and T cells specific to the antigen are produced and persist for a long time

The vaccine exposes the immune system to the pathogen’s antigens without causing the disease. The primary response produces long-lived memory B and T cells.

Compare the secondary response with the primary response.

  • The secondary response is faster (shorter lag)
  • It produces a much higher concentration of antibodies that lasts longer

The secondary response starts much sooner and produces far more antibody, for longer, because memory cells respond immediately.

Explain how herd immunity protects people who cannot be vaccinated.

  • When a high proportion of a population is immune, the pathogen cannot spread easily (few susceptible hosts)
  • So transmission chains are broken and unvaccinated people are unlikely to be exposed

If most people are immune, an infected person is unlikely to meet a susceptible one. The chains of transmission break, so vulnerable people (babies, the immunocompromised) are rarely exposed.

Explain why a new influenza vaccine is needed each year.

  • Antigenic drift: mutations change the surface antigens of the virus, so existing memory cells no longer recognise it

Influenza surface antigens change through antigenic drift, so last year’s memory cells may not recognise this year’s strains.

Some populations of the bacterium Staphylococcus aureus are now resistant to many antibiotics.

Explain how antibiotic resistance becomes common in a bacterial population.

  • Variation exists: some bacteria carry a resistance allele (from a random mutation or gene transfer)
  • The antibiotic is a selection pressure: susceptible bacteria die, resistant ones survive and reproduce
  • The resistant bacteria pass the allele on, so its frequency increases over generations

By chance, a mutation (or gene transfer) gives some bacteria a resistance allele. When the antibiotic is used, susceptible bacteria die, while resistant ones survive and reproduce. Over generations the frequency of the resistance allele increases.

The antibiotic does not cause the mutation, which already existed. Answers that said the bacteria “became resistant” or “adapted” scored poorly.

Distinguish between genetic drift and gene flow.

  • Genetic drift: random change in allele frequencies due to chance, greatest in small populations
  • Gene flow: movement of alleles between populations by migration (interbreeding)

Genetic drift is a random change in allele frequencies from one generation to the next, most significant in small populations. Gene flow is the movement of alleles between populations when individuals migrate and interbreed.

Explain how comparing DNA sequences can show how closely related two species are.

  • DNA accumulates mutations over time, so sequences diverge after species split
  • Fewer differences mean a more recent common ancestor (closer relationship)

Once two species split, mutations accumulate independently in each lineage. The fewer differences between their DNA sequences, the more recently they shared a common ancestor.

Assessor’s report

General comments

This paper covered all areas of study at a gentler level than the VCAA exam. Most questions tested recall and standard descriptions: the steps of PCR, the secretory pathway, inflammation, the stages of respiration. Students who knew the sequence of each process and used the correct terms scored well.

Where marks were lost

  • Missing a step in a sequence. Question 3b needed three stages: rough ER, Golgi, exocytosis. Leaving out the vesicles or the Golgi lost a mark.
  • Vague terms. “The enzyme dies” or “the shape changes” is not enough. Say “the active site changes shape, so the substrate can no longer bind”.
  • Directionality in DNA. Label 5′ and 3′ ends, and never put T in RNA.
  • Natural selection. Resistance alleles exist before the antibiotic is used. The antibiotic selects them. It doesn’t create them.
  • Comparisons. “Compare” (Question 9b) needs both responses mentioned in each point: “faster than…”, “higher than…”.

Advice

If you scored 85% or more, move on to the medium paper. If not, revisit the topic pages linked in your weakest topics, then resit this paper under timed conditions.
