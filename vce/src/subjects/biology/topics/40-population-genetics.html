Gene pools and allele frequencies

A gene pool is all the alleles of all genes in a population. Evolution is a change in allele frequencies in a population over generations. Individuals don't evolve; populations do.

In a population of 100 plants, 36 are AA, 48 are Aa and 16 are aa. Find the frequency of allele A.

An allele frequency is a proportion of all copies of that gene in the population. Each individual carries two copies, so there are \(2N\) alleles in total: homozygotes contribute two copies of their allele and heterozygotes contribute one of each.

Total alleles \(= 2 \times 100 = 200\). A alleles \(= 2(36) + 48 = 120\).

\(p(A) = \frac{120}{200} = 0.6\), so \(q(a) = 0.4\).

Mutations: the source of new alleles

MutationWhat happensTypical effect
Substitution (point)One base replacedSilent (same amino acid), missense (different amino acid) or nonsense (premature stop codon)
Insertion or deletion (point)A base added or removedFrameshift: every codon after it changes, usually producing a non-functional protein (unless the change is in multiples of 3)
Block (chromosomal) mutationsDuplication, deletion, inversion or translocation of a large segmentMany genes affected; can change gene dosage or break genes

Mutations are random: they don't happen because an organism “needs” them. Only mutations in gametes (or cells that give rise to them) are inherited.

Four ways allele frequencies change

MechanismWhat happensEffect on diversity
MutationNew alleles ariseIncreases
Gene flowAlleles move between populations via migration or interbreedingIncreases in the receiving population; makes populations more similar
Genetic driftRandom changes in allele frequencies, strongest in small populationsUsually decreases (alleles can be lost or fixed by chance)
Natural selectionIndividuals with advantageous phenotypes survive and reproduce more, passing on their allelesCan decrease (for the selected trait)
  • Bottleneck effect: a sudden large reduction in population size (disaster, hunting) leaves a small random sample of alleles. Example: northern elephant seals were hunted to perhaps 20–100 individuals in the 1890s; today's large population has very low genetic diversity.
  • Founder effect: a small group starts a new, isolated population, carrying only some of the original alleles, possibly at unusual frequencies (e.g. high rates of certain inherited conditions in isolated island communities).

Natural selection

  1. Variation: individuals differ, partly because of different alleles.
  2. Selection pressure: an environmental factor (predator, disease, climate, antibiotic) affects survival.
  3. Differential survival and reproduction: individuals with advantageous traits leave more offspring.
  4. Inheritance: the advantageous alleles are passed on and increase in frequency over generations.

Explain how a population of Staphylococcus aureus in a hospital can become resistant to an antibiotic.

Natural selection needs existing variation (from random mutation or gene transfer), a selection pressure, and differential survival and reproduction. Over generations, the frequency of the favourable allele increases in the gene pool.

By chance, a few bacteria already carry a mutation (or a plasmid gene) giving resistance. The antibiotic didn't cause it.

When the antibiotic is used (selection pressure), susceptible bacteria die, while resistant ones survive.

Survivors reproduce rapidly (binary fission), passing on the resistance allele; resistance genes can also spread between bacteria on plasmids.

Over many generations, the frequency of the resistance allele increases until most of the population is resistant (e.g. MRSA). Overuse and not finishing courses increase the selection pressure.

Selective breeding

Humans act as the selection pressure, choosing individuals with desired traits to breed (e.g. high-yield wheat, dog breeds, merino wool). It changes allele frequencies quickly but reduces genetic diversity, which can increase inherited disorders and vulnerability to new diseases or environmental change.

Viruses: antigenic drift and shift

Small mutations in viral surface-protein genes (drift) accumulate, allowing viruses to escape existing immunity: a form of evolution by natural selection, with host immunity as the selection pressure. Shift (reassortment between strains) produces sudden major changes. See disease challenges.

  • Individuals don't evolve; populations do (allele frequencies change across generations).
  • Mutations are random; the environment selects, it doesn't cause the useful mutation.
  • Drift is random, selection isn't. Drift can even remove advantageous alleles in small populations.
  • “Survival of the fittest” means reproductive success, not strength.
  • Antibiotic resistance evolves in bacteria, not in the patient.

  2. gene flow

  1. make their gene pools more similar
  2. make them more different
  3. always cause speciation
  4. reduce diversity in both

Exchanging alleles homogenises the populations, which works against speciation.

Distinguish between the founder effect and the bottleneck effect.

Both are forms of genetic drift that reduce diversity. Founder effect: a small group leaves to start a new isolated population, carrying a non-representative sample of alleles. Bottleneck: an existing population is drastically reduced in size (e.g. by a disaster), and the survivors' alleles are a random, non-representative sample.

Explain how selective breeding of a crop for high yield could make it vulnerable to a new fungal disease.

Breeders repeatedly select plants with the high-yield trait, so only a few parent lines contribute alleles. [1] Genetic diversity falls: many alleles, including rare ones that might give resistance, are lost. [1] If a new fungus appears, most plants are genetically similar and equally susceptible. [1] So the whole crop could be devastated (as in the Irish potato famine), because there's little variation for selection to act on. [1]

A substitution mutation changes the codon UAC (tyrosine) to UAG. (a) Name this type of point mutation. (b) Predict its effect on the protein. (c) Would it affect the next generation? Explain.

(a) Nonsense mutation. [1] (b) UAG is a stop codon: translation ends early, giving a shortened, probably non-functional polypeptide. [1] (c) Only if it occurred in a gamete-producing (germline) cell; a mutation in a body (somatic) cell isn't inherited. [1]

A student writes: “The bacteria became resistant because they were exposed to the antibiotic.” Rewrite this to be scientifically accurate.

Some bacteria already carried resistance alleles due to random mutation. The antibiotic acted as a selection pressure, killing susceptible bacteria, so the resistant ones survived, reproduced and increased in frequency over generations.

Summary

  • Evolution = change in allele frequencies over generations.
  • Mutation and gene flow add alleles; drift (random, small populations) and selection change frequencies.
  • Bottleneck and founder effects reduce diversity.
  • Selective breeding and antibiotic use are human-driven selection pressures.
