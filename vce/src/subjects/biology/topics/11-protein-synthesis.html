Overview

StageWhere (eukaryote)Input → output
TranscriptionNucleusDNA template → pre-mRNA
RNA processingNucleuspre-mRNA → mature mRNA
TranslationRibosomes in the cytoplasm (free or on rough ER)mRNA → polypeptide

In prokaryotes there's no nucleus: transcription and translation both happen in the cytoplasm, often at the same time, and there's no splicing.

1. Transcription

  1. RNA polymerase binds to the promoter, and the DNA unwinds and separates locally (hydrogen bonds break).
  2. RNA polymerase moves along the template strand (3′→5′), adding complementary RNA nucleotides to build pre-mRNA 5′→3′ (A–U, T–A, G–C, C–G).
  3. At the termination sequence, RNA polymerase detaches and the pre-mRNA is released; the DNA re-forms its double helix.

2. RNA processing (eukaryotes)

  • Splicing: introns are removed and exons joined (by spliceosomes).
  • 5′ methyl-guanosine cap added: helps the ribosome bind and protects the mRNA from degradation.
  • Poly-A tail (many adenine nucleotides) added to the 3′ end: increases stability and aids export through nuclear pores.
  • Alternative splicing: different combinations of exons can be kept, so one gene can code for several different proteins. This is one reason humans have about 20 000 genes but many more proteins.

3. Translation

  1. Initiation: the small ribosomal subunit binds the mRNA (near the cap) and moves to the start codon AUG. A tRNA with anticodon UAC carrying methionine pairs with it; the large subunit joins.
  2. Elongation: the next tRNA with a complementary anticodon brings its amino acid. A peptide bond forms between the amino acids (a condensation reaction catalysed by rRNA). The ribosome moves along one codon, and the empty tRNA leaves to be recharged.
  3. Termination: at a stop codon, a release factor binds, and the polypeptide and ribosome separate.

Several ribosomes can translate one mRNA at the same time (a polyribosome), producing many copies of the polypeptide quickly.

A section of mRNA reads 5′ AUG UUU GGC 3′. (a) Give the anticodons of the tRNAs that bind to it, in order. (b) How many peptide bonds form between these amino acids?

Anticodons are complementary to the codons: AUG → UAC; UUU → AAA; GGC → CCG.

Three amino acids joined in a chain: 2 peptide bonds.

(a) UAC, AAA, CCG. (b) 2. (Anticodons are usually written 3′→5′ to line up with the codon: 3′ UAC 5′ pairs with 5′ AUG 3′.)

A gene has exons 1, 2, 3 and 4 separated by introns. In muscle cells the protein made from it contains exons 1-2-4; in liver cells, 1-3-4. Explain how this is possible and state one advantage.

The whole gene, including all exons and introns, is transcribed into pre-mRNA in both cell types.

During RNA processing, different exons are spliced out along with the introns: exon 3 is removed in muscle and exon 2 in liver.

Alternative splicing produces different mature mRNAs, and so different polypeptides, from one gene. Advantage: a single gene can code for multiple proteins, increasing the proteome without extra DNA.

  • RNA polymerase doesn't need a primer and reads the template strand, not the coding strand.
  • Introns are transcribed (they're in pre-mRNA). They're removed afterwards.
  • The ribosome reads codons; tRNA anticodons pair with them. tRNA doesn't read DNA.
  • Translation ends at the stop codon, not at the end of the mRNA (there's an untranslated region and the poly-A tail after it).
  • Amino acids are joined by peptide bonds; codon–anticodon pairing uses hydrogen bonds.

Practice

In eukaryotes, transcription occurs in the

  1. cytoplasm
  2. ribosome
  3. nucleus
  4. Golgi apparatus

DNA stays in the nucleus; pre-mRNA is made there.

The enzyme that builds mRNA from a DNA template is

  1. RNA polymerase
  2. DNA polymerase
  3. DNA ligase
  4. reverse transcriptase

RNA polymerase transcribes. DNA polymerase replicates DNA; reverse transcriptase makes DNA from RNA.

Which is not a function of the 5′ cap and poly-A tail?

  1. Protecting mRNA from degradation
  2. Helping export from the nucleus
  3. Helping the ribosome bind
  4. Coding for the first and last amino acids

They aren't translated. The start codon codes for the first amino acid.

A tRNA molecule has the anticodon 3′ GCU 5′. The codon it pairs with, and the amino acid it carries, are coded by

  1. 3′ CGA 5′ on mRNA
  2. 5′ CGA 3′ on mRNA
  3. 5′ GCU 3′ on DNA
  4. 5′ CGT 3′ on mRNA

Complementary and antiparallel: 3′ GCU 5′ pairs with 5′ CGA 3′ (arginine). mRNA has no T.

A mutation deletes the splice site at the boundary of intron 2 in a gene. The most likely effect is

  1. no transcription of the gene
  2. the promoter can't bind RNA polymerase
  3. intron 2 is retained in the mRNA, changing the polypeptide or introducing an early stop codon
  4. the poly-A tail isn't added

Without the splice signal, the intron isn't removed; its sequence is then translated (often producing a premature stop codon or a frameshift).

Which statement comparing prokaryotic and eukaryotic gene expression is correct?

  1. In prokaryotes, translation can begin before transcription is finished
  2. Prokaryotic mRNA is spliced in the nucleus
  3. Eukaryotic ribosomes are found only in the nucleus
  4. Prokaryotes don't use RNA polymerase

With no nuclear membrane, ribosomes attach to mRNA while it's still being transcribed.

Describe the role of each of mRNA, tRNA and rRNA in translation.

mRNA carries the codons that specify the amino acid sequence. tRNA delivers the correct amino acid to the ribosome, matching its anticodon to the codon. rRNA forms part of the ribosome and catalyses peptide bond formation.

A gene is 12 000 base pairs long from promoter to termination sequence, yet its mature mRNA is only 2100 nucleotides long. Give two reasons for the difference.

Introns (often most of a eukaryotic gene) are transcribed but spliced out. [1] The promoter (and other regulatory sequences) isn't transcribed at all. [1] (Also: the transcribed length depends on where termination occurs.) Note that the cap and poly-A tail are added, so they don't explain a shorter mRNA. [1]

Outline the steps from a gene in the nucleus to a polypeptide in the cytoplasm, naming the key molecules at each step.

Transcription: RNA polymerase binds the promoter and uses the template strand to make complementary pre-mRNA. [1] Processing: introns spliced out, exons joined, 5′ cap and poly-A tail added; mature mRNA exits through a nuclear pore. [1] Translation: a ribosome binds the mRNA at AUG; tRNAs with complementary anticodons bring amino acids. [1] Peptide bonds form between adjacent amino acids until a stop codon is reached and the polypeptide is released. [1]

A student says: “Every cell in the body makes every protein, because every cell has the same DNA.” Explain why this is incorrect.

Cells contain the same genes, but only some genes are expressed in each cell type: transcription (and processing and translation) is regulated. Different cells therefore make different sets of proteins (e.g. insulin only in pancreatic β cells).

Summary

  • Transcription (nucleus): RNA polymerase, promoter, template strand → pre-mRNA.
  • Processing: splice out introns, add cap and tail; alternative splicing gives multiple proteins.
  • Translation (ribosome): codon–anticodon pairing, peptide bonds, AUG to stop codon.
