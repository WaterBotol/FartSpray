Features of innate immunity

The innate response is rapid (minutes to hours), non-specific (the same response to any pathogen) and has no memory (it's no faster the second time). It's present from birth.

ComponentRole
NeutrophilsThe most common phagocytes; first to arrive at infection sites; short-lived (they die after engulfing, forming pus)
MacrophagesLong-lived phagocytes in tissues; engulf pathogens and debris; present antigen on MHC II; release cytokines
Dendritic cellsPhagocytes in skin and linings; engulf antigens and migrate to lymph nodes to present them to T helper cells. They're the key link to adaptive immunity.
Mast cellsIn connective tissue; release histamine (and other chemicals) when activated, triggering inflammation
Natural killer (NK) cellsLymphocytes that kill virus-infected and cancerous cells, especially those with reduced MHC I, by inducing apoptosis
EosinophilsRelease toxic granules against large parasites (e.g. worms) too big to engulf; also involved in allergies
Complement proteinsBlood proteins activated in a cascade: they coat pathogens (opsonisation), attract phagocytes (chemotaxis), and form membrane attack complexes that punch holes in bacterial membranes (lysis)
InterferonsProteins released by virus-infected cells that warn neighbouring cells to resist viral replication, and activate NK cells

Phagocytosis

  1. The phagocyte recognises the pathogen (e.g. via complement or antibody coating, or pathogen-specific patterns).
  2. It engulfs the pathogen into a vesicle (phagosome).
  3. A lysosome fuses with it, and digestive enzymes destroy the pathogen.
  4. Macrophages and dendritic cells then display antigen fragments on MHC II (antigen presentation).

Inflammation

  1. Tissue damage or infection activates mast cells, which release histamine.
  2. Histamine causes vasodilation (more blood flow: redness and heat) and increased permeability of capillaries (fluid leaks out: swelling; pressure on nerves: pain).
  3. Complement proteins and cytokines attract phagocytes (neutrophils, then macrophages) into the tissue.
  4. Phagocytes engulf pathogens and debris; clotting proteins wall off the area; tissue repair follows.

Fever: cytokines reset the hypothalamus to a higher body temperature. A moderate fever slows pathogen replication and speeds up immune reactions; a very high fever is dangerous because it can denature the body's own proteins.

A splinter causes a red, hot, swollen, painful area. Explain each sign in terms of the inflammatory response.

Mast cells in the damaged tissue release histamine.

Redness and heat: histamine dilates blood vessels, increasing blood flow to the area.

Swelling: histamine makes capillaries more permeable, so fluid (and phagocytes) move into the tissue.

Pain: swelling presses on nerve endings (and chemicals released stimulate them). The increased permeability also lets phagocytes and complement reach the pathogens.

  • Innate = non-specific, no memory. Don't say macrophages “remember” a pathogen.
  • Histamine is released by mast cells, not by phagocytes or the pathogen.
  • NK cells are lymphocytes but part of innate immunity: they don't need antigen-specific receptors.
  • Interferons don't kill viruses directly; they signal neighbouring cells to resist infection.

Describe the process of phagocytosis by a macrophage.

The macrophage recognises and binds the pathogen, then engulfs it into a vesicle (phagosome). [1] Lysosomes fuse with the vesicle and their enzymes digest the pathogen. [1] Fragments of the pathogen's antigens are displayed on MHC II on the macrophage's surface (antigen presentation). [1]

Antihistamines reduce the symptoms of hay fever. Explain how, and why they're only a partial solution.

In hay fever, pollen (an allergen) activates mast cells to release histamine, causing vasodilation and permeability (runny nose, itching, swelling). [1] Antihistamines block histamine receptors, so these effects are reduced. [1] They don't stop the underlying immune sensitivity (IgE-coated mast cells), so symptoms return each time pollen is encountered. [1]

Compare the innate and adaptive immune responses in terms of speed, specificity, memory and the cells involved.

Speed: innate is rapid (minutes to hours); adaptive is slower on first exposure (days). [1] Specificity: innate is non-specific; adaptive targets a specific antigen. [1] Memory: innate has none; adaptive produces memory cells for a faster, stronger secondary response. [1] Cells: innate uses phagocytes, mast cells, NK cells and eosinophils; adaptive uses B and T lymphocytes. [1]

A student writes: “After the first infection, neutrophils remember the bacteria and kill them faster next time.” Correct this.

Neutrophils are part of innate immunity, which is non-specific and has no memory. The faster second response comes from memory B and T cells of the adaptive response.

Summary

  • Innate: fast, non-specific, no memory.
  • Phagocytes engulf; mast cells release histamine; NK cells kill infected cells; eosinophils attack parasites.
  • Complement: opsonise, attract, lyse. Interferons: warn neighbouring cells.
  • Inflammation: vasodilation + permeability → redness, heat, swelling, pain; then phagocytes arrive.
