Making a recombinant plasmid

  1. Obtain the gene. For a human protein made in bacteria, use cDNA: mRNA is extracted from cells that express the gene and copied into DNA by reverse transcriptase. The cDNA has no introns, which bacteria can't remove.
  2. Cut the gene and the plasmid with the same restriction enzyme, giving complementary sticky ends.
  3. Mix and join: sticky ends base-pair, and DNA ligase seals the backbone to form a recombinant plasmid.
  4. Transform bacteria: plasmids are taken up after heat shock (with calcium chloride) or electroporation. Only a small fraction of cells take up a plasmid.
  5. Select transformed bacteria: grow them on media with an antibiotic. Only bacteria with the plasmid (carrying an antibiotic-resistance gene) survive. A reporter gene (e.g. GFP fluorescence, or disrupting lacZ: white vs blue colonies) shows which plasmids contain the inserted gene.
  6. Culture the bacteria in large fermenters, then extract and purify the protein.

Insulin was first mass-produced in E. coli by inserting genes for the A and B chains into separate plasmids; the purified chains were then joined chemically. Later processes express proinsulin and cut it into insulin. Compared with insulin from pigs and cows, recombinant human insulin is identical to the human hormone (fewer immune reactions), available in unlimited amounts, and free of animal diseases.

A plasmid carries an ampicillin-resistance gene and a lacZ gene (which turns colonies blue on X-gal). The insulin gene is inserted into the middle of lacZ. After transformation, bacteria are plated on ampicillin + X-gal. Which colonies contain the recombinant plasmid?

Bacteria without any plasmid die on ampicillin.

Bacteria with a plasmid that closed without the insert have a working lacZ: blue colonies.

White colonies: they survived (so they have a plasmid), but lacZ is disrupted (so the gene was inserted).

GMOs and transgenic organisms

  • A genetically modified organism (GMO) has had its genome altered by genetic engineering (including gene editing or silencing).
  • A transgenic organism is a GMO containing a gene from a different species.

  • cDNA lacks introns because it's copied from mature mRNA, not because introns were cut out of the DNA.
  • The same restriction enzyme must cut both the gene and the plasmid (for matching sticky ends).
  • Antibiotic resistance selects for any plasmid, not specifically the recombinant one. You need a second marker.
  • Not every GMO is transgenic: a CRISPR knockout adds no foreign DNA.

Why is cDNA, rather than the gene from a human chromosome, used to make human insulin in bacteria?

  1. cDNA is shorter because it has no promoter
  2. bacteria can't read human codons
  3. cDNA has no introns, and bacteria can't splice pre-mRNA
  4. cDNA is made by ligase

Bacteria lack spliceosomes. cDNA made from mature mRNA has only exons.

Ligase forms the phosphodiester bonds.

A plasmid has one EcoRI site within its ampicillin-resistance gene. A gene is inserted at this site. Transformed bacteria carrying the recombinant plasmid will

  1. grow on ampicillin
  2. glow green
  3. be unable to grow on ampicillin
  4. be resistant to all antibiotics

The insert disrupts the resistance gene (insertional inactivation), so recombinant bacteria are ampicillin-sensitive. (Another marker, e.g. tetracycline resistance, would be used to select for any plasmid.)

Outline the steps to produce a recombinant plasmid containing a gene of interest.

Cut the plasmid and the gene with the same restriction enzyme to produce complementary sticky ends. Mix them so the sticky ends base pair. Add DNA ligase to seal the sugar–phosphate backbone, forming the recombinant plasmid.

Bt cotton reduces insecticide use. Explain how growing it widely could eventually make it less effective, and suggest a management strategy.

Insects vary; any individuals with resistance to the Bt toxin survive and reproduce. [1] With strong, constant selection pressure, resistance alleles increase in the pest population until the toxin no longer controls it. [1] Strategy: plant “refuges” of non-Bt crops nearby so susceptible insects survive and mate with resistant ones, slowing resistance; or use crops with two different Bt genes. [1]

Evaluate the use of golden rice to address vitamin A deficiency.

Benefits: provides β-carotene (converted to vitamin A) in a staple food eaten by populations at risk; could reduce blindness and deaths in children. [1] Grown locally, and a humanitarian licence can make it affordable for poorer farmers. [1]

Concerns: possible gene flow to other rice varieties; public acceptance of GMOs; dietary diversity or supplementation may be alternatives; β-carotene content and absorption depend on diet (fat intake). [1] Conclusion: a reasonable judgement balancing these, e.g. a useful tool alongside other strategies. [1]

A student says CRISPR-edited crops are always transgenic. Is this correct?

No. If CRISPR is used only to cut and knock out (or slightly alter) the plant's own gene, and no DNA from another species remains, the plant is genetically modified but not transgenic.

Summary

  • Same restriction enzyme → matching sticky ends → ligase → recombinant plasmid → transformation → selection → culture.
  • cDNA (via reverse transcriptase) avoids introns.
  • GMO: genome engineered; transgenic: contains another species' gene.
  • Weigh yield, pesticide and nutrition benefits against gene flow, resistance, diversity and social issues.
