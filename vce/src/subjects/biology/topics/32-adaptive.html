The lymphatic system and antigen presentation

Lymph vessels collect fluid (lymph) from tissues and carry it through lymph nodes, which are packed with B and T lymphocytes. Dendritic cells (and macrophages) that engulfed a pathogen travel in lymph to a node and present its antigen on MHC class II. There, only the rare T helper cells with a complementary receptor are activated. This is why lymph nodes swell during an infection: the activated lymphocytes are multiplying.

Clonal selection

Each lymphocyte has receptors for one specific antigen, determined before it ever meets that antigen. When an antigen binds, only the matching lymphocytes are selected and multiply into a clone (clonal expansion), then differentiate into effector cells and memory cells.

Humoral response (B cells and antibodies)

  1. A naïve B cell's receptor binds its specific antigen (free in body fluids).
  2. An activated T helper cell recognising the same antigen releases cytokines that activate the B cell.
  3. The B cell undergoes clonal expansion and differentiates into plasma cells, which secrete large amounts of antibody, and memory B cells, which persist for years.

Antibodies don't kill pathogens themselves; they tag, clump and neutralise, so other parts of the immune system can destroy them. They work only on antigens outside cells (in blood, lymph, tissue fluid).

Cell-mediated response (T cells)

Pathogens hiding inside cells (viruses, some bacteria) and cancer cells are dealt with by T cells:

  1. Infected cells display fragments of the pathogen's antigen on MHC class I.
  2. T helper cells activated by antigen presentation release cytokines, stimulating clonal expansion of cytotoxic T cells with receptors for that antigen.
  3. Cytotoxic T cells bind infected cells displaying the antigen on MHC I and release chemicals (e.g. perforin) that cause apoptosis (programmed cell death).
  4. Memory T cells remain for a faster response next time.

Primary vs secondary response

Explain why both humoral and cell-mediated responses are needed to clear a viral infection.

Antibodies act only outside cells: they neutralise virus particles in body fluids, stopping them infecting new cells.

Viruses replicate inside host cells, where antibodies can't reach.

Cytotoxic T cells destroy the infected cells (identified by viral antigen on MHC I), removing the virus factories. The two responses together stop spread and eliminate the source.

  • Plasma cells secrete antibodies; B cells themselves display them as receptors.
  • Cytotoxic T cells recognise MHC I; T helper cells recognise MHC II.
  • Specificity exists before exposure. The antigen selects existing matching cells; it doesn't “teach” cells to make a new receptor.
  • Memory cells don't secrete antibodies; on re-exposure they quickly produce plasma cells.
  • T helper cells don't kill anything; they coordinate via cytokines. (HIV destroys T helper cells, crippling both responses.)

Describe the role of T helper cells in the adaptive immune response.

They're activated when their receptor binds a specific antigen presented on MHC II by a dendritic cell (in a lymph node). [1] They release cytokines that stimulate clonal expansion and differentiation of B cells (into plasma and memory cells) [1] and of cytotoxic T cells. [1]

Explain the process of clonal selection and expansion in the humoral response, from antigen entry to antibody production.

Among millions of B cells, each with a different receptor, only those with receptors complementary to the antigen bind it (selection). [1] A T helper cell specific to the same antigen releases cytokines that activate the B cell. [1] The activated B cell divides repeatedly by mitosis, producing a clone of identical cells (expansion). [1] Most differentiate into plasma cells that secrete that specific antibody; some become long-lived memory B cells. [1]

A person is exposed to measles at age 5 and again at age 15. Sketch and describe the antibody response each time, and explain why they don't get sick the second time.

First exposure: lag of about 1–2 weeks, then a modest rise in antibodies that falls away. [1] Second exposure: very short lag, much higher and longer-lasting antibody level. [1] Memory B (and T) cells rapidly produce plasma cells, so antibodies destroy the virus before it multiplies enough to cause symptoms. [1]

A student says: “Antibodies kill bacteria.” Improve this statement.

Antibodies bind specific antigens on bacteria, marking them for destruction: they opsonise them (enhancing phagocytosis), agglutinate them, neutralise toxins and activate complement, which can lyse them. The killing is done by phagocytes and complement.

Summary

  • Dendritic cells present antigen (MHC II) to T helper cells in lymph nodes.
  • Humoral: B cell → (T helper cytokines) → plasma cells (antibodies) + memory B cells.
  • Cell-mediated: cytotoxic T cells kill cells showing antigen on MHC I; memory T cells.
  • Antibodies neutralise, agglutinate, opsonise and activate complement.
  • Secondary response: faster, larger, longer (memory cells).
