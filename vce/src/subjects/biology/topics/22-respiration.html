ATP totals vary between textbooks (older sources give 36–38, newer ones about 30–32). Use the figure your course uses and be consistent.

The Krebs cycle doesn't use O₂, but it needs a supply of NAD⁺ and FAD. These are regenerated only when the ETC unloads NADH and FADH₂, and the ETC runs only if O₂ is there to accept electrons. No O₂ → ETC stops → NAD⁺ runs out → Krebs cycle stops.

Anaerobic respiration (fermentation)

When O₂ is scarce, cells rely on glycolysis for ATP. To keep glycolysis going, pyruvate is converted into another product, which regenerates NAD⁺ from NADH. Location: cytosol. Yield: only the 2 ATP from glycolysis.

Factors affecting the rate of respiration

  • Temperature: respiration is enzyme-controlled, so rate rises to an optimum then falls as enzymes denature.
  • Glucose availability: less substrate means a slower rate.
  • Oxygen concentration: low O₂ limits the ETC; cells switch to fermentation.

Biofuels from biomass

Bioethanol is made by fermenting sugars with yeast. First-generation bioethanol uses food crops (sugarcane, corn starch). Second-generation uses non-food biomass (crop waste, wood): cellulose must first be broken down into glucose by enzymes (cellulases), which is the difficult, expensive step. Biotechnology improves this with better enzymes and genetically modified microbes. Biofuels are renewable but compete for land and water.

A mouse breathes O₂ labelled with ¹⁸O. In which product of respiration does the ¹⁸O appear first? Where do the carbon atoms of glucose end up?

O₂ is used only in the ETC, where it accepts electrons and H⁺ to form water.

The carbon of glucose is released as CO₂ in the Krebs cycle (and the link step from pyruvate to acetyl-CoA).

¹⁸O appears in water; glucose carbon leaves as CO₂. A common mistake is to think the O₂ breathed in becomes the CO₂ breathed out.

  • Glycolysis happens in the cytosol, not in mitochondria, and doesn't need O₂.
  • Most ATP comes from the ETC, not the Krebs cycle.
  • Fermentation produces no extra ATP beyond glycolysis; its job is to regenerate NAD⁺.
  • Plants respire too, in both light and dark.
  • CO₂ from the Krebs cycle, water from the ETC.

Glycolysis releases no CO₂; the ETC makes water.

Give the location and outputs of each stage of aerobic respiration.

Glycolysis (cytosol): pyruvate, 2 ATP (net), NADH. Krebs cycle (mitochondrial matrix): CO₂, 2 ATP, NADH, FADH₂. ETC (inner membrane/cristae): about 26–34 ATP, water, NAD⁺ and FAD.

Explain why a sprinter's muscles produce lactic acid during a 100 m race, and why this is useful in the short term.

During intense exercise, O₂ can't be delivered fast enough for the ETC to keep up. [1] Pyruvate is converted to lactate, which regenerates NAD⁺ from NADH. [1] This allows glycolysis to continue producing a small amount of ATP quickly, without oxygen. [1]

Compare aerobic respiration and anaerobic fermentation in yeast in terms of location, oxygen requirement, products and ATP yield.

Location: aerobic uses the cytosol and mitochondria; fermentation only the cytosol. [1] O₂: required for aerobic (final electron acceptor); not required for fermentation. [1] Products: CO₂ and water vs ethanol and CO₂. [1] ATP: about 30–38 per glucose vs 2 per glucose. [1]

A student says: “The oxygen we breathe in is breathed out as carbon dioxide.” Explain what actually happens to the oxygen.

O₂ is the final electron acceptor in the ETC: it combines with electrons and H⁺ to form water. The CO₂ we breathe out comes from the carbon atoms of glucose, released in the Krebs cycle.

Summary

  • Glycolysis (cytosol) → Krebs (matrix) → ETC (cristae); most ATP from the ETC.
  • O₂ → water; glucose carbon → CO₂.
  • Fermentation regenerates NAD⁺: lactate (animals) or ethanol + CO₂ (yeast); 2 ATP.
  • Bioethanol from biomass needs cellulose broken down to glucose first.
