Structural isomers

Isomers have the same molecular formula but different arrangements of atoms.

TypeWhat differsExample (formula)
ChainThe carbon skeleton (branching)butane and 2-methylpropane (C₄H₁₀)
PositionalThe position of the functional grouppropan-1-ol and propan-2-ol (C₃H₈O)
Functional groupThe functional group itselfpropanal and propanone (C₃H₆O); propanoic acid and methyl ethanoate (C₃H₆O₂)

Stereoisomers

Same structural formula (same connections), different arrangement in space.

  • Cis–trans (geometric) isomers: C=C can't rotate. If each carbon of the double bond has two different groups, the groups can be on the same side (cis) or opposite sides (trans). But-2-ene has cis and trans forms; but-1-ene doesn't (one carbon has two H).
  • Optical isomers (enantiomers): a carbon with four different groups attached is a chiral centre. The molecule and its mirror image can't be superimposed (like left and right hands). Enantiomers have identical physical properties except how they rotate plane-polarised light, but they can behave very differently in the body (enzymes and receptors are chiral).

Which of these has a chiral carbon: propan-2-ol, butan-2-ol, 2-chloropropane, 2-chlorobutane?

A carbon is chiral when it has four different groups attached. A chiral molecule has two non-superimposable mirror-image forms (enantiomers).

Propan-2-ol: C2 has H, OH, CH₃, CH₃. Two groups are the same, so it's not chiral. 2-chloropropane: likewise (two CH₃).

Butan-2-ol: C2 has H, OH, CH₃, C₂H₅: four different groups. 2-chlorobutane: H, Cl, CH₃, C₂H₅: chiral.

Butan-2-ol and 2-chlorobutane each have a chiral centre (C2), so each exists as a pair of enantiomers.

Intermolecular forces and properties

ForcePresent inRelative strength
Dispersion forcesAll molecules; grow with molecular size (more electrons, more surface contact)Weak individually, significant for large molecules
Dipole–dipolePolar molecules (C=O, C–Cl, …)Moderate
Hydrogen bondingMolecules with H bonded to O, N or F (alcohols, carboxylic acids, amines, amides)Strongest intermolecular force
  • Boiling point rises with chain length (more dispersion forces) and falls with branching (less surface contact).
  • Solubility in water: small molecules that can hydrogen bond with water (short alcohols, acids, amines) are soluble. Solubility falls as the non-polar chain grows.
  • Viscosity rises with chain length and hydrogen bonding.
  • Flashpoint (lowest temperature at which the vapour can ignite) rises with boiling point: small, volatile molecules are more flammable.

Rank these molecules of similar mass by boiling point and explain: butane (58), propanal (58), propan-1-ol (60), ethanoic acid (60).

For molecules of similar size, dispersion forces are similar, so boiling point depends on the extra intermolecular forces: dipole–dipole from polar bonds, and hydrogen bonding from O–H groups (strongest).

Butane: non-polar, dispersion only (lowest, −1 °C). Propanal: polar C=O, dipole–dipole as well (49 °C).

Propan-1-ol: hydrogen bonding via O–H (97 °C). Ethanoic acid: hydrogen bonds through both C=O and O–H, and forms hydrogen-bonded dimers (118 °C).

butane < propanal < propan-1-ol < ethanoic acid. With similar dispersion forces, the type of polar interaction decides.

  • Drawing the same molecule twice (e.g. a chain bent differently) isn't an isomer. Name both to check.
  • Cis–trans needs two different groups on each alkene carbon.
  • Chiral centre = four different groups, where the whole group counts, not just the first atom.
  • Boiling breaks intermolecular forces, never covalent bonds.

Explain why pentane (bp 36 °C) has a higher boiling point than its isomer 2,2-dimethylpropane (bp 10 °C).

Both have the same formula and number of electrons. [1] Pentane is a long, unbranched chain with a large surface area in contact with neighbouring molecules; 2,2-dimethylpropane is compact and nearly spherical. [1] So pentane has stronger total dispersion forces, which need more energy to overcome. [1]

A drug molecule has one chiral centre. Explain why a pharmaceutical company might sell only one enantiomer.

Enantiomers have the same physical properties but different 3D shapes. [1] Enzymes and receptors in the body are chiral, so only one enantiomer may fit the target site and have the desired effect. [1] The other may be inactive or cause harmful side effects (e.g. thalidomide), so selling one enantiomer improves safety and effectiveness. [1]

A student draws \(\ce{CH3CH2CH2CH3}\) and \(\ce{CH3CH2}\)–\(\ce{CH2CH3}\) (the same chain written with a bend) and calls them isomers. Comment.

They're the same compound (butane). Both name as butane; rotation around single bonds doesn't create isomers.

Summary

  • Structural isomers: chain, positional, functional group. Stereoisomers: cis–trans (C=C) and enantiomers (chiral C).
  • Boiling point: dispersion (size, shape) < dipole–dipole < hydrogen bonding.
  • Short polar molecules dissolve in water; long chains don't.
