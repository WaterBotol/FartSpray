Quick tests for functional groups

Before using instruments, chemists often run simple tests that give a visible change when a particular functional group is present. They give preliminary evidence, not a full identification.

Functional groupTestPositive resultWhy
C=C (alkenes)Add bromine water (orange-brown)Decolourises quickly at room temperatureBr₂ adds across the double bond (addition reaction). Alkanes don't react in the dark.
–COOH (carboxylic acids)Add sodium carbonate or sodium hydrogen carbonateEffervescence: bubbles of CO₂ (turns limewater milky)The acid reacts: \(\ce{2RCOOH + CO3^2- -> 2RCOO- + H2O + CO2}\). Alcohols aren't acidic enough to react.
–OH on a primary or secondary carbon (alcohols)Warm with acidified potassium dichromate (orange) or permanganate (purple)Dichromate turns green (Cr³⁺); permanganate turns colourless (Mn²⁺)The alcohol is oxidised. Tertiary alcohols give no change. (Aldehydes also give a positive result.)

Melting point: identity and purity

  • Identity: a pure compound melts at a characteristic temperature. Comparing a sample's melting point with a data table (or with a pure reference sample) helps identify it.
  • Purity: a pure compound melts sharply, over a narrow range (about 1–2 °C). Impurities lower the melting point and broaden the melting range.
  • Mixed melting point: mixing the sample with a pure reference; if the melting point doesn't drop, they're likely the same compound.
  • Boiling point identifies liquids in a similar way, and distillation separates liquids with different boiling points.

Pure aspirin melts at 135–136 °C. A student's synthesised aspirin melts over the range 126–132 °C. Interpret this result and suggest how the product could be purified.

The melting point is lower than for pure aspirin, and the range (6 °C) is broad instead of sharp.

Both features indicate the sample is impure (e.g. it still contains unreacted salicylic acid, water or other by-products).

Impure product. Purify by recrystallisation (dissolve in a minimum of hot solvent, cool, filter the crystals and dry them), then re-measure: the range should become narrow and close to 135 °C.

  • Bromine water tests for C=C, not for alcohols or acids.
  • Tertiary alcohols don't react with acidified dichromate, so “no colour change” doesn't mean “no alcohol”.
  • Impurities lower and broaden the melting range. A higher melting point isn't a sign of impurity.
  • Qualitative tests only suggest a functional group; confirm with IR, NMR or MS.

Practice

Which test distinguishes hexene from hexane?

  1. adding sodium carbonate
  2. measuring the density
  3. adding bromine water: hexene decolourises it
  4. adding acidified dichromate: hexane turns it green

Br₂ adds across hexene's C=C, removing the orange-brown colour.

A compound produces bubbles of gas with sodium hydrogen carbonate solution. It is most likely

  1. a carboxylic acid
  2. an alkene
  3. a tertiary alcohol
  4. an ester

Carboxylic acids react with carbonates and hydrogen carbonates to release CO₂.

An unknown alcohol is warmed with acidified dichromate and the solution stays orange. Which conclusion is justified?

  1. The compound isn't an alcohol.
  2. The compound is a primary alcohol.
  3. The compound is a secondary alcohol.
  4. If it is an alcohol, it is most likely a tertiary alcohol.

Tertiary alcohols resist oxidation, so they don't reduce dichromate.

Compared with a pure sample, an impure sample of a solid organic compound usually

  1. melts at a higher temperature over a narrower range
  2. melts at a lower temperature over a broader range
  3. melts at the same temperature
  4. doesn't melt

Impurities disrupt the crystal lattice: lower and broader melting range.

Three unlabelled liquids are propan-1-ol, propanoic acid and hex-1-ene. Describe a sequence of simple chemical tests to identify each, stating the expected observations.

Add sodium carbonate to each: only propanoic acid effervesces (CO₂). [1] Add bromine water to the other two: hex-1-ene decolourises it quickly. [1] Confirm the remaining liquid by warming with acidified dichromate: it turns from orange to green, showing a primary or secondary alcohol (propan-1-ol). [1] Each test relies on a characteristic reaction of one functional group. [1]

Summary

  • Bromine water → C=C; carbonate → COOH; acidified dichromate or permanganate → 1° or 2° alcohols.
  • Pure compounds melt sharply at a characteristic temperature; impurities lower and broaden the range.
