Medicines from plants

Many medicines began as plant remedies. Chemists identify the active ingredient, extract and purify it, work out its structure, and often modify it to make it safer or more effective.

Plant sourceActive compoundUse and story
Willow barkSalicin (converted in the body to salicylic acid)Pain and fever relief. Salicylic acid irritates the stomach, so chemists made aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid) by esterifying its OH group: a modified natural product.
Eucalyptus leavesCineole (eucalyptol)Decongestant and antiseptic; extracted by steam distillation, a traditional Aboriginal medicinal plant and a major Australian industry.

Extraction

MethodHow it worksBest for
Solvent extractionCrushed plant material is mixed with a solvent in which the active compound is very soluble. The compound moves into the solvent (“like dissolves like”: non-polar solvents for non-polar compounds). Using a separating funnel, two immiscible liquids (e.g. water and an organic solvent) are shaken so the compound moves into the layer it's more soluble in; the layers are then run off separately and the solvent evaporated.Compounds that decompose on heating
Steam distillationSteam passes through the plant material, carrying volatile oils out at temperatures below their normal boiling points. The vapour is condensed; the oil separates from the water as a layer.Volatile essential oils, e.g. eucalyptus oil
Distillation / fractional distillationLiquids are separated by boiling point: the lower-boiling component vaporises first, is condensed and collected.Separating and purifying liquid mixtures, removing solvents

Simple vs fractional distillation

  • Simple distillation works when the boiling points are very different (roughly 25 °C or more apart), or to separate a volatile liquid from dissolved solids: e.g. removing a solvent from an extract.
  • Fractional distillation is needed when the boiling points are close together: e.g. ethanol (78 °C) from water (100 °C), or ethanol from other volatile compounds that fermentation makes. A fractionating column packed with glass beads sits between the flask and the condenser. Vapour repeatedly condenses and re-evaporates on the beads. Each cycle enriches the vapour in the lower-boiling component, so the column is hottest at the bottom and coolest at the top.
  • The temperature at the top of the column should stay at the boiling point of the component being collected. If the flask is heated too strongly, vapour hotter than that boiling point reaches the condenser, higher-boiling components come over too, and the distillate is a mixture.
  • Steam distillation extracts heat-sensitive, water-insoluble oils (e.g. eucalyptus oil) at below 100 °C.

Purifying and identifying the product

  • Purification: distillation for liquids; recrystallisation for solids (dissolve in minimum hot solvent, cool, filter, dry).
  • Checking purity: a sharp melting point close to the literature value (see lab analysis); a single spot in TLC or a single peak in HPLC (see chromatography).
  • Confirming structure: IR (functional groups), mass spectrometry (molar mass, fragments) and NMR (carbon and hydrogen environments) (see IR and MS and NMR).
  • Quantity of active ingredient: HPLC with a calibration curve, or a titration if the compound has a suitable functional group (see titrations).

Structure, chirality and action

  • A medicine must fit and interact with its biological target (often an enzyme or receptor): the right shape and functional groups in the right positions to form hydrogen bonds, ionic interactions and dispersion forces.
  • Targets are made of chiral amino acids, so they usually respond differently to the two enantiomers of a chiral drug. One may be active and the other inactive or harmful: in the 1950s–60s, thalidomide was sold as a mixture of enantiomers and caused severe birth defects (and the enantiomers interconvert in the body, so even a single enantiomer wouldn't have been safe).
  • Many medicines act as enzyme inhibitors, competitive or non-competitive (see enzymes and inhibition).

Eucalyptus oil (mainly cineole, bp 176 °C, insoluble in water) is extracted from leaves by steam distillation. (a) Explain why steam distillation is used rather than simply boiling the leaves at 176 °C. (b) The distillate separates into two layers. How is the oil collected? (c) Suggest one instrumental technique to confirm that the oil contains cineole.

Steam distillation lets a volatile, water-insoluble oil distil at about 100 °C, below its own boiling point, so heat-sensitive compounds survive. Immiscible liquids separate into layers by density.

(a) Steam carries the volatile oil over at a temperature close to 100 °C, well below cineole's boiling point, so the compounds aren't decomposed and less energy is needed.

(b) The oil is immiscible with water and less dense, so it forms the top layer; the layers are separated with a separating funnel (then dried).

(c) e.g. gas or liquid chromatography comparing the retention time with a pure cineole standard; or IR/NMR/MS compared with reference spectra. Steam distillation protects heat-sensitive compounds; a separating funnel collects the immiscible oil; instruments confirm identity.

  • Extraction ≠ purification. An extract is a mixture; it still needs purifying and analysing.
  • Enantiomers have the same formula and most physical properties; they differ in how they interact with other chiral molecules (such as enzymes).
  • Choose the solvent by polarity, and justify it.

A student extracts a crystalline compound from a plant and claims it is pure salicylic acid (mp 159 °C). Describe two different tests that would provide evidence for or against this claim, and the expected result if the claim is correct.

Melting point: a pure sample melts sharply (narrow range) at about 159 °C; a low, broad range would indicate impurities. [2]

Instrumental (any one): IR shows a broad O–H (acid, 2500–3300 cm⁻¹), C=O near 1700 cm⁻¹ and an aromatic pattern matching a reference spectrum; or HPLC gives a single peak with the same retention time as a pure standard; or MS shows a molecular ion at m/z 138. [2]

Summary

  • Natural medicines are extracted (solvent extraction, steam distillation), purified (distillation, recrystallisation) and identified (melting point, chromatography, IR, MS, NMR).
  • Structure and chirality decide how a medicine binds its target; many medicines are enzyme inhibitors.
