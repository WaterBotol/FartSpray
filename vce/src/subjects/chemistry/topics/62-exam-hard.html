Ethanol has a density of 0.789 g mL⁻¹ and a heat of combustion of 29.6 kJ g⁻¹. The energy released by burning 1.00 L of ethanol completely is closest to

  1. 23.4 MJ
  2. 29.6 MJ
  3. 37.5 MJ
  4. 1.36 MJ

\(m = 1000 \times 0.789 = 789\ \text{g}\), and \(789 \times 29.6 = 23\,400\ \text{kJ} = 23.4\ \text{MJ}\).

C divides by the density instead of multiplying. Per-litre energy matters for fuel tanks, and it is where ethanol falls well behind petrol.

\(\ce{2H2(g) + O2(g) -> 2H2O(l)}\), \(\Delta H = -572\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}\). A mixture of 1.00 g of H₂ and 16.0 g of O₂ is ignited. The energy released is closest to

  1. 143 kJ
  2. 286 kJ
  3. 572 kJ
  4. 71.5 kJ

\(n(\ce{H2}) = 0.500\ \text{mol}\) needs only 0.250 mol of O₂, and 0.500 mol is present, so H₂ is limiting. Energy \(= 0.500 \times \frac{572}{2} = 143\ \text{kJ}\).

B assumes 1 mol of H₂ reacts. Always identify the limiting reagent before using ΔH.

8.00 g of ammonium nitrate (M = 80.0 g mol⁻¹) dissolves in water in a calorimeter whose calibration factor (including its contents) is 520 J °C⁻¹. The temperature falls by 6.2 °C. ΔH for the dissolution is closest to

  2. \(-32\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}\)
  3. \(+26\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}\)
  4. \(+3.2\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}\)

\(q = 520 \times 6.2 = 3224\ \text{J}\) absorbed and \(n = 0.100\ \text{mol}\). The temperature fell, so the process is endothermic: \(\Delta H = +32\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}\).

10.0 L of methane is mixed with 10.0 L of oxygen and ignited. All volumes are measured at the same temperature and pressure. The volume of CO₂ produced is

  1. 5.0 L
  2. 10.0 L

Acidified dichromate oxidises ethanol to ethanoic acid. Using \(\ce{Cr2O7^2- + 14H+ + 6e- -> 2Cr^3+ + 7H2O}\) and \(\ce{CH3CH2OH + H2O -> CH3COOH + 4H+ + 4e-}\), the amount of Cr₂O₇²⁻ needed to oxidise 0.030 mol of ethanol is

Compared with the combustion of glucose in a bomb calorimeter, the enthalpy change for the complete oxidation of glucose to CO₂ and H₂O by cellular respiration is

  1. the same, because the reactants and products are the same
  2. smaller in magnitude, because some of the energy is stored in ATP
  3. larger in magnitude, because enzymes are involved
  4. positive, because respiration is endothermic

ΔH depends only on the initial and final states. Respiration captures part of the energy in ATP via coupled reactions, but the overall enthalpy change for glucose → CO₂ + H₂O is the same.

B is the popular trap. The ATP captures part of the energy released. It doesn’t change ΔH for the oxidation itself.

Which statement about the effect of adding a catalyst on the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution for a reaction mixture is correct?

  1. The curve shifts to the right.
  2. The curve becomes taller and narrower.
  3. The curve is unchanged, but a larger proportion of particles have energy greater than the (lower) activation energy.
  4. The area under the curve increases.

A catalyst doesn’t change the particles’ energies, so the curve is unchanged. It moves the activation energy line to the left, so more of the area lies beyond it.

Water is added to an equilibrium mixture of \(\ce{Cr2O7^2-(aq) + H2O(l) <=> 2CrO4^2-(aq) + 2H+(aq)}\). The equilibrium

  1. shifts right, because the reaction quotient falls below K
  2. shifts left, because the reaction quotient rises above K
  3. does not shift, because water is not included in the expression
  4. shifts right, because K increases

\(Q = \frac{[\ce{CrO4^2-}]^2[\ce{H+}]^2}{[\ce{Cr2O7^2-}]}\). Diluting multiplies the numerator by a smaller factor (4 terms) than the denominator (1 term), so Q falls below K and the net forward reaction occurs, towards more dissolved particles.

A concentration–time graph for \(\ce{2NO2(g) <=> N2O4(g)}\) shows that at time \(t_1\) the concentrations of both gases suddenly double, after which [NO₂] gradually decreases and [N₂O₄] gradually increases. The change made at \(t_1\) was

  3. a halving of the volume
  4. the addition of NO₂ only

Only a volume change makes every concentration jump by the same factor at once. The system then shifts towards fewer gas particles.

A back titration is used to find the calcium carbonate content of an antacid tablet because

The effect of an enzyme inhibitor can be overcome by increasing the substrate concentration. The inhibitor

  1. binds permanently to a site away from the active site
  2. binds reversibly to the active site, competing with the substrate
  3. denatures the enzyme
  4. is a coenzyme

A competitive inhibitor occupies the active site reversibly. Flooding the mixture with substrate outcompetes it. Non-competitive inhibition, which changes the active site from elsewhere, can’t be overcome this way.

Compound Z has the molecular formula C₅H₁₀O₂ and a pleasant fruity smell.

  • No O–H, so it is not a carboxylic acid: Z is an ester

The C=O at 1740 cm⁻¹ with a C–O at 1240 cm⁻¹, and no O–H, indicates an ester.

Explain what the signal at 1.2 ppm tells you about the structure of Z.

  • Area 6H: two equivalent CH₃ groups
  • Doublet: the adjacent carbon carries 1 H (a CH), so the fragment is (CH₃)₂CH–

Six equivalent hydrogens means two identical CH₃ groups. They are split into a doublet by a single neighbouring H, so the fragment is (CH₃)₂CH–.

\(\ce{CaCO3(s) + 2HCl(aq) -> CaCl2(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)}\); M(CaCO₃) = 100.1 g mol⁻¹.

Calculate the amount of HCl originally added.

\(0.500 \times 0.05000 = 0.02500\ \text{mol}\).

\(1.420 \times 10^{-3} \times \frac{250.0}{20.00} = 0.01775\ \text{mol}\).

\(7.25 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol}\).

Calcium carbonate is insoluble, so it reacts slowly with acid added from a burette and there is no sharp end point.

Some CO₂ remained dissolved in the solution as carbonic acid. State and explain the effect on the calculated percentage of CaCO₃.

Too low. The dissolved CO₂ also neutralises NaOH, so the titre is too high. The excess HCl is overestimated, so the HCl that reacted, and the CaCO₃, are underestimated.

Aspirin is made by reacting salicylic acid with ethanoic anhydride:
\(\ce{C7H6O3 + C4H6O3 -> C9H8O4 + CH3COOH}\)
M: salicylic acid 138.0; ethanoic anhydride 102.0; aspirin 180.0; ethanoic acid 60.0 g mol⁻¹. Density of ethanoic anhydride = 1.08 g mL⁻¹.

Calculate the atom economy for this synthesis of aspirin.

  • Atom economy = M(aspirin) ÷ total M(reactants) × 100
  • \(= \frac{180.0}{240.0} \times 100 = 75.0\%\)

\(\frac{180.0}{138.0 + 102.0} \times 100 = 75.0\%\).

2.00 g of salicylic acid is reacted with 5.0 mL of ethanoic anhydride. Identify the limiting reagent and calculate the theoretical yield of aspirin.

  • \(n(\text{salicylic acid}) = 0.0145\ \text{mol}\); \(n(\text{anhydride}) = \frac{5.0 \times 1.08}{102.0} = 0.053\ \text{mol}\)
  • Salicylic acid is limiting (1 : 1 ratio)
  • Theoretical yield \(= 0.0145 \times 180.0 = 2.61\ \text{g}\)

Salicylic acid (0.0145 mol) is limiting; the anhydride (0.053 mol) is in excess. The theoretical yield is \(2.61\ \text{g}\).

The student obtains 1.96 g of dry aspirin. Calculate the percentage yield.

  • \(75.1\%\)

\(\frac{1.96}{2.61} \times 100 = 75.1\%\).

The crude product melts over the range 128–133 °C. Pure aspirin melts at 136 °C. What does this indicate?

  • The product is impure (the melting point is lowered and the range is broad)

The product is impure. Impurities lower the melting point and widen the melting range.

Briefly describe how recrystallisation purifies the aspirin.

  • Dissolve in the minimum volume of hot solvent, then cool slowly: aspirin crystallises out while the (more soluble, lower-concentration) impurities stay in solution; filter

Dissolve the crude solid in the minimum volume of hot solvent, then cool. Aspirin is much less soluble when cold, so it crystallises. The impurities, present in small amounts, stay dissolved and are removed by filtration.

Describe the trend in the accepted values, and explain it in terms of molecular structure.

  • ΔH becomes more negative by a roughly constant amount (≈ 650 kJ mol⁻¹) for each extra carbon
  • Each additional CH₂ group adds a constant number of bonds broken and formed (1 extra CO₂ and H₂O per molecule)

Each extra carbon makes ΔH about 650 kJ mol⁻¹ more negative. Every additional –CH₂– adds the same set of bonds, producing one more CO₂ and one more H₂O on combustion.

The enzyme lactase hydrolyses lactose into glucose and galactose.

Distinguish between competitive and non-competitive inhibition of an enzyme such as lactase.

  • Competitive: the inhibitor resembles the substrate and binds (reversibly) to the active site, blocking the substrate
  • Non-competitive: the inhibitor binds to a different (allosteric) site, changing the shape of the active site so the substrate can no longer bind effectively

A competitive inhibitor has a similar shape to the substrate and occupies the active site. A non-competitive inhibitor binds elsewhere on the enzyme and changes the shape of the active site.

A graph of reaction rate against lactose concentration rises and then levels off, even though more lactose is added. Explain.

  • At high substrate concentration, all active sites are occupied (the enzyme is saturated)
  • The rate is then limited by the amount of enzyme, not by the substrate concentration

At high lactose concentration every active site is occupied at any moment, so the enzyme is saturated. Adding more substrate can’t increase the rate, which is now limited by the amount of enzyme.

Assessor’s report

General comments

This paper was set above the standard of the VCAA exam. The chemistry was all on the course, but most questions hid a second step: a limiting reagent inside a thermochemistry question, CO₂ already present in a fuel, a scale-up factor in a back titration, an unfamiliar cell to write half-equations for. A median of about 55% is expected. Above 70% is outstanding.

What separated the top scripts

  • Labelled amounts. The best back-titration answers (Question 8) labelled every amount: “HCl added”, “HCl excess in aliquot”, “HCl excess total”, “HCl reacted”. Unlabelled strings of numbers were hard to award method marks to.
  • Reading the whole stem. The 70% efficiency and the 60% methane in Question 1, and the density of the anhydride in Question 9, were all in the stem. Top students underlined them during reading time.
  • Checking the final structure against every piece of data. In Question 7, “propyl ethanoate” fits the IR and the molecular formula but not the doublet or the septet.
  • Using Q. Questions 3c and MC 14 are easiest with the reaction quotient. Le Chatelier alone is vague when water or dilution is involved.

Common errors

  • Multiplying by an efficiency instead of dividing by it.
  • Treating E° as a prediction of rate (MC 8, Question 5a).
  • Assuming ATP storage changes ΔH for respiration (MC 10).
  • Calling a consistent under-reading “random error” (Question 10c).
  • Forgetting that the product of a primary cell must stay at the electrode for the cell to be rechargeable (Question 2d).

Advice

Below 50%: return to the medium paper and aim for 80% there first. Above 65%: your chemistry is solid. Spend your remaining time on timed full papers, so that exam pressure doesn’t cost you the marks you already know how to earn.
