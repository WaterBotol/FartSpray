Infrared (IR) spectroscopy

Covalent bonds vibrate (stretch and bend) at characteristic frequencies. When IR radiation of the matching frequency passes through a sample, it's absorbed, which shows up as a dip in transmittance at that wavenumber (cm⁻¹). Different bonds absorb at different wavenumbers, so the spectrum reveals the functional groups.

BondWavenumber (cm⁻¹)Appearance
O–H (alcohol)3200–3600Broad, strong (hydrogen bonding)
O–H (carboxylic acid)2500–3500Very broad, often overlapping C–H
N–H (amine, amide)3300–3500Medium, sharper than O–H
C–H2850–3300Strong, sharp (in almost every organic molecule)
C=O (aldehyde, ketone, acid, ester, amide)1670–1750Strong, sharp: the most useful peak
C=C1620–1680Variable
C–O1000–1300Strong (alcohols, esters, acids)

Approximate ranges; use the table in your VCAA data book.

Below about 1500 cm⁻¹ is the fingerprint region: a complex pattern unique to each compound. Matching it with a reference spectrum confirms identity.

(1) Is there a strong peak near 1700? (C=O present.) (2) Is there a broad peak above 3000? (O–H: alcohol if 3200–3600; acid if very broad 2500–3300 and there's a C=O.) (3) Is there an N–H? Those three answers usually narrow the family to one or two options.

Mass spectrometry

Molecules are ionised (usually by losing an electron) and break into fragments. Positive ions are separated by their mass-to-charge ratio (\(m/z\)); with charge +1, \(m/z\) = mass.

  • Molecular ion \(\ce{M+}\): the peak with the highest \(m/z\) (ignore small isotope peaks) gives the molar mass.
  • Base peak: the tallest peak (most abundant, most stable fragment), set to 100% relative intensity.
  • Fragments: the difference between peaks shows what was lost.

An unknown compound has a molecular ion at \(m/z = 60\). Its IR spectrum has a very broad band from 2500 to 3300 cm⁻¹ and a strong peak at 1710 cm⁻¹. Its mass spectrum has peaks at 45 and 15. Identify the compound.

IR identifies functional groups from characteristic absorptions; the mass spectrum's molecular ion gives the molar mass and its fragments reveal pieces of the structure. Together they usually pin down a small molecule.

IR: very broad O–H plus C=O → carboxylic acid.

M = 60: the –COOH group is 45, leaving 15 = CH₃. Fragments at 45 (\(\ce{COOH+}\)) and 15 (\(\ce{CH3+}\)) agree.

Ethanoic acid, \(\ce{CH3COOH}\). Its isomer methyl methanoate (\(\ce{HCOOCH3}\), also 60) would show C=O but no broad O–H.

A mass spectrum shows peaks at \(m/z\) = 78 and 80 with heights in the ratio 3 : 1, and a base peak at 43. Suggest the structure.

Chlorine has two isotopes (³⁵Cl and ³⁷Cl, about 3 : 1), so a compound with one Cl shows two molecular-ion peaks 2 units apart in a 3 : 1 ratio.

M and M + 2 in 3 : 1 → one chlorine. With ³⁵Cl, the rest weighs \(78 - 35 = 43\): \(\ce{C3H7}\).

So C₃H₇Cl. The base peak at 43 is \(\ce{C3H7+}\) (loss of Cl).

1-chloropropane or 2-chloropropane. MS alone can't tell these apart; ¹H NMR would (see NMR).

  • Every organic compound shows C–H (≈ 3000). Don't use it as evidence of anything specific.
  • O–H of an acid vs an alcohol: acid is much broader and lower (2500–3300) and comes with C=O.
  • The molecular ion isn't always the tallest peak; the base peak is.
  • Only positive ions are detected; neutral fragments (radicals) are invisible, and you infer them from the mass difference.
  • IR can't distinguish positional isomers easily except through the fingerprint region.

Practice

An IR spectrum shows a strong peak at 1715 cm⁻¹ but no broad peak above 3000 cm⁻¹. The compound could be

  1. propan-1-ol
  2. propanoic acid
  3. propanone
  4. propene

C=O with no O–H: a ketone (or aldehyde, or ester).

In a mass spectrum, the base peak is

  1. the most intense peak
  2. the peak with the highest \(m/z\)
  3. always the molecular ion
  4. the peak at \(m/z = 15\)

The base peak is set at 100% relative intensity. The highest \(m/z\) (excluding isotopes) is the molecular ion.

A compound shows peaks at \(m/z\) = 108 and 110 of almost equal height. It most likely contains

  1. one chlorine atom
  2. two chlorine atoms
  3. a carboxylic acid group
  4. one bromine atom

A 1 : 1 M : M + 2 ratio is bromine's signature (e.g. bromoethane, C₂H₅Br = 108/110).

A broad absorption at 3200–3550 cm⁻¹ and a strong peak near 1050 cm⁻¹, with no peak near 1700 cm⁻¹, indicates

  1. a carboxylic acid
  2. an alcohol
  3. a ketone
  4. an alkene

O–H (alcohol range) and C–O, with no C=O.

The mass spectrum of a ketone has M⁺ at 72 and a large peak at 43. The ketone is most likely

  1. propanone
  2. pentan-2-one
  3. butanone
  4. butanal

Ketone with M = 72: C₄H₈O, butanone (\(\ce{CH3COCH2CH3}\)). 43 is \(\ce{CH3CO+}\) (loss of C₂H₅, 29). Butanal isn't a ketone.

Which pair of compounds would be hardest to tell apart using IR spectroscopy alone (ignoring the fingerprint region)?

  1. propan-1-ol and propan-2-ol
  2. propanal and propan-1-ol
  3. propanoic acid and propan-1-ol
  4. propene and propanone

Positional isomers have the same functional groups (O–H, C–O, C–H). The fingerprint region differs.

Explain why the O–H absorption of an alcohol is broad.

Alcohol molecules hydrogen bond to each other to varying extents, so the O–H bonds are in slightly different environments with slightly different vibration frequencies, and the absorptions merge into a broad band.

A compound has M⁺ = 74. IR shows a strong peak at 1740 cm⁻¹ and strong C–O at about 1200 cm⁻¹, but no O–H. Suggest two possible structures and name them.

C=O and C–O without O–H: an ester. \(M = 74\): C₃H₆O₂. [1]

Methyl ethanoate \(\ce{CH3COOCH3}\) and ethyl methanoate \(\ce{HCOOCH2CH3}\). [2]

A student oxidises propan-1-ol and wants to know whether they have made propanal or propanoic acid. Explain how IR spectroscopy and mass spectrometry could each show which was formed.

IR: both show C=O (≈ 1700–1750). Propanoic acid also shows a very broad O–H at 2500–3300 cm⁻¹; propanal doesn't (and propan-1-ol's broad O–H at 3200–3600 should have disappeared if the reaction went to completion). [2]

MS: molecular ion at 58 for propanal vs 74 for propanoic acid; the acid also shows a fragment at 45 (\(\ce{COOH+}\)). [2]

A student says the compound in a spectrum must be an alkane because there's a strong peak at 2950 cm⁻¹. Critique this reasoning.

C–H stretches around 2850–3000 cm⁻¹ occur in almost all organic compounds, so this peak doesn't identify a family. An alkane is suggested only by the absence of other peaks (no O–H, C=O, C=C, N–H).

Summary

  • IR identifies bonds: C=O ≈ 1700, O–H broad above 3000 (acid very broad, 2500–3300), N–H 3300–3500.
  • MS: M⁺ gives the molar mass; the base peak is the tallest; mass differences identify lost groups.
  • Cl: M, M + 2 at 3 : 1. Br: 1 : 1.
