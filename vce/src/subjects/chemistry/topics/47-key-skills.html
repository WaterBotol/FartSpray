Key science skills are examined in every VCE Chemistry exam, usually wrapped around an experiment you haven't seen (a calorimetry run, a titration, a rate experiment). These are the terms, used the way VCAA uses them.

Errors

TypeWhat it isChemistry examplesHow to reduce it
RandomUnpredictable variation in both directions; affects precisionJudging a colour change at the end point; small fluctuations in temperature readingsRepeat and average; use instruments with better resolution
SystematicA consistent bias in one direction; affects accuracy; repeating doesn't remove itHeat lost to the surroundings in calorimetry (|ΔH| always too small); an uncalibrated balance; rinsing a burette with water (titre always too large); an impure primary standardImprove the method (insulation, calibration, correct rinsing), calibrate instruments, use pure standards
Personal (mistakes)Human errors such as misreading or spillingOvershooting the end point; recording 21.30 as 12.30Not an “error” to analyse: discard the result and repeat. Don't average it in.

Name the specific error, say whether it's random or systematic, and state the direction of its effect on the result (“heat loss means the measured |ΔH| is lower than the true value”). “Human error” with no detail scores nothing.

An outlier is a result that doesn't fit the pattern. Check for a mistake; if you can't explain it, repeat the measurement. In titrations, only concordant titres are averaged, so a rough titre or an outlier is excluded, with a reason.

Uncertainty and significant figures

  • Reading uncertainty: for an analogue scale, usually half the smallest division (a burette marked every 0.10 mL is read to ±0.05 mL); for a digital reading, ± one unit in the last digit.
  • Glassware has a stated tolerance (e.g. a 25.00 mL class A pipette is accurate to about ±0.03 mL). Volumetric glassware is far more precise than a measuring cylinder, which is why it's used for standard solutions and aliquots.
  • Percentage uncertainty = absolute uncertainty ÷ measured value × 100. Small measurements have large percentage uncertainties, so a larger titre or larger temperature change gives a more reliable result.
  • Significant figures: when multiplying or dividing, give the answer to the fewest significant figures in the data; when adding or subtracting, to the fewest decimal places. Keep extra figures during a calculation and round only at the end.

A thermometer marked every 1 °C (read to ±0.5 °C) measures an initial temperature of 21.0 °C and a final temperature of 26.0 °C in a solution calorimetry experiment. (a) Estimate the percentage uncertainty in the temperature change. (b) Suggest one change to reduce it.

\(\Delta T = 26.0 - 21.0 = 5.0\ °\text{C}\). Each reading carries ±0.5 °C, so the change is uncertain by up to about ±1.0 °C when the two uncertainties are combined.

Percentage uncertainty \(\approx \frac{1.0}{5.0} \times 100 = 20\%\).

(a) About 20%. (b) Use a thermometer or temperature probe with better resolution (e.g. 0.1 °C), or use more reactant so the temperature change is larger (same absolute uncertainty, smaller percentage).

Presenting and analysing data

  • Tables: independent variable in the first column, units in the headings, consistent decimal places.
  • Graphs: independent variable on the x-axis; line graphs for continuous data, bar charts for categories; a line or smooth curve of best fit, not dot-to-dot.
  • Calibration curves (HPLC, colorimetry, UV–visible): read unknowns by interpolation. Extrapolating beyond the standards is unreliable, so dilute the sample into range instead (see chromatography).
  • Conclusions say whether the data supports the hypothesis (never “proves”), quote key values, compare with accepted values (percentage difference) and state limitations.

Safety, ethics and sustainability

  • Risk assessment: identify hazards (flammable alcohols, corrosive acids, toxic heavy-metal solutions, hot equipment), assess risk, and state controls (fume hood, safety glasses, gloves, no naked flames near volatile liquids, correct waste disposal). Use the safety data sheet (SDS) for each chemical.
  • Green chemistry in the lab: use the smallest practical amounts (microscale), safer solvents and reagents, renewable feedstocks where possible, and plan to minimise waste. These are principles VCAA expects you to apply when evaluating a method, not just list.
  • Ethics and integrity: record data honestly (including unexpected results), acknowledge sources and help, and don't manipulate data to fit a hypothesis.
  • Primary vs secondary data: primary data you collect yourself; secondary data comes from others (data books, journals, reliable websites). Reference secondary sources consistently.

Practice

A student's four measurements of the heat of combustion of ethanol are −1205, −1210, −1208 and −1212 kJ mol⁻¹. The data-book value is −1360 kJ mol⁻¹. The results are best described as

  1. accurate and precise
  2. precise but not accurate
  3. accurate but not precise
  4. neither accurate nor precise

The values agree closely with each other (precise) but are all about 11% below the true magnitude (not accurate), which points to a systematic error such as heat loss.

Which change would reduce a systematic error in a spirit-burner calorimetry experiment?

  1. repeating the experiment five times and averaging
  2. using a thermometer with a finer scale
  3. recording the temperature more often
  4. adding a draught shield and a lid to reduce heat loss

Heat loss always makes |ΔH| too small, so it's systematic. Repeating (A) reduces random error only; B improves resolution.

During a titration, a student overshoots the end point by several millilitres in one trial. The best way to handle this result is to

  1. discard it as a personal error and repeat the titration
  2. include it in the average, because all data must be used
  3. treat it as a random error and average it with the others
  4. subtract a few millilitres from it and keep it

A known mistake isn't part of the data's natural variation. Record it in the logbook with the reason, exclude it, and repeat.

A 25.00 mL pipette has an uncertainty of ±0.03 mL and a 10.00 mL pipette ±0.02 mL. Which statement is correct?

  1. The 10.00 mL pipette has the smaller percentage uncertainty.
  2. Both have the same percentage uncertainty.
  3. The 25.00 mL pipette has the smaller percentage uncertainty (0.12% vs 0.20%).
  4. Percentage uncertainty can't be calculated for glassware.

0.03/25.00 = 0.12%; 0.02/10.00 = 0.20%. Larger volumes generally give smaller percentage uncertainties.

A result is calculated from 0.0250 mol and 12.47 g. To how many significant figures should the answer be given?

  1. 2
  2. 3
  3. 4
  4. 5

0.0250 has 3 significant figures (leading zeros don't count; the trailing zero does), 12.47 has 4. Give the answer to the fewer: 3.

Two classes use different calorimeters and thermometers to measure the heat of neutralisation and get closely matching results. This demonstrates the result's

  1. accuracy
  2. repeatability
  3. resolution
  4. reproducibility

Different people and equipment getting the same result: reproducibility. It doesn't prove accuracy, since both could share a systematic error.

In a solution calorimetry experiment, the student calibrates the calorimeter electrically but forgets to put the lid on during the reaction (it was on during calibration). Explain the effect on the calculated ΔH.

Without the lid, more heat is lost to the surroundings during the reaction than during calibration. [1] The temperature change is therefore smaller than it would have been, a systematic error. [1] Using the calibration factor with this smaller ΔT gives an energy (and |ΔH|) that is too small. [1]

A student determines the concentration of ethanoic acid in vinegar by titration with NaOH. Identify one random error and one systematic error in this procedure, state the effect of each on the result, and suggest how each could be reduced.

Random: judging the exact end-point colour (or reading the burette meniscus), which scatters the titres above and below the true value. [1] Reduce by repeating until three concordant titres are obtained and averaging them. [1]

Systematic: e.g. rinsing the burette with water, diluting the NaOH so every titre is too large and the calculated acid concentration too high (or: NaOH that has absorbed CO₂ and has a lower concentration than stated). [1] Reduce by rinsing the burette with the NaOH solution (or standardising the NaOH against a primary standard). [1]

Suggest how the spirit-burner investigation comparing alcohols could be made more consistent with the principles of green chemistry, without reducing the validity of the results.

Use smaller amounts of each alcohol (e.g. burn a fixed small mass), reducing waste and hazard. [1] Reuse the water and can, and dispose of residual alcohols correctly rather than down the sink. [1] Choose alcohols that can be made from renewable sources (e.g. bioethanol), and keep controlled variables identical so validity isn't affected. [1]

Summary

  • Accuracy is about the true value; precision is about agreement between repeats.
  • Random errors scatter results and are reduced by repeats; systematic errors bias results and need a better method.
  • Name the error, its type and the direction of its effect.
  • Report to sensible significant figures and use “supports”, not “proves”.
