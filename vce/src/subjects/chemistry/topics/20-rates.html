Collision theory

For particles to react, they must:

  1. collide;
  2. with at least the activation energy \(E_a\);
  3. in the correct orientation.

The rate depends on the frequency of successful collisions. Anything that increases the collision frequency, or the fraction of collisions with enough energy, increases the rate.

The Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution

In a sample of gas (or solution), particles have a range of kinetic energies. The area under the curve represents the total number of particles, and the area to the right of \(E_a\) is the number with enough energy to react.

Raising the temperature makes particles move faster (more frequent collisions) and shifts the distribution to higher energies, so a much larger fraction exceeds \(E_a\). The second effect is far bigger. A 10 °C rise often roughly doubles the rate, but increases the collision frequency by only a few per cent.

The factors

ChangeEffect on collisionsEffect on rate
Increase concentration (solutions)More particles per unit volume → more frequent collisionsIncreases
Increase pressure (gases, by reducing volume)Particles closer together → more frequent collisionsIncreases
Increase surface area (smaller pieces)More particles exposed → more frequent collisionsIncreases
Increase temperatureMore frequent collisions and a larger fraction with \(E \ge E_a\)Increases (strongly)
Add a catalystProvides an alternative pathway with lower \(E_a\) → a larger fraction of collisions succeedIncreases; \(\Delta H\) unchanged; catalyst not consumed

Measuring rates

Rate = change in amount (or concentration) ÷ time. Common methods: volume of gas collected over time, mass loss as gas escapes, colour change measured with a colorimeter, or a precipitate hiding a mark. On a graph of product vs time, the rate is the gradient: steepest at the start, falling to zero when a reactant runs out.

Excess marble chips (CaCO₃) react with 50.0 mL of 1.00 M HCl, and the CO₂ volume is recorded: \(\ce{CaCO3(s) + 2HCl(aq) -> CaCl2(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)}\). Predict how the curve changes if: (a) powdered CaCO₃ is used instead; (b) 50.0 mL of 2.00 M HCl is used; (c) 100 mL of 0.500 M HCl is used.

(a) Larger surface area: steeper initial gradient, but the same final volume (HCl is limiting and unchanged: 0.0500 mol).

(b) Higher concentration: steeper start and double the final volume (0.100 mol HCl).

(c) Same moles of HCl (0.0500 mol) but lower concentration: slower start, same final volume.

Final volume depends on the amount of limiting reactant; initial rate depends on concentration, surface area and temperature. Final \(V(\ce{CO2})\) in the original experiment \(= 0.0250 \times 24.8 = 0.620\text{ L}\) at SLC.

  • A catalyst doesn't change the MB curve. It moves the \(E_a\) line to the left.
  • Higher temperature doesn't lower \(E_a\). It changes the particles, not the barrier.
  • Area under the MB curve stays the same when temperature changes (same number of particles): the peak moves right and gets lower.
  • Rate vs yield: changing the rate doesn't change how much product you finally get (unless it's an equilibrium, where temperature changes \(K\)).
  • “More collisions” isn't enough: say “more frequent successful collisions” (per unit time).

Practice

A catalyst increases the rate of a reaction by

  1. increasing the kinetic energy of the particles
  2. increasing the collision frequency
  3. providing a pathway with a lower activation energy
  4. making ΔH more negative

A greater fraction of collisions then has at least the (lower) activation energy.

When the temperature of a gas increases, the Maxwell–Boltzmann curve

  1. becomes taller and moves left
  2. becomes lower and broader, with its peak moving to higher energy; the area stays the same
  3. keeps the same shape but the area increases
  4. shifts the activation energy to the left

Same number of particles means same area; energies spread out and increase. \(E_a\) is unaffected by temperature.

Powdered zinc reacts faster with acid than a lump of zinc of the same mass because

  1. more zinc atoms are exposed, so collisions with acid are more frequent
  2. the activation energy is lower for powder
  3. powder has more zinc atoms
  4. the acid is more concentrated around powder

Same number of atoms, but greater surface area.

1.0 g of Mg ribbon reacts with excess 1.0 M HCl. The experiment is repeated with excess 2.0 M HCl. Compared with the first experiment, the second has

  1. the same rate and twice the volume of H₂
  2. a faster rate and twice the volume of H₂
  3. the same rate and the same volume of H₂
  4. a faster rate and the same volume of H₂

Mg is limiting (acid in excess both times), so the final volume is unchanged. Higher concentration gives a faster rate.

Increasing the pressure of a gaseous reaction mixture by adding an inert gas at constant volume will

  1. increase the rate, because the pressure is higher
  2. decrease the rate
  3. not change the rate, because the reactant concentrations are unchanged
  4. lower the activation energy

The reactants' partial pressures (concentrations) don't change, so their collision frequency doesn't either.

On a graph of product volume against time, the rate of reaction at any time is given by

  1. the area under the graph
  2. the gradient of the tangent at that time
  3. the final volume divided by the total time
  4. the \(y\)-value at that time

Instantaneous rate = gradient. Option C gives the average rate.

A student says: “Raising the temperature increases the rate mainly because particles collide more often.” Evaluate this statement.

Partly true but incomplete: collisions do become more frequent, but the main reason is that a much larger fraction of collisions now have energy ≥ \(E_a\) (the MB distribution shifts to higher energies). The collision frequency only rises by a few per cent for a 10 °C increase, while the rate often doubles.

Summary

  • Successful collisions need enough energy and the right orientation.
  • Concentration, pressure, surface area: more frequent collisions. Temperature: mainly more energetic collisions. Catalyst: lower \(E_a\).
  • Final amount of product depends on the limiting reactant, not the rate.
