Technique

EquipmentRinse withWhy
BuretteThe solution it will contain (titrant)Water left inside would dilute the titrant
PipetteThe solution it will deliver (aliquot)Water would dilute the aliquot
Conical flaskDeionised waterExtra water doesn't change the moles of analyte in the aliquot
Volumetric flaskDeionised waterIt's filled to the mark with water anyway
  • Titre: the volume delivered from the burette. Repeat until you have three concordant titres (within 0.10 mL of each other), and average those.
  • Equivalence point: reactants have been added in the exact mole ratio. End point: when the indicator changes colour. A good indicator makes these nearly the same.
  • Primary standard: a substance pure enough to weigh directly to make a solution of accurately known concentration. It must be very pure, stable in air (not hygroscopic), and have a high molar mass (smaller weighing error). Example: anhydrous sodium carbonate.

The calculation pattern

  1. \(n\)(titrant) = \(c \times V\) (in L), using the average concordant titre.
  2. Mole ratio from the balanced equation → \(n\)(analyte) in the aliquot.
  3. Scale up by the dilution factor or aliquot fraction to the original sample.
  4. Convert to what's asked (mol L⁻¹, g L⁻¹, % m/v, mg per serve).

25.00 mL of vinegar is diluted to 250.0 mL in a volumetric flask. 20.00 mL aliquots of the diluted solution are titrated with 0.1000 M NaOH, giving titres of 18.40, 18.90, 18.45 and 18.50 mL. Calculate the concentration of ethanoic acid in the vinegar in mol L⁻¹ and in % (m/v).

Concordant titres: 18.40, 18.45, 18.50 (18.90 is an outlier). Average = 18.45 mL.

\(n(\ce{NaOH}) = 0.1000 \times 0.01845 = 1.845 \times 10^{-3}\text{ mol}\). \(\ce{CH3COOH + NaOH -> CH3COONa + H2O}\) (1 : 1), so \(n(\text{acid}) = 1.845 \times 10^{-3}\) mol in 20.00 mL.

Diluted concentration \(= \frac{1.845 \times 10^{-3}}{0.02000} = 0.09225\ \text{M}\). Dilution factor 10, so the vinegar is 0.9225 M.

0.9225 M; mass per litre \(= 0.9225 \times 60.0 = 55.4\text{ g L}^{-1}\), i.e. 5.54% (m/v) (grams per 100 mL).

Citric acid, \(\ce{H3C6H5O7}\) (\(M = 192.1\)), reacts: \(\ce{H3C6H5O7 + 3NaOH -> Na3C6H5O7 + 3H2O}\). A 20.00 mL aliquot of juice needs 15.60 mL of 0.1000 M NaOH. Find the citric acid concentration in g L⁻¹.

\(n(\ce{NaOH}) = 1.560 \times 10^{-3}\text{ mol}\), so \(n(\text{citric}) = \frac{1.560 \times 10^{-3}}{3} = 5.200 \times 10^{-4}\text{ mol}\).

Mass in 20.00 mL \(= 5.200 \times 10^{-4} \times 192.1 = 0.0999\text{ g}\).

\(\frac{0.0999}{0.02000} = 5.00\text{ g L}^{-1}\). Forgetting the 1 : 3 ratio triples the answer.

Choosing an indicator

An indicator changes colour over a narrow pH range. Near the equivalence point the pH jumps sharply (the near-vertical part of the titration curve). Pick an indicator whose colour-change range lies inside that jump, so the end point matches the equivalence point. The pH at equivalence depends on the salt formed.

TitrationpH at equivalenceWhySuitable indicator
Strong acid + strong base (HCl + NaOH)7The salt (NaCl) is neutral. The jump is very large (about pH 3–11).Most indicators work: bromothymol blue, phenolphthalein or methyl orange
Weak acid + strong base (ethanoic acid in vinegar, citric acid + NaOH)Above 7 (about 8–9)The ethanoate ion is a weak base, so the solution is basic at equivalence. The jump is smaller and sits above 7.Phenolphthalein
Strong acid + weak base (HCl + NH₃, or Na₂CO₃ as a primary standard)Below 7 (about 4–5)The ammonium ion is a weak acid, so the solution is acidic at equivalence.Methyl orange or methyl red
Weak acid + weak baseNear 7, but no sharp jumpThe pH changes gradually, so no indicator gives a sharp end point.Avoid; use a pH meter instead

Typical ranges. In the exam, use the values in the VCAA data book's indicator table.

A 2-mark answer names the indicator and links it to the pH at equivalence: “The salt, sodium ethanoate, is basic, so the equivalence point is above pH 7 (about 8.7). Phenolphthalein changes colour between 8.3 and 10.0, within the steep rise, so its end point coincides with the equivalence point.”

  • Equivalence ≠ neutral. Only a strong acid–strong base titration reaches equivalence at pH 7.
  • Methyl orange in a vinegar titration changes colour at about pH 4, long before equivalence, so the titre is far too small and the calculated concentration too low.
  • Use only a couple of drops of indicator. Indicators are weak acids themselves; too much reacts with the titrant.

Redox titrations

An iron tablet is dissolved in acid and made up to 100.0 mL. 20.00 mL aliquots of this Fe²⁺ solution are titrated with 0.0200 M KMnO₄, with an average titre of 15.20 mL. Calculate the mass of iron in the tablet.

\(\ce{MnO4- + 8H+ + 5Fe^2+ -> Mn^2+ + 5Fe^3+ + 4H2O}\). \(n(\ce{MnO4-}) = 0.0200 \times 0.01520 = 3.04 \times 10^{-4}\text{ mol}\).

\(n(\ce{Fe^2+}) = 5 \times 3.04 \times 10^{-4} = 1.52 \times 10^{-3}\text{ mol}\) in 20.00 mL, so \(7.60 \times 10^{-3}\text{ mol}\) in 100.0 mL.

\(m = 7.60 \times 10^{-3} \times 55.8 = 0.424\text{ g}\) of iron. Permanganate is self-indicating: the end point is the first permanent pale pink.

Vitamin C (ascorbic acid, \(\ce{C6H8O6}\), \(M = 176.1\)) reacts with iodine: \(\ce{C6H8O6 + I2 -> C6H6O6 + 2H+ + 2I-}\). A 25.00 mL sample of juice needs 12.40 mL of 0.00500 M I₂ (starch indicator). Calculate the vitamin C content in mg per 100 mL.

\(n(\ce{I2}) = 0.00500 \times 0.01240 = 6.20 \times 10^{-5}\text{ mol} = n(\text{vit C})\).

Mass \(= 6.20 \times 10^{-5} \times 176.1 = 0.01092\text{ g} = 10.9\text{ mg}\) in 25.00 mL.

43.7 mg per 100 mL. End point: the first permanent blue-black colour (excess iodine with starch).

Practice

A student titrates vinegar (ethanoic acid) with standardised NaOH using methyl orange (range 3.1–4.4) as the indicator. Compared with using phenolphthalein, the calculated concentration of ethanoic acid will be

  1. the same, because any indicator works for an acid–base titration
  2. too low, because the colour changes well before the equivalence point
  3. too high, because the colour changes after the equivalence point
  4. too high, because methyl orange reacts with the acid

The equivalence point of a weak acid–strong base titration is about pH 8.7. Methyl orange changes at about pH 4, while much of the acid is still unreacted, so the titre (and the calculated concentration) is too small.

Hydrochloric acid is titrated against a solution of ammonia. Using the indicator table above, select a suitable indicator and justify your choice.

Methyl orange (or methyl red). [1] The salt formed, ammonium chloride, is acidic (NH₄⁺ is a weak acid), so the pH at equivalence is below 7, about 5; methyl orange's range (3.1–4.4) or methyl red's (4.4–6.2) falls within the steep part of the curve. [1]

25.00 mL of Fe²⁺ solution reacts with 20.00 mL of 0.0100 M KMnO₄. The concentration of Fe²⁺ is

  1. 0.00800 M
  2. 0.0100 M
  3. 0.0500 M
  4. 0.0400 M

\(n(\ce{MnO4-}) = 2.00 \times 10^{-4}\); \(n(\ce{Fe^2+}) = 1.00 \times 10^{-3}\); \(c = \frac{1.00 \times 10^{-3}}{0.02500} = 0.0400\ \text{M}\).

Which is the best choice for a primary standard?

  1. solid NaOH, which absorbs water and CO₂ from the air
  2. concentrated HCl
  3. anhydrous sodium carbonate
  4. ethanoic acid

Pure, stable and not hygroscopic. NaOH absorbs water and CO₂, so its mass isn't reliable.

20.00 mL of NaOH solution is neutralised by 17.50 mL of 0.1200 M HCl. Calculate the concentration of the NaOH.

\(n(\ce{HCl}) = 0.1200 \times 0.01750 = 2.100 \times 10^{-3}\text{ mol} = n(\ce{NaOH})\). \(c = \frac{2.100 \times 10^{-3}}{0.02000} = 0.1050\ \text{M}\).

A 1.20 g sample of impure oxalic acid (\(\ce{H2C2O4}\), \(M = 90.0\)) is dissolved and made up to 250.0 mL. 25.00 mL aliquots react with 0.0200 M KMnO₄, average titre 21.30 mL: \(\ce{2MnO4- + 6H+ + 5H2C2O4 -> 2Mn^2+ + 10CO2 + 8H2O}\). Calculate the % purity.

\(n(\ce{MnO4-}) = 0.0200 \times 0.02130 = 4.26 \times 10^{-4}\). [1]

\(n(\ce{H2C2O4}) = \frac52 \times 4.26 \times 10^{-4} = 1.065 \times 10^{-3}\) in 25 mL; \(1.065 \times 10^{-2}\) in 250 mL. [1]

Mass \(= 1.065 \times 10^{-2} \times 90.0 = 0.9585\text{ g}\). [1]

Purity \(= \frac{0.9585}{1.20} \times 100 = 79.9\%\). [1]

In the vinegar analysis above, the student rinsed the burette with deionised water instead of NaOH solution. Explain the effect on the calculated % (m/v) of ethanoic acid.

Water left in the burette dilutes the NaOH, so it's less concentrated than 0.1000 M. [1] A larger volume is needed to reach the end point (larger titre). [1] The calculation uses the larger titre with 0.1000 M, overestimating the moles of NaOH, so the calculated % (m/v) is too high. [1]

In the iron tablet titration, a student forgets to multiply by 5 (the permanganate : iron ratio) but correctly scales from 20.00 mL to 100.0 mL. What mass do they report, and by what factor is it wrong?

\(3.04 \times 10^{-4} \times 5\text{ (aliquots)} \times 55.8 = 0.0848\text{ g}\): five times too small (the correct mass is 0.424 g).

Summary

  • Rinse the burette and pipette with their solutions; the flask with water.
  • Average concordant titres → \(n = cV\) → mole ratio → scale up → convert units.
  • Choose an indicator whose range sits in the steep jump at equivalence: phenolphthalein for weak acid + strong base, methyl orange for strong acid + weak base.
  • Self-indicating permanganate (pink); iodine/starch (blue-black).
