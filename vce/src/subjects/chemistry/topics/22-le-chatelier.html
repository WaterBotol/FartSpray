The principle

If an equilibrium system is disturbed, the system adjusts so as to partially oppose the change.

DisturbanceNet shiftDoes \(K\) change?
Add a reactant (or remove a product)Forwards (uses up some of what was added)No
Decrease volume (increase pressure)Towards fewer gas particlesNo
Increase volume (decrease pressure)Towards more gas particlesNo
Add an inert gas at constant volumeNo shift (concentrations unchanged)No
Dilute an aqueous equilibrium (add water)Towards more dissolved particlesNo
Increase temperatureIn the endothermic directionYes: \(K\) decreases if the forward reaction is exothermic, increases if endothermic
Add a catalystNo shift; equilibrium is reached fasterNo

For concentration and volume changes, use \(Q\) vs \(K\) or collision frequency, not just “the system opposes the change”. Example: “Halving the volume doubles all concentrations. For \(\ce{N2O4 <=> 2NO2}\), \(Q = \frac{[\ce{NO2}]^2}{[\ce{N2O4}]}\) doubles, so \(Q > K\) and the reverse reaction is favoured until \(Q = K\).”

Reading concentration–time graphs

  • Vertical jump in one species → that species was added or removed.
  • All species jump by the same factor → volume change.
  • No jumps, gradual changes in opposite directions → temperature change.
  • Changes in the Change row follow the mole ratio (e.g. NO₂ changes twice as much as N₂O₄).

Explain the effect of increasing the temperature of the Haber equilibrium on (a) the rate of reaction, (b) the equilibrium yield of ammonia, and (c) the value of \(K\).

(a) Rate increases: a greater proportion of collisions have energy ≥ \(E_a\) (for both forward and reverse reactions).

(b) Yield decreases: the forward reaction is exothermic, so the system shifts in the endothermic (reverse) direction to absorb some of the added heat.

(c) \(K\) decreases: for an exothermic forward reaction, \(K\) falls as temperature rises. That's why a “moderate” temperature is a compromise between an acceptable rate and an acceptable yield.

\(\ce{N2O4(g) <=> 2NO2(g)}\), \(\Delta H = +57\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}\). N₂O₄ is colourless and NO₂ is brown. Describe and explain the colour changes when (a) the gas syringe is plunged in to halve the volume, and (b) the mixture is placed in ice water.

(a) Immediately darker (NO₂ concentration doubles), then gradually lighter (but still darker than at the start) as the system shifts left to fewer gas particles.

(b) Lighter: lowering the temperature favours the exothermic direction (reverse), converting brown NO₂ into colourless N₂O₄. \(K\) decreases.

(a) darker then partly lighter; (b) lighter. “Partially opposes” matters: after compression the colour never returns all the way to its original shade.

  • Only temperature changes \(K\).
  • Catalysts don't change yield; they just get you to equilibrium faster.
  • Equal numbers of gas particles on each side (e.g. \(\ce{H2 + I2 <=> 2HI}\)): volume changes cause no shift.
  • Shifts only partially oppose. After adding a reactant, its concentration settles above the original value.
  • Adding a solid (e.g. more CaCO₃ in \(\ce{CaCO3(s) <=> CaO(s) + CO2(g)}\)) causes no shift.

Practice

For \(\ce{2SO2(g) + O2(g) <=> 2SO3(g)}\), \(\Delta H < 0\), which change increases the equilibrium yield of SO₃?

  1. Increasing the temperature
  2. Adding a catalyst
  3. Decreasing the volume
  4. Adding argon at constant volume

3 → 2 gas particles: smaller volume favours the forward reaction.

A catalyst is added to an equilibrium mixture. Which statement is correct?

  1. The yield of products increases
  2. \(K\) increases
  3. Only the forward rate increases
  4. Forward and reverse rates both increase by the same factor

The lower-energy pathway works in both directions. The position of equilibrium is unchanged.

For an endothermic forward reaction, increasing the temperature

  1. increases \(K\) and shifts the equilibrium forwards
  2. decreases \(K\) and shifts it forwards
  3. increases \(K\) and shifts it backwards
  4. has no effect on \(K\)

Heat is effectively a “reactant”; more heat favours the forward (endothermic) reaction, and \(K\) increases.

On a concentration–time graph for \(\ce{H2 + I2 <=> 2HI}\), all three concentrations suddenly double and then stay constant. The disturbance was

  1. an increase in temperature
  2. halving the volume
  3. adding HI
  4. adding a catalyst

All concentrations jumping by the same factor means a volume change. With 2 gas particles each side, \(Q\) is unchanged, so there's no shift afterwards.

Extra NO₂ is added to an equilibrium mixture of N₂O₄ and NO₂ at constant volume and temperature. When equilibrium is re-established, compared with the original equilibrium,

  1. [NO₂] is back to its original value
  2. [NO₂] is lower and [N₂O₄] is higher
  3. [NO₂] and [N₂O₄] are both higher
  4. \(K\) is higher

The shift only partially removes the added NO₂, and some becomes N₂O₄. Both end up higher (see the graph: 0.674 M and 1.26 M vs 0.60 and 1.00).

In the Haber process, a temperature of about 450 °C is used rather than 200 °C because

  1. the rate at 200 °C is too slow, even though the yield would be higher
  2. the yield is higher at 450 °C
  3. \(K\) is larger at 450 °C
  4. the catalyst doesn't work above 200 °C

The reaction is exothermic, so lower temperatures give higher yields, but too slowly: 450 °C is a compromise.

\(\ce{CO(g) + 2H2(g) <=> CH3OH(g)}\), \(\Delta H = -91\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}\). State the effect on the equilibrium yield of methanol of (a) raising the temperature, (b) increasing the pressure by compressing the mixture, (c) removing methanol as it forms.

(a) Decreases (exothermic forward; shifts backwards; \(K\) falls). (b) Increases (3 → 1 gas particles). (c) Increases (lower [CH₃OH] makes \(Q < K\), so the system shifts forwards).

Using \(Q\) and \(K\), explain why halving the volume of \(\ce{N2O4 <=> 2NO2}\) shifts the equilibrium to the left.

Halving the volume doubles every concentration. [1] \(Q = \frac{[\ce{NO2}]^2}{[\ce{N2O4}]}\) becomes \(\frac{(2a)^2}{2b} = 2 \times \frac{a^2}{b} = 2K\). [1] Since \(Q > K\), the net reverse reaction occurs, converting NO₂ into N₂O₄ until \(Q = K\). [1]

Sketch a concentration–time graph for \(\ce{N2(g) + 3H2(g) <=> 2NH3(g)}\) (exothermic) showing: equilibrium, then at time \(t_1\) some NH₃ is removed, then at \(t_2\) the temperature is increased. Describe each feature.

Flat lines at equilibrium. [1] At \(t_1\): NH₃ drops vertically; then NH₃ rises gradually while N₂ and H₂ fall gradually (H₂ falls 3× as much as N₂; NH₃ rises 2× as much as N₂ falls). [1] At \(t_2\): no vertical jumps; NH₃ gradually decreases, N₂ and H₂ gradually increase (reverse reaction favoured). [1] New flat sections at each new equilibrium; the second has a smaller \(K\). [1]

Helium is pumped into a sealed, rigid container holding an equilibrium mixture of N₂, H₂ and NH₃. The total pressure rises. A student predicts more NH₃ will form. Are they right?

No. At constant volume, the concentrations (partial pressures) of N₂, H₂ and NH₃ are unchanged, so \(Q\) still equals \(K\): no shift. Only changing the volume (or the reacting species' concentrations) causes a shift.

Summary

  • Concentration and volume changes shift the position; only temperature changes \(K\).
  • Increase T → shift in the endothermic direction.
  • Smaller volume → fewer gas particles; inert gas at constant V → no shift.
  • Industry balances rate (hot, catalyst, high P) against yield (often cooler).
