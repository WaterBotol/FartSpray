How NMR works

Nuclei such as ¹H and ¹³C behave like tiny magnets. In a strong magnetic field they can absorb radio waves and flip their spin. The exact frequency depends on the chemical environment: electrons around a nucleus shield it, and nearby electronegative atoms (O, Cl, C=O) pull electrons away, deshielding it and shifting its signal to higher \(\delta\).

  • Chemical shift \(\delta\) (ppm) is measured relative to TMS, tetramethylsilane, \(\ce{Si(CH3)4}\), set at 0 ppm. TMS is used because its 12 equivalent H give one strong peak, upfield of almost everything, and it's inert and volatile (easy to remove).
  • Samples are dissolved in deuterated solvents (e.g. \(\ce{CDCl3}\)) so the solvent gives no ¹H signal.

¹³C NMR

Each peak corresponds to a set of equivalent carbons (a carbon environment). Routine ¹³C spectra show no splitting, and peak heights aren't used.

¹H NMR

A high-resolution ¹H spectrum gives four pieces of information:

  1. Number of peak sets = number of hydrogen environments.
  2. Chemical shift = the type of environment.
  3. Integration (relative peak area) = the ratio of H atoms in each environment.
  4. Splitting: a set of H with \(n\) H on neighbouring carbons is split into \(n + 1\) peaks.

Approximate values. Use the table in your data book.

0 neighbours: singlet (s); 1: doublet (d); 2: triplet (t); 3: quartet (q); 6: septet. Peak heights within a multiplet follow Pascal's triangle (1 : 2 : 1 for a triplet, 1 : 3 : 3 : 1 for a quartet). H on O (OH, COOH) usually appear as singlets and don't split their neighbours.

Predict the high-resolution ¹H NMR spectrum of ethanol, \(\ce{CH3CH2OH}\).

Three environments: CH₃, CH₂, OH. Ratio 3 : 2 : 1.

CH₃ (δ ≈ 1.2) has 2 neighbouring H (on CH₂): triplet. CH₂ (δ ≈ 3.7, next to O) has 3 neighbours (CH₃): quartet. OH: singlet (variable, about 2.6).

1.2 ppm triplet (3H), 3.7 ppm quartet (2H), about 2.6 ppm singlet (1H). The triplet–quartet pair is the fingerprint of an ethyl group.

How would ¹H NMR distinguish 1-chloropropane from 2-chloropropane?

1-chloropropane \(\ce{CH3CH2CH2Cl}\): three environments (3 : 2 : 2): CH₃ triplet ≈ 1.0, CH₂ sextet ≈ 1.8, CH₂Cl triplet ≈ 3.5.

2-chloropropane \(\ce{CH3CHClCH3}\): two environments (6 : 1): a doublet ≈ 1.5 (6H) and a septet ≈ 4.1 (1H).

Number of peak sets (3 vs 2), integration (3 : 2 : 2 vs 6 : 1) and splitting all differ. ¹³C NMR also distinguishes them (3 peaks vs 2).

Compound X, C₄H₈O₂, shows: IR: strong 1740 cm⁻¹, strong 1240 cm⁻¹, no broad peak above 3000. ¹³C NMR: 4 peaks, one at 171 ppm. ¹H NMR: 4.1 (quartet, 2H), 2.0 (singlet, 3H), 1.3 (triplet, 3H). Identify X.

IR: C=O and C–O, no O–H → ester. ¹³C: 171 ppm = ester C=O; 4 carbon environments.

¹H: 3H singlet at 2.0 → \(\ce{CH3-CO-}\) with no neighbours. 2H quartet at 4.1 → \(\ce{-O-CH2-}\) next to a CH₃. 3H triplet at 1.3 → CH₃ next to CH₂.

Assemble: \(\ce{CH3-CO-O-CH2-CH3}\).

Ethyl ethanoate. Its isomer methyl propanoate (\(\ce{CH3CH2COOCH3}\)) would instead show a 3H singlet at about 3.7 (OCH₃) and a quartet near 2.3.

  • Splitting counts neighbours (H on adjacent C), not the H in the group itself.
  • Symmetry reduces environments: propanone has one ¹H environment and two ¹³C environments.
  • Integration gives a ratio: 1 : 1.5 might mean 2H : 3H.
  • The TMS peak (0 ppm) isn't part of the compound.
  • OH peaks are singlets and don't split neighbours (in typical VCE spectra).

Practice

How many peaks appear in the ¹³C NMR spectrum of butanone, \(\ce{CH3COCH2CH3}\)?

  1. 3
  2. 4
  3. 2
  4. 8

All four carbons are in different environments.

In the ¹H NMR spectrum of \(\ce{CH3CH2Br}\), the CH₃ signal is a

  1. singlet
  2. doublet
  3. triplet
  4. quartet

CH₃ has 2 neighbouring H (on CH₂): \(2 + 1 = 3\).

How many hydrogen environments does 2-methylpropane, \(\ce{(CH3)3CH}\), have?

  1. 2
  2. 4
  3. 10
  4. 3

Nine equivalent H in the three CH₃ groups, plus the single CH. Ratio 9 : 1.

TMS is used as a reference in NMR because it

  1. has many different H environments
  2. reacts with the sample to form a standard
  3. absorbs at a very high chemical shift
  4. gives a single strong peak, set at 0 ppm, and is inert

12 equivalent H give one peak at a lower δ than almost all organic H.

A compound C₃H₆O has a single peak in its ¹H NMR spectrum. It is

  1. propanal
  2. propanone
  3. prop-2-en-1-ol
  4. propan-1-ol

\(\ce{CH3COCH3}\): both CH₃ groups are equivalent (δ ≈ 2.1). Propan-1-ol is C₃H₈O anyway.

In ethyl ethanoate, the CH₃ group attached to the C=O appears as a

  1. triplet, because it's near the ethyl group
  2. quartet
  3. singlet
  4. doublet

Its neighbouring carbon is the C=O carbon, which has no H. So it's a singlet at about 2.0 ppm.

A ¹H spectrum shows: δ 11.8 (s, 1H), 2.4 (q, 2H), 1.2 (t, 3H). The compound is

  1. propanoic acid
  2. propanal
  3. propan-1-ol
  4. methyl ethanoate

11.8 singlet = COOH; ethyl group (q + t) attached to C=O (CH₂ at 2.4).

Predict the ¹H NMR spectrum (shift, integration, splitting) of propanone and of methyl ethanoate.

Propanone: one singlet at about 2.1 ppm (6H).

Methyl ethanoate \(\ce{CH3COOCH3}\): singlet about 2.0 ppm (3H, CH₃CO) and singlet about 3.7 ppm (3H, OCH₃). No splitting, as neither CH₃ has neighbouring H.

A compound C₃H₈O has a ¹³C spectrum with 2 peaks (25 and 64 ppm) and a ¹H spectrum: 1.2 (doublet, 6H), 2.2 (singlet, 1H), 4.0 (septet, 1H). Identify it, explaining each piece of evidence.

Two C environments in C₃ means symmetry: two equivalent CH₃ groups. [1]

6H doublet: two equivalent CH₃ next to one H. 1H septet at 4.0: a CH next to 6 H and to O (high δ). [1]

1H singlet at 2.2: OH. 64 ppm is C–O. [1]

Propan-2-ol, \(\ce{(CH3)2CHOH}\). [1]

Compound Y has M⁺ = 88 in its mass spectrum. IR: 1740 and 1200 cm⁻¹, no broad O–H. ¹H NMR: 3.7 (s, 3H), 2.3 (q, 2H), 1.1 (t, 3H). ¹³C NMR: 4 peaks. Deduce the structure of Y and explain how the data rules out ethyl ethanoate.

M = 88 and ester IR → C₄H₈O₂ ester. [1]

3H singlet at 3.7: \(\ce{-O-CH3}\) (no neighbours, attached to O). [1] Ethyl group (q 2H + t 3H) with CH₂ at 2.3: next to C=O. [1]

Y is methyl propanoate, \(\ce{CH3CH2COOCH3}\). [1]

Ethyl ethanoate would show the quartet at about 4.1 (\(\ce{-O-CH2-}\)) and the singlet at about 2.0 (\(\ce{CH3CO-}\)). [1]

A student says the CH₂ in \(\ce{CH3CH2CH2Cl}\) (the middle one) should be a quartet because it's next to a CH₃. Correct them.

The middle CH₂ has neighbours on both sides: 3 H (CH₃) + 2 H (CH₂Cl) = 5 neighbouring H, so it's split into \(5 + 1 = 6\) peaks: a sextet.

Summary

  • ¹³C: number of peaks = carbon environments; shift = carbon type.
  • ¹H: environments, shift, integration (H ratio), splitting (\(n + 1\), neighbours).
  • Ethyl group: triplet (3H) + quartet (2H). Isopropyl: doublet (6H) + septet (1H).
  • Combine MS (molar mass), IR (functional groups) and NMR (connectivity).
