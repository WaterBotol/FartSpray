Two ways to measure energy

Heating water (simple calorimetry)
\[ q = mc\Delta T \]

\(m\) = mass of water (g), \(c = 4.18\ \text{J g}^{-1}\,\text{K}^{-1}\) (data book), \(\Delta T\) in °C or K (same size). 1 mL of water ≈ 1 g.

Calibrated calorimeter
\[ \text{CF} = \frac{E}{\Delta T} = \frac{VIt}{\Delta T} \qquad q = \text{CF} \times \Delta T \]

The calibration factor (J °C⁻¹) includes the water, container, thermometer and stirrer. It's more accurate than \(mc\Delta T\).

Then convert heat to an enthalpy change: \(\Delta H = -\dfrac{q}{n}\) for an exothermic reaction (the temperature rose), \(+\dfrac{q}{n}\) for an endothermic one (the temperature fell).

A spirit burner heats 200 g of water from 20.0 °C to 45.0 °C, and burns 1.20 g of ethanol. (a) Calculate the energy absorbed by the water. (b) Calculate the experimental heat of combustion in kJ g⁻¹. (c) Given the data-book value of 29.6 kJ g⁻¹, find the percentage of energy transferred to the water.

(a) \(q = 200 \times 4.18 \times 25.0 = 20\,900\text{ J} = 20.9\text{ kJ}\).

(b) \(\frac{20.9}{1.20} = 17.4\text{ kJ g}^{-1}\).

(c) \(\frac{17.4}{29.6} \times 100 = 58.8\%\).

20.9 kJ; 17.4 kJ g⁻¹; about 59%. The rest heats the air, the can and the clamp, or goes into incomplete combustion (soot on the can).

A bomb calorimeter is calibrated by passing a current of 2.50 A at 5.00 V for 120 s; the temperature rises by 0.60 °C. Then 0.500 g of ethanol (\(M = 46.0\)) is burnt in it, raising the temperature by 5.20 °C. Calculate the molar heat of combustion of ethanol.

\(E = VIt = 5.00 \times 2.50 \times 120 = 1500\text{ J}\); \(\text{CF} = \frac{1500}{0.60} = 2500\text{ J °C}^{-1}\).

\(q = 2500 \times 5.20 = 13\,000\text{ J} = 13.0\text{ kJ}\).

\(n = \frac{0.500}{46.0} = 0.01087\text{ mol}\), so \(\frac{13.0}{0.01087} = 1196\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}\).

\(\Delta H_c = -1.20 \times 10^3\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}\) (negative: exothermic). It's lower in magnitude than the data-book value (1360), suggesting heat loss or incomplete combustion.

5.00 g of ammonium nitrate (\(M = 80.0\)) dissolves in 100 g of water in a polystyrene cup, and the temperature falls by 3.9 °C. Calculate \(\Delta H\) for \(\ce{NH4NO3(s) -> NH4+(aq) + NO3-(aq)}\).

\(q = 100 \times 4.18 \times 3.9 = 1630\text{ J}\) absorbed from the water.

\(n = \frac{5.00}{80.0} = 0.0625\text{ mol}\).

\(\Delta H = +\frac{1.63}{0.0625} = \) +26.1 kJ mol⁻¹ (positive: the solution got colder).

50.0 mL of 1.00 M HCl is mixed with 50.0 mL of 1.00 M NaOH. The temperature rises by 6.7 °C. Assuming the solution behaves like 100 g of water, find \(\Delta H\) for \(\ce{H+(aq) + OH-(aq) -> H2O(l)}\).

\(q = 100 \times 4.18 \times 6.7 = 2800\text{ J}\). Use the total mass of the mixture.

\(n(\ce{H2O}) = 0.0500\text{ mol}\).

\(\Delta H = -\frac{2.80}{0.0500} = \) −56.0 kJ mol⁻¹.

Errors and improvements

Source of errorEffect on measured |ΔH|Improvement
Heat lost to surroundingsToo small (systematic)Insulate; lid; calibrate electrically; draught shield
Incomplete combustion (soot)Too smallBetter air supply; bomb calorimeter with excess O₂
Fuel evaporating from the burner wickToo small (mass loss counted as burnt)Cap the burner when weighing; weigh immediately
Heat absorbed by the container is ignored (using \(mc\Delta T\))Too smallUse a calibration factor
Thermometer resolution / readingRandomDigital probe; repeat trials; extrapolate a temperature–time graph

“Heat loss” on its own rarely gets the mark. Say where the heat goes and which way it pushes the result: “heat lost to the air means \(\Delta T\) is smaller than it should be, so the calculated heat of combustion is lower than the true value.”

  • \(m\) is the mass of the water (or solution), not the mass of fuel.
  • Joules vs kilojoules: \(mc\Delta T\) gives joules. Convert before dividing by moles.
  • Sign of ΔH comes from the direction of temperature change, not from the arithmetic.
  • Calibration factor units: J °C⁻¹ (or kJ °C⁻¹). A CF already includes the water, so don't also use \(mc\Delta T\).

Practice

The energy needed to raise the temperature of 250 mL of water by 12.0 °C is closest to

  1. 3.0 kJ
  2. 12.5 kJ
  3. 50.2 kJ
  4. 1.25 kJ

\(250 \times 4.18 \times 12.0 = 12\,540\text{ J} \approx 12.5\text{ kJ}\).

During calibration, 4.20 kJ of electrical energy raises the calorimeter's temperature by 1.40 °C. The calibration factor is

  1. 5.88 kJ °C⁻¹
  2. 0.333 kJ °C⁻¹
  3. 4.18 kJ °C⁻¹
  4. 3.00 kJ °C⁻¹

\(\text{CF} = \frac{4.20}{1.40} = 3.00\text{ kJ °C}^{-1}\).

A student uses a spirit burner and gets a heat of combustion for methanol much lower than the data-book value. Which is the least likely explanation?

  1. The thermometer read 0.5 °C too high throughout
  2. Heat was lost to the air around the can
  3. Soot formed on the bottom of the can
  4. Methanol evaporated from the wick before the final weighing

A constant offset cancels in \(\Delta T = T_f - T_i\). The others all reduce the energy measured per gram.

In a calibrated calorimeter (CF = 1.85 kJ °C⁻¹), 0.0200 mol of a compound dissolves and the temperature falls by 2.40 °C. \(\Delta H\) for the dissolution is

  1. −222 kJ mol⁻¹
  2. −4.44 kJ mol⁻¹
  3. +222 kJ mol⁻¹
  4. +4.44 kJ mol⁻¹

\(q = 1.85 \times 2.40 = 4.44\text{ kJ}\); \(\frac{4.44}{0.0200} = 222\). The temperature fell, so it's endothermic: +222 kJ mol⁻¹.

Why is electrical calibration more accurate than using \(q = mc\Delta T\) for a calorimeter?

  1. It removes all heat loss
  2. It accounts for heat absorbed by all parts of the calorimeter, not just the water
  3. It makes the reaction faster
  4. It avoids having to measure temperature

The calibration factor includes the container, stirrer and thermometer. Some heat loss still occurs, but it happens similarly in calibration and reaction, so it partly cancels.

Burning 0.800 g of a fuel heats 150 g of water from 18.5 °C to 38.9 °C. Calculate the heat of combustion in kJ g⁻¹, assuming no heat loss.

\(q = 150 \times 4.18 \times 20.4 = 12\,791\text{ J} = 12.8\text{ kJ}\). \(\frac{12.8}{0.800} = 16.0\text{ kJ g}^{-1}\).

A calorimeter is calibrated with 6.00 V and 1.80 A for 150 s, giving a 0.820 °C rise. 0.350 g of octane (\(M = 114\)) is then burnt, raising the temperature by 7.95 °C. Calculate \(\Delta H_c\) for octane and the percentage error compared with −5460 kJ mol⁻¹.

\(E = 6.00 \times 1.80 \times 150 = 1620\text{ J}\); CF \(= \frac{1620}{0.820} = 1976\text{ J °C}^{-1}\). [1]

\(q = 1976 \times 7.95 = 15\,710\text{ J} = 15.71\text{ kJ}\). [1]

\(n = \frac{0.350}{114} = 3.070 \times 10^{-3}\text{ mol}\); \(\Delta H = -\frac{15.71}{0.003070} \approx -5116\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}\). [1]

Error: \(\frac{5460 - 5116}{5460} \times 100 = 6.3\%\) too low in magnitude. [1]

A student measures the heat of neutralisation using a glass beaker instead of an insulated cup. Explain how this affects the result, and suggest two improvements.

Glass conducts heat, so more heat is lost to the surroundings; \(\Delta T\) is lower than it should be and the calculated |ΔH| is too small. [1]

Improvements: use an insulated (polystyrene) cup with a lid; calibrate the calorimeter electrically; record temperature over time and extrapolate back to the moment of mixing. [2]

A student calculates \(q = mc\Delta T\) using the mass of fuel burnt (1.20 g) instead of the mass of water. Explain why this is wrong in terms of what \(q = mc\Delta T\) represents.

\(q = mc\Delta T\) is the heat absorbed by the substance whose temperature changed: here, the water. The fuel's mass is used afterwards, to convert \(q\) into energy per gram (or per mole, via \(n\)).

Summary

  • \(q = mc\Delta T\) (water) or \(q = \text{CF} \times \Delta T\) (calibrated), with \(\text{CF} = \frac{VIt}{\Delta T}\).
  • \(\Delta H = \mp\frac{q}{n}\): negative if the temperature rose.
  • Heat loss and incomplete combustion make experimental |ΔH| too small.
