A reaction is predicted to be spontaneous when an oxidant (left side) is higher in the series than a reductant (right side): the oxidant is reduced (its half-equation runs forwards) and the reductant is oxidised (its half-equation runs backwards). A quick check: the two reacting species make a “negative gradient” (top left to bottom right).

Predict whether a reaction occurs, and write the overall equation if it does: (a) copper wire in silver nitrate solution; (b) copper wire in 1 M hydrochloric acid.

(a) Oxidant \(\ce{Ag+}\) (+0.80) is above reductant Cu (+0.34 on the right side): reaction. \(\ce{Cu -> Cu^2+ + 2e-}\) and \(\ce{Ag+ + e- -> Ag}\) (×2).

(b) The oxidant available is \(\ce{H+}\) (0.00), which is below Cu (+0.34): no reaction predicted.

(a) \(\ce{Cu(s) + 2Ag+(aq) -> Cu^2+(aq) + 2Ag(s)}\) (silver crystals form and the solution turns blue); (b) no reaction.

Galvanic cells

A galvanic cell separates the oxidant and reductant into two half-cells, so electrons are forced to travel through an external circuit: chemical energy becomes electrical energy.

AnodeCathode
ProcessOxidation (the reductant loses e⁻)Reduction (the oxidant gains e⁻)
Polarity in a galvanic cellNegative (−)Positive (+)
ElectronsFlow through the wire from anode to cathode
Salt bridge ionsAnions move towards the anodeCations move towards the cathode

Memory aids: AN OX and RED CAT (anode: oxidation, cathode: reduction). The salt bridge (e.g. filter paper soaked in \(\ce{KNO3}\)) completes the circuit and keeps each half-cell electrically neutral.

Cell potential (theoretical, standard conditions)
\[ E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ_{\text{oxidant half-cell (cathode)}} - E^\circ_{\text{reductant half-cell (anode)}} \]

This is the maximum voltage under standard conditions. The actual voltage is lower once current flows.

A galvanic cell is made from Ag⁺/Ag and Ni²⁺/Ni half-cells. Identify the anode and cathode, write the half-equations and overall equation, state the direction of electron flow, and calculate \(E^\circ_{\text{cell}}\).

The stronger oxidant is \(\ce{Ag+}\) (+0.80), so the Ag electrode is the cathode (+): \(\ce{Ag+(aq) + e- -> Ag(s)}\).

Ni is the stronger reductant, so the Ni electrode is the anode (−): \(\ce{Ni(s) -> Ni^2+(aq) + 2e-}\).

Overall: \(\ce{Ni(s) + 2Ag+(aq) -> Ni^2+(aq) + 2Ag(s)}\). Electrons flow Ni → Ag through the wire.

\(E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = 0.80 - (-0.25) = 1.05\text{ V}\). The \(\ce{Ag+}\) half-equation was doubled to balance electrons, but its \(E^\circ\) is not doubled.

A half-cell contains 1 M \(\ce{Fe^3+}\) and 1 M \(\ce{Fe^2+}\) with a platinum electrode; it's connected to a Zn²⁺/Zn half-cell. Give the cathode reaction, the overall equation and \(E^\circ_{\text{cell}}\). Why is platinum used?

\(\ce{Fe^3+}\) (+0.77) is the stronger oxidant: cathode \(\ce{Fe^3+(aq) + e- -> Fe^2+(aq)}\) on the Pt. Zn is oxidised at the anode.

Overall: \(\ce{Zn(s) + 2Fe^3+(aq) -> Zn^2+(aq) + 2Fe^2+(aq)}\). \(E^\circ = 0.77 - (-0.76) = 1.53\text{ V}\).

Neither \(\ce{Fe^3+}\) nor \(\ce{Fe^2+}\) is a solid that can act as an electrode, so an inert, conducting electrode (Pt or graphite) is needed. It provides a surface for electron transfer but doesn't react.

Limitations of predictions

  • Rate: the series says whether a reaction is favourable, not how fast. A predicted reaction may be too slow to notice (e.g. Al is protected by an oxide layer).
  • Non-standard conditions: concentrations other than 1 M, other temperatures, or gases not at 100 kPa change the potentials, and can even reverse close predictions.
  • Other species: water, dissolved O₂ or spectator ions may react instead.

Primary cells

A primary cell is a galvanic cell that can't be recharged, because its products migrate away or the reaction isn't practically reversible. Examples: alkaline cells (Zn anode, MnO₂ cathode, KOH electrolyte paste) and lithium button cells. A “dry cell” uses an electrolyte paste instead of a liquid.

  • Don't multiply \(E^\circ\) when you scale a half-equation.
  • Galvanic cell polarity: anode −, cathode +. (It's the other way round in electrolysis.)
  • Electrons never travel through the salt bridge or the solution; only ions move there.
  • Choose a salt bridge that won't form a precipitate. KCl with an Ag⁺ half-cell forms AgCl(s); use KNO₃.
  • The anode loses mass (metal dissolves); a metal cathode gains mass as metal is deposited.

The strongest reductant in the table above is

  1. F₂
  2. Li
  3. Li⁺
  4. F⁻

Bottom right of the series. Li⁺ is a very weak oxidant.

In a Zn/Cu galvanic cell, electrons flow

  1. from Cu to Zn through the wire
  2. through the salt bridge from Zn to Cu
  3. from Cu to Zn through the salt bridge
  4. from Zn to Cu through the wire

Zn is oxidised (anode); electrons travel through the external wire to the Cu cathode.

The Ag⁺/Ag and Cu²⁺/Cu half-cells are connected with a KNO₃ salt bridge. The nitrate ions move

  1. towards the copper half-cell
  2. towards the silver half-cell
  3. through the wire
  4. nowhere: they're spectator ions

Cu is the anode (Cu²⁺ ions are being produced), so anions move there to balance the growing positive charge.

\(E^\circ_{\text{cell}}\) for a cell made from Mg²⁺/Mg and Pb²⁺/Pb half-cells is

  1. 2.50 V
  2. −2.24 V
  3. 2.24 V
  4. 2.37 V

\(-0.13 - (-2.37) = 2.24\text{ V}\).

Which metal could be used as a sacrificial anode to protect an iron pipe, according to the series?

  1. Copper
  2. Magnesium
  3. Lead
  4. Nickel

It must be a stronger reductant than Fe (lower on the right side) so it's oxidised in preference: Mg (−2.37) or Zn (−0.76).

Aluminium is predicted to react with water, yet aluminium saucepans don't dissolve when you boil water in them. The best explanation is

  1. the electrochemical series is wrong
  2. water is a stronger reductant than Al
  3. the reaction is endothermic
  4. a protective oxide layer makes the reaction extremely slow

The series predicts what's energetically favourable, not the rate. The Al₂O₃ layer stops the reaction.

Which pair will not react when mixed under standard conditions?

  1. Cl₂ and Br⁻
  2. Fe³⁺ and Cu
  3. Br₂ and Cl⁻
  4. Ag⁺ and Fe²⁺

For Br₂ (+1.09) to oxidise Cl⁻, the Cl₂/Cl⁻ couple (+1.36) would have to be lower. It's higher, so no reaction. The others are all oxidant-above-reductant pairs.

For a galvanic cell made of Cu²⁺/Cu and Zn²⁺/Zn, state (a) the anode, (b) the polarity of the Cu electrode, (c) \(E^\circ_{\text{cell}}\).

(a) Zinc. (b) Positive (it's the cathode). (c) \(0.34 - (-0.76) = 1.10\text{ V}\).

Three metals X, Y and Z are tested: X displaces Y from a solution of Y²⁺ ions; Z doesn't react with X²⁺ ions; Y displaces Z from a solution of Z²⁺ ions. Rank X, Y and Z as reductants, strongest first.

X reduces Y²⁺: X is a stronger reductant than Y. Y reduces Z²⁺: Y is stronger than Z. Z doesn't reduce X²⁺: consistent. [2]

Order: X > Y > Z. [1]

A student builds a cell from a Pt electrode in 1 M Br₂/Br⁻ and a Ni electrode in 1 M Ni²⁺. (a) Write the half-equation at each electrode. (b) Calculate the theoretical voltage. (c) The measured voltage is 1.22 V. Give two reasons for the difference.

(a) Cathode (Pt): \(\ce{Br2(aq) + 2e- -> 2Br-(aq)}\); anode (Ni): \(\ce{Ni(s) -> Ni^2+(aq) + 2e-}\). [2]

(b) \(1.09 - (-0.25) = 1.34\text{ V}\). [1]

(c) Non-standard conditions (concentrations or temperature not exactly standard); internal resistance of the cell and salt bridge; current drawn by the meter. [1]

A student says: “In the Ag/Ni cell, the Ag⁺ half-equation is multiplied by 2, so \(E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = 2(0.80) - (-0.25) = 1.85\text{ V}\).” Explain the error.

\(E^\circ\) is an intensive property (energy per unit charge), so it doesn't depend on how many electrons are transferred. Scaling a half-equation doesn't change its \(E^\circ\): the correct value is 1.05 V.

Summary

  • Oxidant higher than reductant in the series → spontaneous reaction predicted.
  • Galvanic: anode (−) oxidation, cathode (+) reduction; e⁻ from anode to cathode through the wire.
  • \(E^\circ_{\text{cell}}\) = higher \(E^\circ\) − lower \(E^\circ\); never scale \(E^\circ\).
  • Predictions ignore rate and assume standard conditions.
