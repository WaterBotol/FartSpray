Enzymes are protein catalysts

An enzyme speeds up a reaction by providing an alternative pathway with a lower activation energy. It isn't consumed, and it doesn't change \(\Delta H\) or the position of equilibrium.

  • Active site: a pocket formed by the enzyme's tertiary structure, where the substrate binds to form an enzyme–substrate complex.
  • Specificity: the shape and R-group chemistry of the active site suit only particular substrates. In the induced fit model, the active site changes shape slightly as the substrate binds, straining its bonds.
  • Stereospecificity: an enzyme often acts on only one enantiomer of a chiral substrate.

Changing the pH changes the charges on acidic and basic R groups (–COOH ⇌ –COO⁻, –NH₂ ⇌ –NH₃⁺). This disrupts the ionic bonds and hydrogen bonds that hold the tertiary structure, so the active site changes shape and the substrate no longer fits.

Coenzymes are small non-protein organic molecules (many derived from B vitamins) that some enzymes need to function, often by carrying electrons or groups between reactions.

Enzyme inhibition: how many medicines work

Many medicines work by inhibiting a particular enzyme, slowing a reaction that causes disease or symptoms.

Competitive inhibitorNon-competitive inhibitor
Where it bindsThe active site, because its shape and functional groups resemble the substrateA different site on the enzyme (an allosteric site)
EffectBlocks the substrate from binding while it occupies the active siteChanges the enzyme's shape, so the active site no longer works properly
Adding more substrateOutcompetes the inhibitor, so the rate can return towards the maximumDoesn't restore the rate; the maximum rate stays lower
Medicine examplesStatins (block an enzyme in cholesterol synthesis); ibuprofen (blocks the COX enzymes that make inflammatory prostaglandins)Some anti-HIV drugs (non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors) bind away from the active site

A drug that inhibits an enzyme must fit and interact with its binding site: the right shape, functional groups positioned to hydrogen bond or form ionic interactions, and often the right enantiomer. Because enzymes are made of chiral amino acids, they usually bind one enantiomer of a chiral drug much better than the other. For ibuprofen, the S-enantiomer is the active inhibitor.

  • Enzymes aren't “killed” (they aren't alive). They're denatured.
  • Low temperature slows enzymes without denaturing them; activity returns on warming.
  • Enzymes don't change ΔH or equilibrium yield.
  • The active site isn't just a shape: the R groups there interact chemically with the substrate.

  3. pepsin is hydrolysed immediately in the intestine

A medicine has a shape very similar to the natural substrate of an enzyme and binds to its active site. Increasing the substrate concentration restores the reaction rate. The medicine is

  1. a non-competitive inhibitor
  2. a competitive inhibitor
  3. a coenzyme
  4. a denaturing agent

It competes with the substrate for the active site, so enough substrate can outcompete it.

A racemic drug contains equal amounts of two enantiomers, but only one inhibits the target enzyme. The best explanation is that

  1. the enantiomers have different molecular formulas
  2. one enantiomer has a higher boiling point
  3. the inactive enantiomer is always toxic
  4. the enzyme's binding site is chiral, so only one enantiomer's 3D arrangement of groups fits and interacts correctly

Enantiomers have identical formulas and most physical properties, but a chiral binding site distinguishes them.

Lactase hydrolyses lactose into glucose and galactose. (a) Write the equation using molecular formulas. (b) Explain why lactase doesn't hydrolyse sucrose. (c) Lactose-free milk is made by adding lactase at 4 °C over 24 h rather than at 60 °C for 1 h. Suggest a reason.

(a) \(\ce{C12H22O11 + H2O}\) \(\ce{-> C6H12O6 + C6H12O6}\). [1]

(b) Specificity: sucrose has a different shape and linkage, so it doesn't fit (or interact correctly with) lactase's active site. [2]

(c) At 60 °C lactase would denature and stop working (and heating could affect the milk's quality); at 4 °C it works slowly but stays active while the milk stays fresh. [1]

A student says an enzyme increases the yield of a reaction. Correct this statement.

Enzymes are catalysts: they increase the rate of both forward and reverse reactions equally, so equilibrium is reached faster, but the equilibrium position (yield) and ΔH are unchanged.

Summary

  • Enzymes: specific protein catalysts; the active site comes from the tertiary structure.
  • High temperature and extreme pH denature enzymes; cold only slows them.
  • Competitive inhibitors block the active site (overcome by more substrate); non-competitive inhibitors bind elsewhere and change its shape.
  • Many medicines are enzyme inhibitors; chirality often decides which enantiomer is active.
