Gas equations

Ideal gas equation
\[ PV = nRT \]

\(P\) in kPa, \(V\) in L, \(T\) in K (\(T = \theta + 273\)), \(R = 8.31\ \text{J K}^{-1}\,\text{mol}^{-1}\).

Molar volume at SLC
\[ V = n \times 24.8\ \text{L mol}^{-1} \]

SLC = standard laboratory conditions: 25 °C and 100 kPa.

Avogadro's hypothesis: equal volumes of gases at the same temperature and pressure contain equal numbers of particles. So for gases at the same T and P, volume ratios = mole ratios. You can use the equation coefficients directly on volumes.

Calculate the volume of CO₂ at SLC produced by the complete combustion of 5.00 g of propane: \(\ce{C3H8(g) + 5O2(g) -> 3CO2(g) + 4H2O(l)}\).

\(n(\ce{C3H8}) = \frac{5.00}{44.0} = 0.1136\text{ mol}\).

\(n(\ce{CO2}) = 3 \times 0.1136 = 0.3409\text{ mol}\).

\(V = 0.3409 \times 24.8 = \) 8.45 L.

A 10.0 L cylinder holds methane at 500 kPa and 20 °C. What mass of methane does it contain?

\(T = 20 + 273 = 293\text{ K}\).

\(n = \frac{PV}{RT} = \frac{500 \times 10.0}{8.31 \times 293} = 2.05\text{ mol}\).

\(m = 2.05 \times 16.0 = \) 32.9 g. (kPa × L gives J, which matches \(R\) in J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.)

20.0 L of methane is mixed with 50.0 L of oxygen and ignited. After cooling to SLC (water condenses), what is the total volume of gas remaining, and what does it consist of?

\(\ce{CH4(g) + 2O2(g) -> CO2(g) + 2H2O(l)}\). 20.0 L CH₄ needs 40.0 L O₂; 50.0 L is available, so CH₄ is limiting.

O₂ left over: \(50.0 - 40.0 = 10.0\text{ L}\). CO₂ formed: 20.0 L. Water is liquid at SLC, so it contributes no gas volume.

30.0 L: 20.0 L CO₂ and 10.0 L O₂.

How many litres of O₂ at SLC are needed to burn 100 g of octane completely, and how much energy is released? (\(\Delta H_c = -5460\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}\) of octane)

\(n(\ce{C8H18}) = \frac{100}{114} = 0.877\text{ mol}\).

\(\ce{2C8H18 + 25O2 -> 16CO2 + 18H2O}\): \(n(\ce{O2}) = 0.877 \times \frac{25}{2} = 10.96\text{ mol}\), so \(V = 10.96 \times 24.8 = 272\text{ L}\).

272 L of O₂; energy \(= 0.877 \times 5460 = 4.79 \times 10^3\text{ kJ}\). Air is about 21% O₂, so the engine draws in about 1300 L of air for that 100 g of fuel.

  • Kelvin, always: 20 °C is 293 K. Using °C in \(PV = nRT\) gives nonsense.
  • 24.8 L mol⁻¹ is only for gases at SLC. Water at 25 °C is a liquid, and conditions other than SLC need \(PV = nRT\).
  • Units: kPa with L; Pa with m³. Mixing them is out by 1000.
  • Volume ratios only work for gases at the same T and P.

Practice

The volume occupied by 0.250 mol of nitrogen at SLC is

  1. 5.60 L
  2. 24.8 L
  3. 6.20 L
  4. 99.2 L

\(0.250 \times 24.8 = 6.20\text{ L}\). (5.60 L uses the old STP value, 22.4.)

12.4 L of propane burns completely. At the same temperature and pressure, the volume of CO₂ produced is

  1. 37.2 L
  2. 12.4 L
  3. 62.0 L
  4. 4.13 L

\(\ce{C3H8 : CO2} = 1 : 3\) by volume.

0.300 mol of gas is in a 5.00 L container at 30 °C. The pressure is closest to

  1. 15.0 kPa
  2. 1.49 kPa
  3. 1510 kPa
  4. 151 kPa

\(P = \frac{0.300 \times 8.31 \times 303}{5.00} = 151\text{ kPa}\). Option A uses 30 instead of 303 K.

A 1.25 g sample of an unknown gas occupies 0.620 L at SLC. Its molar mass is

  1. 31.0 g mol⁻¹
  2. 50.0 g mol⁻¹
  3. 0.775 g mol⁻¹
  4. 2.02 g mol⁻¹

\(n = \frac{0.620}{24.8} = 0.0250\text{ mol}\); \(M = \frac{1.25}{0.0250} = 50.0\).

10.0 L of H₂ and 10.0 L of O₂ react to form water. After cooling to SLC, the gas remaining is

  1. 10.0 L of H₂
  2. none
  3. 5.0 L of O₂
  4. 10.0 L of water vapour

\(\ce{2H2 + O2 -> 2H2O}\): 10.0 L H₂ uses 5.0 L O₂, leaving 5.0 L O₂. The water is liquid at SLC.

At constant temperature, doubling the pressure on a fixed amount of gas will

  1. halve its volume
  2. double its volume
  3. double the number of moles
  4. not change its volume

\(PV = nRT\) with \(n, T\) fixed: \(V \propto \frac{1}{P}\).

What volume do 3.00 mol of gas occupy at 150 kPa and 40 °C?

\(V = \frac{nRT}{P} = \frac{3.00 \times 8.31 \times 313}{150} = 52.0\text{ L}\).

Methane at SLC releases how much energy per litre? Compare it with hydrogen (\(\Delta H_c = -286\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}\)) and comment on what this means for storing hydrogen.

Methane: \(\frac{890}{24.8} = 35.9\text{ kJ L}^{-1}\). Hydrogen: \(\frac{286}{24.8} = 11.5\text{ kJ L}^{-1}\). [2]

Hydrogen gives about one-third as much energy per litre, so it must be highly compressed or liquefied to store useful amounts, even though it has the highest energy per gram. [1]

A camping stove burns butane: \(\ce{2C4H10(g) + 13O2(g) -> 8CO2(g) + 10H2O(g)}\), \(\Delta H = -5760\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}\). (a) What volume of butane at SLC must be burnt to boil 1.50 kg of water from 20 °C (to 100 °C), if 40% of the energy reaches the water? (b) What volume of CO₂ at SLC is produced?

(a) \(q = 1500 \times 4.18 \times 80 = 501.6\text{ kJ}\); energy released needed \(= \frac{501.6}{0.40} = 1254\text{ kJ}\). [1]

Per mol of butane: \(\frac{5760}{2} = 2880\text{ kJ}\); \(n = \frac{1254}{2880} = 0.4354\text{ mol}\); \(V = 0.4354 \times 24.8 = 10.8\text{ L}\). [2]

(b) \(n(\ce{CO2}) = 4 \times 0.4354 = 1.742\text{ mol}\), so \(V = 43.2\text{ L}\). [1]

A student calculates the volume of water produced when 1.00 mol of methane burns at SLC as \(2 \times 24.8 = 49.6\text{ L}\). What's wrong?

At 25 °C, water is a liquid, so molar volume doesn't apply. 2.00 mol of water is 36.0 g, which is about 36 mL of liquid.

Summary

  • \(PV = nRT\) (kPa, L, K) or \(V = 24.8n\) at SLC.
  • Same T and P: volume ratios = coefficient ratios.
  • Find the limiting reactant before calculating products or leftovers.
