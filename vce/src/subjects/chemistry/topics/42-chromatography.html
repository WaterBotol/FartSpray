How chromatography separates

A mixture is carried by a mobile phase (liquid or gas) past a stationary phase (solid or liquid coated on a solid). Components constantly adsorb onto the stationary phase and desorb back into the mobile phase. A component that's attracted more strongly to the stationary phase (or less soluble in the mobile phase) moves more slowly, so the components separate.

Normal phase (e.g. silica TLC plate, polar): polar components stick and move slowly; non-polar ones travel furthest. Reversed phase HPLC (non-polar stationary phase, polar solvent such as water/methanol): polar components come out first (short retention time), non-polar ones later.

Thin-layer and paper chromatography

Retardation factor
\[ R_f = \frac{\text{distance moved by the component}}{\text{distance moved by the solvent front}} \]

\(R_f\) is always between 0 and 1. Under identical conditions (same plate, solvent and temperature), a component always has the same \(R_f\), so comparing with standards identifies it.

The solvent front travels 8.0 cm. A dye in an ink moves 3.2 cm; a reference dye A moves 3.2 cm on the same plate. Calculate \(R_f\) and comment.

\(R_f = \frac{3.2}{8.0} = 0.40\).

0.40, the same as dye A, so the ink probably contains A. A second test (a different solvent, or an instrumental method) would make the identification more certain, since two substances can share an \(R_f\).

HPLC

In high-performance liquid chromatography, the mobile phase is pumped at high pressure through a column packed with tiny particles (large surface area, excellent separation). A detector (often UV absorbance) produces a chromatogram: peaks against time.

  • Retention time \(R_t\) (time from injection to peak): identifies a component by comparison with standards run under the same conditions.
  • Peak area: proportional to the amount (concentration) of that component.

Quantitative analysis: calibration curves

  1. Run standard solutions of known concentration and record each peak area.
  2. Plot peak area vs concentration and draw the line of best fit (the calibration curve).
  3. Run the unknown under identical conditions, then read its concentration from the curve.
  4. Multiply by any dilution factor.

Caffeine standards of 25, 50, 75 and 100 mg L⁻¹ give HPLC peak areas of 1250, 2480, 3760 and 5010. An energy drink was diluted by a factor of 10 (10.0 mL made up to 100.0 mL) and gave a peak area of 3100. Calculate the caffeine concentration in the drink, and the mass of caffeine in a 250 mL can.

Line of best fit: area ≈ 50.2 × concentration − 15. So for area 3100: concentration ≈ \(\frac{3100 + 15}{50.2} = 62.0\) mg L⁻¹ (in the diluted solution).

Undiluted drink: \(62.0 \times 10 = 620\) mg L⁻¹.

620 mg L⁻¹; a 250 mL can contains \(0.250 \times 620 = 155\) mg of caffeine. Diluting was necessary because the undiluted drink's peak would have been off the calibration range.

  • Measure to the centre of the spot and from the baseline (not from the bottom of the plate).
  • \(R_f\) and retention time depend on conditions: solvent, stationary phase, temperature and flow rate must match the standards.
  • Peak height ≠ amount. Use peak area.
  • Don't forget the dilution factor, and don't apply it twice.
  • The baseline must be above the solvent level in TLC, or the sample dissolves into the solvent reservoir.

Practice

In TLC, a component with a high \(R_f\)

  1. is strongly adsorbed to the stationary phase
  2. is weakly adsorbed to the stationary phase and very soluble in the mobile phase
  3. has a high molar mass
  4. didn't dissolve in the solvent

It spends more time in the mobile phase, so it travels further.

In reversed-phase HPLC (non-polar stationary phase, polar mobile phase), which elutes first?

  1. the most polar component
  2. the least polar component
  3. the component with the largest molar mass
  4. they all elute together

Polar molecules are attracted to the polar mobile phase and hardly stick to the non-polar column.

In HPLC, the quantity used to determine concentration is the

  1. retention time
  2. \(R_f\) value
  3. peak height only
  4. peak area

Peak area is proportional to the amount. Retention time identifies the component.

A spot moves 4.5 cm and the solvent front 7.5 cm. The \(R_f\) is

  1. 1.67
  2. 3.0
  3. 0.60
  4. 0.40

\(\frac{4.5}{7.5} = 0.60\).

An unknown gives a peak area greater than that of the most concentrated standard. The best action is to

  1. extend the line of best fit and read the value
  2. dilute the sample by a known factor and rerun it
  3. report the concentration as equal to the top standard
  4. increase the flow rate

Extrapolating is unreliable (the detector response may stop being linear). Dilute into range, then multiply back.

Explain why a mixture of dyes separates on a paper chromatogram.

Each dye has a different balance of attraction to the stationary phase (paper) and solubility in the mobile phase (solvent). Dyes that adsorb more strongly spend less time in the moving solvent and travel a shorter distance, so they separate.

Using the calibration curve figure above, a 5.00 mL sample of a river water was made up to 50.0 mL and gave a peak area of 5.0. What was the concentration in the river water?

From the curve: 24.5 mg L⁻¹ in the diluted sample. [1] Dilution factor = \(\frac{50.0}{5.00} = 10\). [1] River water: 245 mg L⁻¹. [1]

A sports anti-doping lab uses HPLC to test urine for a banned stimulant. (a) How is the stimulant identified? (b) How is its concentration found? (c) Give two conditions that must be kept constant between standards and samples.

(a) By matching the retention time of a peak with that of a pure standard of the stimulant run under identical conditions. [1]

(b) Run standards of known concentration, plot peak area vs concentration, and read the sample's concentration from the calibration curve (accounting for any dilution). [2]

(c) Any two: column (stationary phase), mobile phase composition, flow rate/pressure, temperature, detector settings, injection volume. [1]

Two substances have the same retention time on an HPLC column. A student concludes they're the same substance. Evaluate this.

Not necessarily: different compounds can have the same retention time on one column. Running again with a different column or mobile phase, or using a second technique (e.g. mass spectrometry of the eluted peak), would confirm identity.

Summary

  • Separation depends on adsorption to the stationary phase vs solubility in the mobile phase.
  • \(R_f\) (TLC) and \(R_t\) (HPLC) identify components under fixed conditions.
  • Peak area + calibration curve gives concentration; multiply by the dilution factor.
