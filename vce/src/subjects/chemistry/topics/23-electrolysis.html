Electrolytic cells

An electrolytic cell uses an external power supply to force a non-spontaneous redox reaction: electrical energy becomes chemical energy.

Galvanic cellElectrolytic cell
EnergyChemical → electricalElectrical → chemical
ReactionSpontaneousNon-spontaneous (driven)
Anode (oxidation)NegativePositive (connected to + terminal)
Cathode (reduction)PositiveNegative (connected to − terminal)
Set-upUsually two half-cells and a salt bridgeOften a single container

Oxidation is always at the anode and reduction is always at the cathode. Only the polarity (sign) swaps between cell types. In electrolysis, the negative cathode attracts cations, which get reduced.

Predicting the products

  1. List every species present: ions, water (if aqueous), and the electrode material (if it's not inert).
  2. Cathode: the strongest oxidant present (highest on the left of the series) is reduced.
  3. Anode: the strongest reductant present (lowest on the right of the series) is oxidised. That includes a non-inert anode metal itself.
  4. Minimum voltage needed ≈ the difference between the two \(E^\circ\) values (in practice more is needed).

VCAA accepts this when the question says the solution is concentrated. Otherwise, go with the electrochemical series and mention the limitation.

1.0 M CuSO₄ is electrolysed with graphite electrodes. Write the half-equations at each electrode, the overall equation, and describe what's observed.

Species: Cu²⁺, SO₄²⁻, H₂O. Cathode: strongest oxidant is Cu²⁺ (+0.34) vs H₂O (−0.83): \(\ce{Cu^2+(aq) + 2e- -> Cu(s)}\).

Anode: the reductants are SO₄²⁻ (not in the series as a reductant; very hard to oxidise) and H₂O: \(\ce{2H2O(l) -> O2(g) + 4H+(aq) + 4e-}\).

Overall (× 2 for Cu): \(\ce{2Cu^2+(aq) + 2H2O(l) -> 2Cu(s) + O2(g) + 4H+(aq)}\).

Pink-brown copper coats the cathode; bubbles of O₂ form at the anode; the blue colour fades (Cu²⁺ used up) and the pH falls (H⁺ produced). Minimum voltage \(\approx 1.23 - 0.34 = 0.89\text{ V}\).

Electroplating and refining

To plate an object with a metal M: make the object the cathode, use an anode of M, and use a solution of M's ions. The anode dissolves at the same rate as metal deposits on the cathode, so the electrolyte concentration stays constant.

Rechargeable (secondary) cells

In a secondary cell, the products of discharge stay in contact with the electrodes, so the reaction can be reversed by an external power supply:

  • Discharging = galvanic (spontaneous; anode −).
  • Recharging = electrolytic (the charger forces the reverse reaction). The electrode that was the anode becomes the cathode, and vice versa. The charger's − terminal is connected to the battery's − terminal.
CellDischarge: anode (−)Discharge: cathode (+)
Lead–acid (car battery)\(\ce{Pb(s) + SO4^2-(aq) -> PbSO4(s) + 2e-}\)\(\ce{PbO2(s) + SO4^2-(aq) + 4H+(aq) + 2e- -> PbSO4(s) + 2H2O(l)}\)
Lithium-ion (one common type)\(\ce{LiC6 -> C6 + Li+ + e-}\) (lithium leaves the graphite)\(\ce{CoO2 + Li+ + e- -> LiCoO2}\) (lithium enters the metal oxide)

Lithium-ion cells have a high voltage and energy density because lithium is very light and a very strong reductant. They use non-aqueous electrolytes, because lithium would react with water.

Green hydrogen

Electrolysis of water (\(\ce{2H2O -> 2H2 + O2}\), non-spontaneous, \(\Delta H = +572\ \text{kJ}\) per 2 mol) powered by solar or wind makes hydrogen without CO₂ emissions. The hydrogen can later be used in a fuel cell: renewable electricity is stored as chemical energy.

  • Polarity: in electrolysis the anode is positive. In galvanic cells it's negative.
  • Don't forget water in aqueous electrolysis, or a reactive anode.
  • Very weak oxidants (Na⁺, K⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺) aren't reduced in water: H₂ forms instead. Those metals need molten electrolytes.
  • During recharging, the battery's positive electrode becomes the anode.

1 M potassium nitrate solution is electrolysed with inert electrodes. The product at the cathode is

  1. hydrogen gas
  2. potassium metal
  3. nitrogen dioxide
  4. oxygen gas

K⁺ (−2.93 V) is a far weaker oxidant than water (−0.83 V), so water is reduced: \(\ce{2H2O + 2e- -> H2 + 2OH-}\).

To electroplate a spoon with silver, the spoon should be

  1. the anode, in a solution of Ag⁺ ions
  2. the cathode, in a solution of Cu²⁺ ions
  3. the anode, with a silver cathode
  4. the cathode, with a silver anode, in a solution of Ag⁺ ions

Ag⁺ is reduced onto the cathode (spoon); the silver anode dissolves to replace the Ag⁺.

While a lead–acid battery is being recharged, the reaction at the electrode connected to the positive terminal of the charger is

  1. \(\ce{Pb + SO4^2- -> PbSO4 + 2e-}\)
  2. \(\ce{PbSO4 + 2H2O -> PbO2 + SO4^2- + 4H+ + 2e-}\)
  3. \(\ce{PbO2 + SO4^2- + 4H+ + 2e- -> PbSO4 + 2H2O}\)
  4. \(\ce{PbSO4 + 2e- -> Pb + SO4^2-}\)

The positive terminal is the anode during recharging (oxidation): the discharge cathode reaction runs in reverse, regenerating PbO₂.

1 M potassium iodide solution is electrolysed with inert electrodes. Write the half-equations at each electrode and state what is observed around the anode.

Cathode: \(\ce{2H2O(l) + 2e- -> H2(g) + 2OH-(aq)}\). Anode: \(\ce{2I-(aq) -> I2(aq) + 2e-}\).

A yellow-brown colour (iodine) appears around the anode, and bubbles form at the cathode.

Explain why aluminium is produced by electrolysis of molten alumina (Al₂O₃ dissolved in molten cryolite) rather than an aqueous solution of an aluminium salt.

Al³⁺ (−1.66 V) is a much weaker oxidant than water (−0.83 V). [1] In aqueous solution, water would be reduced to H₂ at the cathode instead of Al³⁺. [1] With no water present in the melt, Al³⁺ is the strongest oxidant and is reduced to Al. [1]

A phone battery is a lithium-ion cell. (a) State the energy transformation during use and during charging. (b) Explain why these cells can be recharged whereas an alkaline primary cell can't. (c) Why must the electrolyte be non-aqueous?

(a) Use: chemical → electrical (galvanic). Charging: electrical → chemical (electrolytic). [1]

(b) The discharge products remain in contact with the electrodes (Li⁺ stays in the cell and inserts into the electrode materials), so an applied voltage can reverse the reaction. [2]

(c) Lithium is a very strong reductant and would react with water, producing H₂. [1]

A student says: “In electrolysis, the anode is positive, so it must be where reduction happens.” Correct this.

Oxidation always occurs at the anode (AN OX), whatever the cell type. The positive anode attracts anions, which lose electrons to it; reduction happens at the negative cathode.

Summary

  • Electrolytic: forced reaction; anode +, cathode −; oxidation still at the anode.
  • Cathode: strongest oxidant reduced. Anode: strongest reductant (including an active anode) oxidised.
  • Water competes in aqueous solutions; reactive metals need molten electrolytes.
  • Secondary cells: discharge = galvanic, recharge = electrolytic.
