Superposition: waves add

When two waves (or pulses) overlap, the displacement at each point is the sum of the displacements each wave would cause on its own. This is the principle of superposition. After they pass, each wave carries on unchanged, as if the other had never been there.

  • Constructive: crests meet crests, so the displacements add to a bigger one.
  • Destructive: a crest meets a trough, so the displacements partly or completely cancel.
  • Example: a 2.0 cm upward pulse and a 1.0 cm downward pulse of the same shape overlap exactly. At that instant the string shows a 1.0 cm upward pulse. A moment later both pulses re-emerge with their original shapes.

Superposition is also what makes the bright and dark bands in Young's double-slit experiment. Standing waves are superposition of a wave with its own reflection.

How a standing wave forms

  1. A wave travels along a string towards a fixed end.
  2. At the fixed end it reflects and comes back inverted (a crest returns as a trough), with the same frequency, wavelength and speed.
  3. The incident and reflected waves travel in opposite directions through each other and superpose.
  4. If the string's length suits the wavelength, the result is a pattern that doesn't travel: a standing wave. Some points never move (nodes, permanent destructive interference); halfway between them the string oscillates with the largest amplitude (antinodes).
FeatureStanding wave
NodesZero displacement at all times; adjacent nodes are \(\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\) apart
AntinodesMaximum amplitude, midway between nodes; node to antinode is \(\tfrac{\lambda}{4}\)
PhaseAll points between two adjacent nodes move up and down together; points in neighbouring segments move in opposite directions
EnergyNot carried along the string: it stays in each segment, swapping between kinetic and elastic potential energy

Strings fixed at both ends

Both ends of a guitar or violin string are held still, so both ends must be nodes. The string can only hold a whole number of half-wavelengths (“loops”):

\[ L = n\frac{\lambda}{2} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \lambda_n = \frac{2L}{n} \qquad f_n = \frac{v}{\lambda_n} = \frac{nv}{2L} = nf_1 \qquad (n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots) \]
  • \(n = 1\) is the fundamental (first harmonic): one loop, \(\lambda = 2L\). The \(n\)th harmonic has \(n\) loops, \(n\) antinodes and \(n + 1\) nodes (counting both ends).
  • Allowed frequencies are whole-number multiples of the fundamental. Any other frequency doesn't set up a standing wave.
  • The wave speed \(v\) depends on the string (its tension and mass per metre), not on the frequency. A tighter or lighter string has a higher \(v\), so higher frequencies; a longer string gives lower frequencies.

A guitar string of length 0.65 m carries waves at 286 m s⁻¹. Find (a) the wavelength and frequency of the fundamental and (b) the frequency and wavelength of the third harmonic, and the number of nodes and antinodes it has.

With nodes at both ends the string can only hold whole numbers of half-wavelengths. The wave speed is fixed by the string, so each allowed wavelength has one allowed frequency, a whole-number multiple of the fundamental.

(a) Fundamental: \(\lambda_1 = 2L = 2(0.65) = 1.30\ \text{m}\); \(f_1 = v/\lambda_1 = 286/1.30 = 220\ \text{Hz}\).

(b) \(f_3 = 3f_1 = 660\ \text{Hz}\); \(\lambda_3 = 2L/3 = 0.433\ \text{m}\).

(a) 1.30 m, 220 Hz (b) 660 Hz, 0.433 m; 4 nodes (including both ends) and 3 antinodes.

A 1.8 m string fixed at both ends vibrates in four loops at 50 Hz. Find the wavelength and wave speed, and the next higher frequency that would produce a standing wave.

Each loop of a standing wave is half a wavelength, so counting loops gives the wavelength directly; the wave equation then gives the speed. The next standing wave adds one more loop.

Four loops: \(n = 4\), so \(\lambda = 2L/4 = 0.90\ \text{m}\).

\(v = f\lambda = 50 \times 0.90 = 45\ \text{m s}^{-1}\).

0.90 m and 45 m s⁻¹. The fundamental is \(50/4 = 12.5\ \text{Hz}\), so the next standing wave is \(n = 5\): 62.5 Hz.

A string vibrates in its second harmonic at 25 Hz. At \(t = 0\) it is at maximum displacement. Describe its shape at \(t = 0.010\ \text{s}\) and \(t = 0.020\ \text{s}\).

In a standing wave every point oscillates in step within a loop. Over one period the pattern goes from maximum displacement, through flat, to the inverted shape and back.

\(T = 1/25 = 0.040\ \text{s}\). So 0.010 s is \(\tfrac T4\) and 0.020 s is \(\tfrac T2\).

At 0.010 s the string is momentarily flat (every point passing through equilibrium at maximum speed). At 0.020 s it has the same shape as at \(t = 0\) but inverted: each loop has swapped sides.

Standing waves beyond strings

  • Microwave ovens: microwaves reflect off the metal walls and form standing waves. Food at nodes barely heats (cold spots), which is why the turntable rotates. Cold spots are \(\tfrac\lambda2\) apart.
  • Lasers: light bounces between two mirrors, and only wavelengths that fit a whole number of half-wavelengths between them build up.
  • Electrons in atoms: de Broglie modelled an orbiting electron as a standing wave that fits a whole number of wavelengths around the orbit (\(n\lambda = 2\pi r\)), explaining why energy levels are quantised (see energy levels).
  • The fundamental wavelength is 2L, not L. One loop is half a wavelength.
  • Adjacent nodes are λ/2 apart, not λ.
  • Count nodes including the fixed ends. The \(n\)th harmonic has \(n + 1\) nodes and \(n\) antinodes.
  • A standing wave doesn't transport energy along the string, even though it's made of two travelling waves that each do.
  • Half a period later the shape is inverted, not identical. A quarter-period later the string is flat.

Practice

In a standing wave, the distance between two adjacent nodes is

  1. \(\tfrac\lambda4\)
  2. \(\tfrac\lambda2\)
  3. \(\lambda\)
  4. \(2\lambda\)

One loop spans half a wavelength.

A string fixed at both ends is 1.5 m long and the waves on it travel at 30 m s⁻¹. The frequency of its fundamental is

  1. 5 Hz
  2. 45 Hz
  3. 10 Hz
  4. 20 Hz

\(\lambda_1 = 2L = 3.0\ \text{m}\); \(f_1 = v/\lambda = 30/3.0 = 10\ \text{Hz}\). Using \(\lambda = L\) gives the trap answer 20 Hz.

A 1.2 m string fixed at both ends has a wave speed of 96 m s⁻¹. Which frequency could not produce a standing wave on it?

  1. 40 Hz
  2. 80 Hz
  3. 120 Hz
  4. 100 Hz

\(f_1 = 96/(2 \times 1.2) = 40\ \text{Hz}\). Allowed frequencies are 40, 80, 120, … Hz; 100 Hz isn't a whole-number multiple of 40 Hz.

A string vibrates in its third harmonic. Two points lie on opposite sides of the same node, each 5 cm from it. At any instant these points

  1. move in opposite directions
  2. move in the same direction
  3. are both stationary
  4. move in the same direction but with different amplitudes

Neighbouring loops are half a cycle out of phase: when one loop is up, the next is down.

A standing wave on a string has a frequency of 50 Hz and is at maximum displacement at \(t = 0\). At \(t = 0.005\ \text{s}\) the string

  1. has the same shape as at \(t = 0\)
  2. has the inverted shape
  3. is flat
  4. has half its original amplitude everywhere

\(T = 0.020\ \text{s}\), so 0.005 s is a quarter-period: the string passes through equilibrium. At 0.010 s it would be inverted.

Explain how plucking a guitar string, which is fixed at both ends, produces a standing wave. Refer to superposition and to nodes and antinodes.

Waves travel along the string and reflect (inverted) at each fixed end, so waves of the same frequency and wavelength travel in opposite directions along the string. [1] They superpose: at some points they always cancel (destructive interference), giving nodes, including at both fixed ends; midway between, they reinforce, giving antinodes of maximum amplitude. [1] Only wavelengths that fit a whole number of half-wavelengths into the string (\(L = n\lambda/2\)) produce a stable pattern, so the string vibrates at its fundamental and harmonics. [1]

With the turntable removed, a plate of cheese in a microwave oven melts in spots 6.1 cm apart, with unmelted cheese in between. Explain the pattern and use it to estimate the microwave frequency.

Microwaves reflect from the oven walls and superpose with the incoming waves to form a standing wave. The melted spots are antinodes (large oscillating fields) and the unmelted regions are nodes. [1] Adjacent antinodes are \(\lambda/2\) apart, so \(\lambda = 2 \times 0.061 = 0.122\ \text{m}\). [1] \(f = c/\lambda = 3.0\times10^8/0.122 = 2.5\times10^9\ \text{Hz}\) (2.5 GHz). [1]

Summary

  • Superposition: overlapping waves add displacements, then continue unchanged.
  • A travelling wave and its inverted reflection superpose into a standing wave with nodes (always still) and antinodes (maximum amplitude), \(\lambda/2\) apart.
  • Nodes at both ends: \(\lambda_n = 2L/n\), \(f_n = nv/2L = nf_1\); the \(n\)th harmonic has \(n\) loops.
