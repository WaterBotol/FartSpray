A 60 kg person stands on bathroom scales in a lift that is accelerating upwards at \(2.0\ \text{m s}^{-2}\). The scales read

  1. 468 N
  2. 588 N
  3. 708 N
  4. 1200 N

\(N - mg = ma\), so \(N = m(g + a) = 60 \times 11.8 = 708\ \text{N}\).

A (468 N) uses \(g - a\), which applies when accelerating downwards (or decelerating on the way up).

A 3.0 kg trolley on a smooth horizontal bench is connected by a light string over a frictionless pulley to a hanging 1.0 kg mass. The acceleration of the system is closest to

  1. \(2.5\ \text{m s}^{-2}\)
  2. \(3.3\ \text{m s}^{-2}\)
  3. \(7.4\ \text{m s}^{-2}\)
  4. \(9.8\ \text{m s}^{-2}\)

Only the hanging weight drives the whole 4.0 kg system: \(a = \frac{1.0 \times 9.8}{4.0} = 2.45\ \text{m s}^{-2}\).

B divides by the trolley’s mass only. The weight has to accelerate both masses.

A rollercoaster loop has a radius of 8.0 m at the top. The minimum speed at the top for riders to stay in contact with their seats is closest to

  1. \(2.8\ \text{m s}^{-1}\)
  2. \(8.9\ \text{m s}^{-1}\)
  3. \(12.5\ \text{m s}^{-1}\)
  4. \(78\ \text{m s}^{-1}\)

At the minimum speed the normal force is zero and gravity alone supplies the centripetal force: \(v = \sqrt{gr} = \sqrt{78.4} = 8.9\ \text{m s}^{-1}\).

A curve of radius 100 m is banked so that cars travelling at \(20\ \text{m s}^{-1}\) need no sideways friction. The banking angle is closest to

  1. \(12^\circ\)
  2. \(22^\circ\)
  3. \(38^\circ\)
  4. \(68^\circ\)

\(\tan\theta = \frac{v^2}{rg} = \frac{400}{980} = 0.41\), so \(\theta = 22^\circ\).

D is the complement of the correct angle, from using \(\frac{rg}{v^2}\).

A ball is thrown horizontally at \(15\ \text{m s}^{-1}\) from a window 20 m above level ground. It lands a horizontal distance from the wall closest to

  1. 20 m
  2. 30 m
  3. 40 m
  4. 45 m

Fall time \(t = \sqrt{\frac{2h}{g}} = \sqrt{\frac{40}{9.8}} = 2.0\ \text{s}\), so \(x = 15 \times 2.0 = 30\ \text{m}\).

A 1.0 kg ball moving at \(4.0\ \text{m s}^{-1}\) collides head-on with a stationary 3.0 kg ball. The 1.0 kg ball rebounds at \(2.0\ \text{m s}^{-1}\). Which statement is correct?

  1. The collision is inelastic and 6.0 J of kinetic energy is lost.
  2. The collision is elastic.
  3. The collision is inelastic and 2.0 J of kinetic energy is lost.
  4. The collision is impossible because momentum would not be conserved.

Momentum: \(4.0 = -2.0 + 3.0v \Rightarrow v = 2.0\ \text{m s}^{-1}\). KE before \(= 8.0\ \text{J}\); after \(= \tfrac12(1)(2)^2 + \tfrac12(3)(2)^2 = 2.0 + 6.0 = 8.0\ \text{J}\). No KE lost, so it’s elastic.

Getting the sign of the rebound velocity right in the momentum equation is the whole question.

A 20 N force pulls a box 5.0 m along the floor. The force acts at \(60^\circ\) above the horizontal. The work done by the force is

  1. 50 J
  2. 87 J
  3. 100 J
  4. 173 J

Only the component along the displacement does work: \(W = Fd\cos\theta = 20 \times 5.0 \times \cos 60^\circ = 50\ \text{J}\).

A spring with \(k = 100\ \text{N m}^{-1}\) is already stretched by 0.10 m. The extra energy needed to stretch it to 0.20 m is

  1. 0.50 J
  2. 1.0 J
  3. 1.5 J
  4. 2.0 J

\(\Delta E = \tfrac12 k(x_2^2 - x_1^2) = 50(0.040 - 0.010) = 1.5\ \text{J}\).

A is \(\tfrac12 k(0.10)^2\), treating the extra 0.10 m as if it started from zero. Energy depends on \(x^2\), so each extra centimetre costs more.

The area under a graph of gravitational field strength against distance from the centre of a planet, between two distances, represents the

  1. gravitational force on a 1 kg mass
  2. change in gravitational potential energy per kilogram between those distances
  3. orbital speed at those distances
  4. average field strength between those distances

Field strength is force per kilogram, and force × distance is energy, so the area is energy per kilogram (\(\text{J kg}^{-1}\)).

Satellite A orbits a planet at radius \(r\) with period \(T\). Satellite B orbits the same planet at radius \(4r\). The period of B is

  1. \(2T\)
  2. \(4T\)
  3. \(8T\)
  4. \(16T\)

Kepler: \(T^2 \propto r^3\), so \(T_B = T \times 4^{3/2} = 8T\).

Two point charges exert a force \(F\) on each other. Both charges are doubled and the distance between them is also doubled. The new force is

  1. \(\frac{F}{4}\)
  2. \(\frac{F}{2}\)
  3. \(F\)
  4. \(4F\)

\(F \propto \frac{q_1q_2}{r^2}\): the numerator is multiplied by 4 and the denominator by 4.

A proton moves due east through a magnetic field that points vertically upwards. The magnetic force on the proton is directed

  1. north
  2. south
  3. west
  4. vertically downwards

Right-hand rule for a positive charge: fingers along \(\vec B\) (up), thumb along \(\vec v\) (east), palm faces south. The force is perpendicular to both velocity and field.

Check the result is perpendicular to both the velocity and the field. That alone rules out C and D.

In a velocity selector, crossed electric and magnetic fields let particles of one speed pass through undeflected. With \(E = 2.0 \times 10^4\ \text{V m}^{-1}\) and \(B = 0.10\ \text{T}\), that speed is

  1. \(2.0 \times 10^3\ \text{m s}^{-1}\)
  2. \(2.0 \times 10^5\ \text{m s}^{-1}\)
  3. \(2.0 \times 10^7\ \text{m s}^{-1}\)
  4. \(5.0 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{m s}^{-1}\)

Forces balance when \(qE = qvB\), so \(v = \frac{E}{B} = 2.0 \times 10^5\ \text{m s}^{-1}\), independent of charge and mass.

The rate of rotation of an AC generator is doubled. Compared with before, the output has

  1. double the peak EMF and the same frequency
  2. the same peak EMF and double the frequency
  3. double the peak EMF and double the frequency
  4. four times the peak EMF and double the frequency

Faster rotation halves the time for each flux change, doubling the rate of change of flux (so the peak EMF), and completes twice as many cycles per second.

A 10 Ω resistor is connected to an AC supply with a peak voltage of 20 V. The average power dissipated in the resistor is

  1. 10 W
  2. 20 W
  3. 28 W
  4. 40 W

\(V_{\text{RMS}} = \frac{20}{\sqrt2}\), so \(P = \frac{V_{\text{RMS}}^2}{R} = \frac{200}{10} = 20\ \text{W}\).

D uses the peak voltage in \(P = \frac{V^2}{R}\). Average power always uses RMS values.

An ideal step-down transformer converts 240 V to 12 V and supplies a 60 W lamp. The current in the primary coil is

  1. 0.050 A
  2. 0.25 A
  3. 5.0 A
  4. 20 A

An ideal transformer conserves power: \(I_p = \frac{60}{240} = 0.25\ \text{A}\). (The secondary current is 5.0 A.)

Sound of wavelength 0.50 m and light of wavelength 500 nm both pass through a doorway 0.90 m wide. Which statement is correct?

  1. Both diffract significantly because the doorway is the same for both.
  2. The sound diffracts significantly but the light diffraction is negligible.
  3. The light diffracts more because its frequency is higher.
  4. Neither diffracts because the doorway is much wider than both wavelengths.

Diffraction is significant when \(\frac{\lambda}{w}\) is not tiny. For sound \(\frac{\lambda}{w} \approx 0.6\); for light it’s about \(5 \times 10^{-7}\).

Light of wavelength 600 nm passes through two slits 0.30 mm apart onto a screen 2.0 m away. The spacing between adjacent bright fringes is

  1. 0.40 mm
  2. 0.90 mm
  3. 4.0 mm
  4. 40 mm

\(\Delta x = \frac{\lambda L}{d} = \frac{600 \times 10^{-9} \times 2.0}{0.30 \times 10^{-3}} = 4.0 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m}\).

Unit conversions decide this one: nm to m and mm to m. Write the powers of ten explicitly.

An electron and a photon have the same wavelength. They must also have the same

  1. speed
  2. energy
  3. momentum
  4. frequency

For both, \(\lambda = \frac{h}{p}\). Equal wavelengths mean equal momenta. Their energies and speeds differ.

The energy equivalent of 1.0 g of mass is

  1. \(3.0 \times 10^{5}\ \text{J}\)
  2. \(9.0 \times 10^{13}\ \text{J}\)
  3. \(9.0 \times 10^{16}\ \text{J}\)
  4. \(3.0 \times 10^{8}\ \text{J}\)

A 2.0 kg block is pulled up a ramp inclined at \(25^\circ\) by a 20 N force parallel to the ramp. A constant friction force of 3.0 N acts on the block.

Calculate the component of the weight acting down the ramp.

  • \(mg\sin 25^\circ = 8.3\ \text{N}\)

\(2.0 \times 9.8 \times \sin 25^\circ = 8.3\ \text{N}\).

Calculate the acceleration of the block.

  • Net force \(= 20 - 8.3 - 3.0 = 8.7\ \text{N}\)
  • \(a = 4.4\ \text{m s}^{-2}\)

\(F_{\text{net}} = 20 - 8.28 - 3.0 = 8.72\ \text{N}\), so \(a = \frac{8.72}{2.0} = 4.4\ \text{m s}^{-2}\) up the ramp.

Leaving out friction or the weight component were equally common. List all forces along the ramp first.

The block starts from rest and moves 2.0 m up the ramp. Calculate the work done by the 20 N force and the kinetic energy gained by the block.

  • Work by the applied force \(= 40\ \text{J}\)
  • Kinetic energy gained \(= F_{\text{net}}d = 17\ \text{J}\) (17.4 J)

\(W = 20 \times 2.0 = 40\ \text{J}\). \(E_k = F_{\text{net}} d = 8.72 \times 2.0 = 17\ \text{J}\).

Account for the difference between these two energies.

  • About 17 J becomes gravitational potential energy and 6.0 J becomes thermal energy through friction

\(\Delta E_g = 8.28 \times 2.0 = 16.6\ \text{J}\) and friction produces \(3.0 \times 2.0 = 6.0\ \text{J}\) of thermal energy: \(17.4 + 16.6 + 6.0 = 40\ \text{J}\).

A rollercoaster car and its riders have a total mass of 400 kg. The car passes over the top of a vertical loop of radius 10 m, upside down, at \(12\ \text{m s}^{-1}\).

Calculate the magnitude and direction of the net force on the car at the top of the loop.

  • \(F = \frac{mv^2}{r} = 5.8 \times 10^3\ \text{N}\)
  • Directed downwards (towards the centre of the loop)

\(F = \frac{400 \times 12^2}{10} = 5760\ \text{N}\), vertically down towards the centre.

Calculate the normal force exerted by the track on the car at the top.

  • \(N + mg = \frac{mv^2}{r}\)
  • \(N = 1.8 \times 10^3\ \text{N}\) (1840 N) downwards

\(N = 5760 - 400 \times 9.8 = 1840\ \text{N}\), acting downwards (the track pushes down on the upside-down car).

Some answers added the weight instead of subtracting it. At the top both forces point down, so they add to give the net force.

Calculate the apparent weight of a 60 kg rider at the top, as a multiple of their normal weight.

  • Apparent weight \(= m\left(\frac{v^2}{r} - g\right) = 276\ \text{N}\)
  • Ratio \(= \frac{276}{588} = 0.47\)

\(N = 60(14.4 - 9.8) = 276\ \text{N}\), and \(\frac{276}{588} = 0.47\): the rider feels about half their normal weight.

Calculate the maximum height of the stone above the top of the cliff.

  • \(h = \frac{v_y^2}{2g} = 4.7\ \text{m}\)

\(v_y = 9.64\ \text{m s}^{-1}\), \(h = \frac{9.64^2}{19.6} = 4.7\ \text{m}\).

Calculate the time of flight.

  • Sets up \(-30 = 9.64t - 4.9t^2\) (taking up as positive)
  • Solves the quadratic, rejecting the negative root
  • \(t = 3.6\ \text{s}\) (3.65 s)

\(4.9t^2 - 9.64t - 30 = 0\): \(t = \frac{9.64 + \sqrt{9.64^2 + 4(4.9)(30)}}{9.8} = \frac{9.64 + 26.1}{9.8} = 3.6\ \text{s}\).

\(x = 11.5 \times 3.65 = 42\ \text{m}\).

  • \(v = 12\ \text{m s}^{-1}\)

\(v = \frac{30\,000}{2500} = 12\ \text{m s}^{-1}\).

Calculate the kinetic energy lost in the collision.

  • KE before \(= 3.0 \times 10^5\ \text{J}\) and after \(= 1.8 \times 10^5\ \text{J}\)
  • Loss \(= 1.2 \times 10^5\ \text{J}\)

Explain how crumple zones reduce injuries to occupants in a collision like this.

  • The change in momentum of the occupants is fixed by the collision
  • Crumpling increases the time over which the car (and occupants) stop, so the average force \(F = \frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t}\) on the occupants is smaller

The occupants’ change in momentum is set by the collision. Crumple zones deform progressively, increasing the stopping time, so the average force on the occupants is smaller.

At 4.9 m extension, where the cord force equals the weight. Above this point the net force is downward (speeding up); below it, upward (slowing down), so speed peaks where the net force is zero.

Assessor’s report

General comments

This paper matched the demand of a typical VCAA Physics exam. Nearly every Section B question chained two or three ideas: forces then energy, energy then circular motion, gradient then threshold frequency. Students who wrote the principle first (“momentum is conserved”, “the net force is zero at equilibrium”) and then substituted gained method marks even when their arithmetic slipped.

Question-type patterns

  • Quadratics in kinematics and springs. Questions 3b and 5b both needed a quadratic. Set up one equation with a sign convention and solve it once. Splitting the motion into stages costs time and invites errors.
  • Reading data tables as graphs. Question 13 is a VCAA staple: gradient = \(h\) (in eV s), the frequency intercept = threshold frequency, and the voltage intercept = −work function. Always use points far apart on the line of best fit, not a single data point.
  • Proportional reasoning. Kepler’s law (\(T^2 \propto r^3\)), Coulomb’s law and the mass-spectrometer radius (\(r \propto \sqrt m\) at fixed voltage) all reward writing the proportionality before substituting.

Common errors

  • Adding instead of subtracting the weight at the top of a loop. At the top both the normal force and the weight point to the centre.
  • Using peak rather than RMS values for average power.
  • Claiming the EMF is greatest when the flux is greatest. It is greatest when the flux is changing fastest (flux zero).
  • Assuming the heavier isotope has a smaller radius because it is slower.
  • Forgetting the cord’s own length in bungee energy problems.

Advice

Aim for 75% or more here before the harder paper. If your multiple-choice score was much higher than your written score, practise writing full explanations: principle, link to the situation, conclusion.
