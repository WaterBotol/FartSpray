The three laws

Newton's laws link forces (pushes and pulls) to changes in motion. Almost every mechanics question in Unit 3 is one of these laws in disguise, so learn them in words as well as symbols.

First law: inertia

An object stays at rest, or keeps moving at constant velocity (same speed, same direction), unless an unbalanced (net) external force acts on it.

The key word is velocity, not speed. If the direction changes, even at constant speed, there must be a net force. That is why every circular motion problem has a net force.

Inertia is the tendency of an object to resist changes to its motion, and it depends only on mass. A passenger lurches forward when a bus brakes because their body keeps moving forward: no force pushes them forward. The seatbelt supplies the backward force that changes their motion.

Second law: \(F_{\text{net}} = ma\)

Newton's second law
\[ \vec{F}_{\text{net}} = m\vec{a} \]

\(F_{\text{net}}\) is the vector sum of all forces on the object (N), \(m\) its mass (kg) and \(a\) its acceleration (m s⁻²). The acceleration is always in the same direction as the net force.

Two consequences to remember:

  • If \(F_{\text{net}} = 0\) then \(a = 0\). The object is in equilibrium: at rest or moving at constant velocity.
  • The net force tells you the direction of the acceleration, not the direction of motion. A ball thrown upward is moving up while its net force (weight) points down.

Third law: force pairs

If object A exerts a force on object B, then B exerts a force on A that is equal in magnitude and opposite in direction.

Third-law pairs always act on two different objects, are always the same type of force, and exist at the same instant. Because they act on different objects they never cancel each other out.

For a book resting on a table, the weight (Earth pulls book down) and the normal force (table pushes book up) are equal and opposite, but they are not a Newton's third law pair. Both act on the same object (the book), and they are different types of force. They are equal only because the book is in equilibrium; in an accelerating lift they are not equal at all.

  • Pair partner of the book's weight: the book pulls the Earth upward with an equal gravitational force.
  • Pair partner of the normal force: the book pushes down on the table with an equal contact force.

Drawing free-body diagrams

A free-body diagram (FBD) shows one object and every force acting on it, each drawn as an arrow from the object with length roughly proportional to its size.

  1. Isolate one object. Draw it as a box or dot.
  2. Add weight first (always there near Earth), then one contact force for every surface or string touching it.
  3. Never draw forces the object exerts on other things.
  4. Never draw “net force”, “centripetal force” or “\(ma\)” as a separate arrow. Those are the result of the real forces.
  5. Choose axes along and perpendicular to the acceleration. On a slope, that means along and perpendicular to the slope.

Resolving forces into components

Forces are vectors. When forces are not all along one line, split each into two perpendicular components and apply \(F_{\text{net}} = ma\) separately along each axis.

Components of a force \(F\) at angle \(\theta\) to the x-axis
\[ F_x = F\cos\theta \qquad F_y = F\sin\theta \]\[ F = \sqrt{F_x^2 + F_y^2}, \qquad \tan\theta = \frac{F_y}{F_x} \]

Objects on inclined planes

Tilt your axes so that x runs along the slope. Then the normal force has no x-component, and only weight needs resolving:

\[ \text{along the slope: } mg\sin\theta \qquad \text{into the slope: } mg\cos\theta \]\[ \text{frictionless slope: } a = g\sin\theta, \qquad N = mg\cos\theta \]

Sanity-check sin vs cos with extreme angles. At \(\theta = 0^\circ\) (flat) the along-slope component must be zero, and \(\sin 0^\circ = 0\). At \(\theta = 90^\circ\) (vertical) the object free-falls, and \(g\sin 90^\circ = g\).

A 12 kg box is dragged across a horizontal floor by a rope angled 30° above the horizontal. The tension in the rope is 50 N and friction on the box is 20 N.

(a) Calculate the acceleration of the box. (b) Calculate the normal force on the box.

Newton's second law works separately in perpendicular directions. Horizontally the box speeds up, so the horizontal forces are unbalanced; vertically it doesn't move, so the vertical forces must balance. A force at an angle contributes to both directions through its components.

Resolve the tension. Only the horizontal component drags the box forward:

\[ T_x = 50\cos 30^\circ = 43.3\ \text{N}, \]\[ T_y = 50\sin 30^\circ = 25\ \text{N (upward)} \]

Horizontal: apply \(F_{\text{net}} = ma\).

\[ a = \frac{T_x - F_f}{m} = \frac{43.3 - 20}{12} = 1.94\ \text{m s}^{-2} \]

Vertical: no vertical acceleration, so forces balance. The rope is lifting part of the weight, so the floor pushes up less:

\[ N + T_y = mg \Rightarrow N = 12(9.8) - 25 = 117.6 - 25 = 92.6\ \text{N} \]

(a) \(a \approx 1.9\ \text{m s}^{-2}\) in the direction of the pull. (b) \(N \approx 93\ \text{N}\) upward. The normal force is less than \(mg\) because the rope carries some of the weight.

A 5.0 kg block slides down a ramp inclined at 25° to the horizontal.

(a) Find its acceleration if the ramp is frictionless. (b) Find the normal force. (c) Find the acceleration if a constant friction force of 8.0 N acts.

On a slope, choose axes along and perpendicular to the surface. Weight splits into \(mg\sin\theta\) down the slope (which drives the motion) and \(mg\cos\theta\) into the slope (balanced by the normal force, since nothing accelerates into the surface).

Weight component along the slope:

\[ mg\sin\theta = 5.0 \times 9.8 \times \sin 25^\circ = 20.7\ \text{N} \]

(a) Frictionless: this is the only force along the slope.

\[ a = \frac{20.7}{5.0} = 4.14\ \text{m s}^{-2} \quad (= g\sin 25^\circ) \]

(b) Perpendicular to the slope nothing accelerates, so

\[ N = mg\cos\theta = 5.0 \times 9.8 \times \cos 25^\circ = 44.4\ \text{N} \]

(c) With friction acting up the slope (opposing the slide):

\[ a = \frac{mg\sin\theta - F_f}{m} = \frac{20.7 - 8.0}{5.0} = 2.54\ \text{m s}^{-2} \]

(a) 4.1 m s⁻² down the slope (b) 44 N perpendicular to the slope (c) 2.5 m s⁻² down the slope.

Coplanar forces in two dimensions

When several forces act in a plane, add them as vectors (tip to tail) or add components. If the object is in equilibrium the components in each direction sum to zero, which lets you find unknown forces. Hanging signs and ropes at angles are covered on the Tension page.

Apparent weight and lifts

Scales measure the normal force, not your weight. The normal force is your apparent weight. Your true weight \(mg\) never changes in a lift.

Normal force in a lift (up positive)
\[ N - mg = ma \quad\Rightarrow\quad N = m(g + a) \]

Use \(a > 0\) for upward acceleration and \(a < 0\) for downward acceleration. If \(a = -g\) (free fall), \(N = 0\) and you feel weightless.

A lift moving downward but slowing down is accelerating upward, so you feel heavier. A lift moving upward at constant speed has \(a = 0\), so the scale reads your true weight. Always ask “which way is the acceleration?”, never “which way is it moving?”.

A 60 kg student stands on bathroom scales in a lift. The lift is moving downward and slowing down at 1.5 m s⁻². What reading (in newtons, and in the “kg” the scale displays) do the scales show?

Scales read the normal force, not your weight. In an accelerating lift the normal force must provide both support and the extra (or reduced) force needed to accelerate you, so \(N - mg = ma\). What matters is the direction of the acceleration, not of the motion.

Direction of acceleration. Moving down and slowing means the acceleration is opposite to the velocity: upward, so \(a = +1.5\ \text{m s}^{-2}\).

Apply \(N = m(g + a)\):

\[ N = 60(9.8 + 1.5) = 678\ \text{N} \]

Scale display. Scales divide the force by 9.8:

\[ \frac{678}{9.8} = 69.2\ \text{“kg”} \]

678 N (the scale shows about 69 kg). This is exactly the same as for a lift moving up and speeding up at 1.5 m s⁻².

Solving any Newton's-law problem

  1. Draw an FBD for each object separately.
  2. Choose a positive direction (usually the direction of acceleration).
  3. Resolve any angled forces into components.
  4. Write \(F_{\text{net}} = ma\) along the direction of acceleration, and \(\Sigma F = 0\) perpendicular to it.
  5. Solve, then check the sign and size make sense.

In “show that” or “explain” questions, name each force and the object exerting it, e.g. “the normal force from the seat on the rider”. Answers like “the force” or “gravity pushes” lose marks. Always write the equation you use before substituting.

Practice

A book rests on a table. According to Newton's third law, the reaction to the gravitational force of Earth on the book is:

  1. the normal force of the table on the book
  2. the force of the book on the table
  3. the gravitational force of the book on Earth
  4. the friction force of the table on the book

Third-law pairs involve the same two objects and the same type of force. Earth pulls the book (gravity), so the book pulls Earth (gravity) with equal magnitude. The normal force (A) is a different type of force on the same object: it is balanced with weight only because \(a = 0\).

A car travels along a straight, level highway at a constant 100 km h⁻¹. Which statement about the forces on the car is correct?

  1. The driving force is larger than the resistive forces.
  2. The net force on the car is zero.
  3. There is a net forward force that keeps the car moving.
  4. There are no horizontal forces on the car.

Constant velocity means \(a = 0\), so \(F_{\text{net}} = 0\) (first law). The driving force exactly balances drag and rolling resistance. No net force is needed to keep something moving.

A person stands on scales in a lift that is moving upward but slowing down. Compared to their true weight, the scales read:

  1. less than their true weight
  2. equal to their true weight
  3. more than their true weight
  4. zero

Moving up and slowing means the acceleration is downward. Then \(N = m(g + a)\) with \(a < 0\), so \(N < mg\). The direction of travel is a distractor.

Two horizontal forces act on a 2.0 kg puck on frictionless ice: 3.0 N east and 4.0 N north. The magnitude of the puck's acceleration is:

  1. 0.50 m s⁻²
  2. 1.5 m s⁻²
  3. 3.5 m s⁻²
  4. 2.5 m s⁻²

\(F_{\text{net}} = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5.0\ \text{N}\) (at 53° north of east), so \(a = 5.0/2.0 = 2.5\ \text{m s}^{-2}\). Option C adds the forces as if they were in a line.

A block slides down a frictionless slope. As the angle of the slope is increased, the normal force on the block:

  1. increases
  2. stays the same, because the mass has not changed
  3. decreases
  4. becomes equal to \(mg\)

\(N = mg\cos\theta\). As \(\theta\) increases, \(\cos\theta\) decreases, so \(N\) decreases. It equals \(mg\) only on a flat surface (\(\theta = 0\)).

An astronaut far from any planet throws a spanner. After it leaves her hand, the spanner will:

  1. slow down and stop, because no force is pushing it
  2. keep moving in a straight line at constant speed
  3. accelerate, because it was given a force
  4. curve back towards the astronaut

With no net force the spanner keeps its velocity (first law). The force from her hand acted only while she was in contact. Things only “naturally stop” on Earth because friction and drag act.

A 1500 kg car brakes while travelling down a hill inclined at 10° to the horizontal. The braking (friction) force is 6000 N. Ignoring air resistance, calculate the car's deceleration.

Take down the slope as positive. Weight component down the slope: \(mg\sin 10^\circ = 1500 \times 9.8 \times 0.174 = 2.55\times10^3\ \text{N}\).

Braking acts up the slope: \(F_{\text{net}} = 2553 - 6000 = -3447\ \text{N}\).

\(a = \dfrac{-3447}{1500} = -2.3\ \text{m s}^{-2}\), so the car decelerates at 2.3 m s⁻². Common error: forgetting that gravity helps the car down the hill, giving 4.0 m s⁻².

A lift cable snaps and the lift falls freely. Use Newton's second law to explain why a passenger standing on scales in the lift would read zero, even though gravity still acts on them.

In free fall the passenger's acceleration is \(g\) downward. Newton's second law (up positive): \(N - mg = m(-g)\), so \(N = 0\).

Gravity (weight \(mg\)) still acts: it provides all of the passenger's acceleration, so the scales don't need to push. The scales read the normal force, so they read zero. The passenger experiences apparent weightlessness, not an absence of gravity.

A 40 kg crate is pushed up a rough ramp inclined at 20° by a force of 250 N acting parallel to the ramp. Friction on the crate is 60 N.

(a) Calculate the acceleration of the crate. (b) The push is removed while the crate is still moving up the ramp. Calculate its acceleration now.

Weight component down the ramp: \(mg\sin 20^\circ = 40 \times 9.8 \times 0.342 = 134\ \text{N}\).

(a) Moving up the ramp, friction acts down the ramp. \(F_{\text{net}} = 250 - 134 - 60 = 56\ \text{N}\), so \(a = 56/40 = 1.4\ \text{m s}^{-2}\) up the ramp.

(b) Still moving up, so friction still acts down the ramp: \(F_{\text{net}} = -134 - 60 = -194\ \text{N}\), so \(a = -194/40 = -4.9\ \text{m s}^{-2}\), i.e. 4.9 m s⁻² down the ramp (slowing it). Trap: friction opposes the direction of sliding, so it flips only once the crate starts sliding back down.

A student claims: “When I push a heavy box and it doesn't move, it is because the box pushes back on me with an equal force, so the forces cancel.” Evaluate this claim using Newton's laws.

The claim is incorrect. The box pushing back on the student is the third-law partner of the student's push, but it acts on the student, not on the box, so it cannot cancel a force on the box. [1]

The box stays still because the forces on the box are balanced: the student's push is matched by static friction from the floor on the box (first law, \(F_{\text{net}} = 0\)). [1]

Third-law pairs act on different objects and so never cancel; balanced forces act on the same object. [1]

Summary

  • No net force → constant velocity (which includes at rest). Net force → acceleration in the direction of the net force.
  • Third-law pairs act on different objects and never cancel. Weight and normal force are not a pair.
  • On a slope: \(mg\sin\theta\) along, \(mg\cos\theta\) into the slope.
  • Apparent weight = normal force = \(m(g + a)\), with \(a\) positive upward. Direction of motion is irrelevant.
