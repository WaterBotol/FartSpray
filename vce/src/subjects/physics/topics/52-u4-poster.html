What you have to do

Unit 4 Area of Study 2 asks you to design and carry out your own investigation into fields, motion or light, then communicate it in a scientific poster. It draws on everything in the key science skills and data analysis pages.

  • Logbook: you must keep a logbook recording planning, raw data, changes to the method and observations as you go. It authenticates your work; your teacher will check it.
  • Poster: presented using the VCAA poster format. The VCAA template has a word limit (around 600 words); check your school's version for the exact requirements.

The scientific poster, section by section

SectionWhat to includeCommon mistakes
TitleThe question under investigation, stated precisely: “How does the release height of a magnet affect the peak emf induced in a coil?”Vague titles like “Magnets and coils”
Summary statementThe VCAA template also asks for a one-sentence summary of the key finding (a “communication statement”), placed near the topLeaving it out, or restating the aim instead of the finding
IntroductionRelevant physics background (with key equations), the aim, and a hypothesis with a reasonBackground that doesn't connect to the hypothesis
MethodologyThe type of investigation (controlled experiment), variables (IV, DV, controlled), equipment, a concise method, safety and ethical considerations. A labelled diagram or photo saves words.Recipe-style steps with no justification; missing controlled variables
ResultsProcessed data in a clear table and a properly drawn graph (linearised if appropriate) with error bars, gradient and unitsRaw data only; dot-to-dot graphs; no units or uncertainties
DiscussionInterpret the trend with physics; compare with theory or accepted values (% difference); evaluate errors and uncertainty, validity and limitations; suggest specific improvements and further questions“Human error”; claiming the hypothesis is “proven”; ignoring outliers
ConclusionAnswer the question in the title directly, state whether the data supports the hypothesis, and give key valuesIntroducing new information
References and acknowledgementsSources used (consistent referencing style) and people who helpedMissing entirely

Data supports or does not support a hypothesis. It never “proves” it. Refer to the evidence: “The linear relationship between \(v^2\) and \(h\) (gradient 18.9 m s⁻², 3.6% below the theoretical 19.6 m s⁻²) supports the hypothesis.”

Plan an investigation into how the release height of a trolley on a ramp affects its speed at the bottom. Include the hypothesis, variables, method outline, how the data will be analysed, one safety consideration and the main sources of error.

A good investigation tests a physics prediction: here energy conservation predicts \(v^2 = 2gh\), so plotting \(v^2\) against \(h\) should give a straight line of gradient \(2g\). The plan identifies one continuous independent variable, controls everything else, and links each error to its effect on the graph.

Hypothesis: “If the release height increases, then the speed at the bottom will increase, with \(v^2\) proportional to \(h\), because gravitational potential energy \(mgh\) is converted to kinetic energy \(\tfrac12 mv^2\).”

Variables: IV = release height (5 values, 0.10–0.50 m, measured vertically with a metre ruler). DV = speed at the bottom (light gate and card of known length). Controlled: same trolley and card, same ramp and surface, same light-gate position, trolley released from rest (not pushed).

Method outline: for each height, release the trolley from rest five times and record the light-gate time; calculate speed = card length ÷ time; average.

Analysis: plot \(v^2\) against \(h\) with error bars; the gradient should be \(2g = 19.6\ \text{m s}^{-2}\) if no energy is lost. Compare the measured gradient and calculate the percentage of energy lost.

Safety: place a stopper or cushion at the end of the ramp so the trolley doesn't fall; keep feet clear.

Errors: friction and air resistance (systematic, gradient lower than 19.6 m s⁻²); measuring the height to the wrong point on the trolley (systematic, creates an intercept); small variations in release (random, reduced by repeats).

A controlled experiment with a linearised graph whose gradient tests the physics. This structure maps directly onto the poster sections.

Writing a strong discussion

  1. Describe the trend in the processed data (linear? through the origin?).
  2. Explain it with physics (the relevant equation and why it predicts this shape).
  3. Compare with theory: percentage difference between your gradient and the theoretical value.
  4. Evaluate the data: precision (spread of repeats, size of error bars), accuracy (agreement with theory), specific random and systematic errors and their direction of effect, outliers.
  5. Limitations and validity: were all relevant variables controlled? Does the range of data allow a general conclusion?
  6. Improvements and extensions: specific, practical changes (light gates instead of stopwatches, more heights across a wider range) and a follow-up question.

“Do more trials” only helps with random error. If your main problem was friction (systematic), say how to reduce it (an air track) or account for it (measure the energy lost). Generic improvements with no link to a specific weakness earn little credit.

Practice

The main purpose of the logbook in the practical investigation is to:

  1. replace the poster
  2. record and authenticate planning, raw data and observations as the investigation proceeds
  3. store references only
  4. record the final conclusion

The logbook is the ongoing, dated record that shows the work is genuinely yours and documents raw data and changes.

Which statement is most appropriate for the conclusion of a scientific poster?

  1. “The experiment proved that \(v^2 = 2gh\).”
  2. “There was a lot of human error, so the results are unreliable.”
  3. “The results were good.”
  4. “The linear relationship between \(v^2\) and \(h\) supports the hypothesis, although the gradient was 4% below the theoretical value.”

Evidence-based, uses “supports” rather than “proves”, and quantifies the agreement with theory.

In the poster, where should the variables, equipment and safety considerations appear?

  1. Methodology
  2. Introduction
  3. Discussion
  4. Conclusion

The methodology section describes how the investigation was designed and conducted, including variables and risk management.

A student investigates how the angle of a projectile launcher affects the horizontal range. Their results peak at 40° rather than the predicted 45°. Suggest two physics reasons for this, and one improvement to the method.

Air resistance reduces horizontal velocity during flight and lowers the optimal angle below 45°. [1]

If the launcher releases the ball above the landing surface (launch height above floor level), the optimum angle is also less than 45°. [1]

Other acceptable points: inconsistent launch speed at different angles (the spring does different amounts of work), or uncertainty in reading the angle. [1]

Improvement: launch from floor level (or account for the launch height in the theory), use a denser ball to reduce the relative effect of air resistance, and measure the launch speed with a light gate at each angle to confirm it is constant. [1]

Summary

  • Investigate fields, motion or light; keep a logbook; present a VCAA-format poster.
  • Sections: title (question), summary statement, introduction (aim, hypothesis), methodology, results, discussion, conclusion, references.
  • Discussion: trend → physics → compare with theory → evaluate errors → limitations → specific improvements.
  • Data supports (not proves) a hypothesis.
