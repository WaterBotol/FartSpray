Describing a sinusoidal voltage

QuantityMeaningRelationship
Period \(T\)Time for one complete cycle (s)\(T = 1/f\)
Frequency \(f\)Cycles per second (Hz); equals the generator's rotation rate\(f = 1/T\)
Peak (amplitude) \(V_p\)Maximum value measured from zero
Peak-to-peak \(V_{p\text{-}p}\)From the highest point to the lowest point\(V_{p\text{-}p} = 2V_p\)
RMS \(V_{\text{rms}}\)The DC voltage that would deliver the same average power to a resistor\(V_{\text{rms}} = V_p/\sqrt2\)

Currents are described the same way: \(I_{p\text{-}p} = 2I_p\) and \(I_{\text{rms}} = I_p/\sqrt2\).

Why RMS?

The average of a sine-wave voltage over a whole cycle is zero (as much positive as negative), yet AC clearly heats a kettle. Power in a resistor depends on \(V^2\) (or \(I^2\)), which is always positive. The root-mean-square value takes the square of the voltage, finds its mean over a cycle, then takes the square root. For a sine wave that works out to \(V_p/\sqrt2\).

RMS values and power
\[ V_{\text{rms}} = \frac{V_p}{\sqrt2} \qquad I_{\text{rms}} = \frac{I_p}{\sqrt2} \]\[ P_{\text{avg}} = V_{\text{rms}}I_{\text{rms}} = I_{\text{rms}}^2R = \frac{V_{\text{rms}}^2}{R} \]

A 10 V rms AC supply and a 10 V battery deliver the same average power to the same resistor. Household voltages, appliance ratings and meter readings are all RMS unless stated otherwise.

Australian mains electricity

Mains power in Australia is 230 V rms at 50 Hz. So:

\[ V_p = 230\sqrt2 = 325\ \text{V} \qquad V_{p\text{-}p} = 650\ \text{V} \qquad T = \frac{1}{50} = 0.020\ \text{s} = 20\ \text{ms} \]

A 2400 W kettle runs from 230 V rms mains. Find (a) the rms current, (b) the kettle's resistance, (c) the peak current and (d) the peak-to-peak current.

RMS values are the equivalent DC values that deliver the same power, so power calculations use RMS directly. Peak and peak-to-peak values describe the actual sinusoid.

(a) \(I_{\text{rms}} = P/V_{\text{rms}} = 2400/230 = 10.4\ \text{A}\).

(b) \(R = V_{\text{rms}}/I_{\text{rms}} = 230/10.43 = 22.0\ \Omega\).

(c) \(I_p = \sqrt2 \times 10.43 = 14.8\ \text{A}\).

(d) \(I_{p\text{-}p} = 2 \times 14.8 = 29.5\ \text{A}\).

(a) 10 A (b) 22 Ω (c) 15 A (d) 30 A. Power ratings use RMS, so \(P = V_{\text{rms}}I_{\text{rms}}\) needs no √2.

\(P = V_pI_p\) gives twice the true average power. If you're given peak values, convert to RMS first. Also check what a question asks for: “peak-to-peak” is double the peak, and students regularly halve or double the wrong thing.

Reading an oscilloscope (CRO) trace

An oscilloscope shows voltage (vertical) against time (horizontal) on a grid. The vertical sensitivity (volts per division) and time base (time per division) convert squares into values.

An oscilloscope is set to 5 V/div and 2 ms/div. A sine wave on the screen rises 4.0 divisions above the centre line, and one complete cycle spans 4.0 divisions horizontally. Find \(V_p\), \(V_{p\text{-}p}\), \(V_{\text{rms}}\) and the frequency.

On an oscilloscope, vertical divisions × volts/div give voltage and horizontal divisions × time/div give time. Read peak from the centre line, period from one full cycle.

Peak: \(4.0\ \text{div} \times 5\ \text{V/div} = 20\ \text{V}\). Peak-to-peak: 40 V.

RMS: \(20/\sqrt2 = 14.1\ \text{V}\).

Period: \(4.0\ \text{div} \times 2\ \text{ms/div} = 8.0\ \text{ms}\); frequency \(= 1/0.0080 = 125\ \text{Hz}\).

\(V_p = 20\ \text{V}\), \(V_{p\text{-}p} = 40\ \text{V}\), \(V_{\text{rms}} = 14\ \text{V}\), \(f = 125\ \text{Hz}\).

Practice

An AC supply of peak voltage 20 V is connected across a 10 Ω resistor. The average power dissipated is:

  1. 40 W
  2. 20 W
  3. 28 W
  4. 14 W

\(V_{\text{rms}} = 20/\sqrt2 = 14.1\ \text{V}\); \(P = V_{\text{rms}}^2/R = 200/10 = 20\ \text{W}\). Using the peak gives 40 W, double the true value.

Australian mains supply is 230 V rms. Its peak-to-peak voltage is closest to:

  1. 230 V
  2. 325 V
  3. 460 V
  4. 650 V

\(V_p = 230\sqrt2 = 325\ \text{V}\), so \(V_{p\text{-}p} = 650\ \text{V}\).

The DC voltage that would deliver the same power to a resistor as a 12 V rms AC supply is:

  1. 12 V
  2. 17 V
  3. 8.5 V
  4. 24 V

That's the definition of RMS: 12 V rms AC and 12 V DC give the same power.

The average value of a sinusoidal AC voltage over one complete cycle is:

  1. \(V_p/\sqrt2\)
  2. \(V_p\)
  3. zero
  4. \(2V_p\)

The positive and negative halves cancel, so the average is zero. That's exactly why RMS is used to describe AC.

A generator's output has a peak-to-peak voltage of 170 V and a period of 40 ms. Find its peak voltage, RMS voltage and frequency.

\(V_p = 170/2 = 85\ \text{V}\); \(V_{\text{rms}} = 85/\sqrt2 = 60\ \text{V}\); \(f = 1/0.040 = 25\ \text{Hz}\).

A heater is rated at 1500 W when connected to 230 V rms. What power would it deliver if connected to a 230 V DC supply, and to an AC supply with a peak of 230 V?

230 V DC delivers the same power as 230 V rms: 1500 W.

With \(V_p = 230\ \text{V}\), \(V_{\text{rms}} = 163\ \text{V}\). Power ∝ \(V^2\): \(P = 1500 \times (163/230)^2 = 1500 \times \tfrac12 = 750\ \text{W}\).

Summary

  • \(T = 1/f\); \(V_{p\text{-}p} = 2V_p\); \(V_{\text{rms}} = V_p/\sqrt2\).
  • RMS AC and the same DC voltage give the same power. Use RMS in \(P = VI\).
  • Australian mains: 230 V rms, 325 V peak, 650 V peak-to-peak, 50 Hz.
  • Oscilloscope: divisions × V/div for voltage, divisions × time/div for time.
