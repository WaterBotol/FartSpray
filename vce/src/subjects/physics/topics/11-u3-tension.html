What tension is

Tension is the pulling force a string, rope, cable, chain or tow bar exerts on whatever it is attached to. In VCE the string is usually ideal: massless and inextensible. That gives you three facts to use:

  • The tension is the same at every point along one string, even around an ideal (frictionless, massless) pulley.
  • Objects joined by a taut string have the same magnitude of acceleration.
  • A string can only pull, never push. If the maths gives a negative tension, the string has gone slack.

Two people pull on opposite ends of a rope, each with 200 N. The tension is 200 N, not 400 N and not zero. Each end of the rope pulls on each person with 200 N. The same idea applies to a spring balance with a 2.0 kg mass hanging from each end over two pulleys: it reads 19.6 N (it shows “2 kg”), exactly as if one end were tied to a wall.

The two methods for connected bodies

Every connected-bodies question can be done with two steps. Learn this routine and they become easy marks.

Step 1: whole system (tension is internal and disappears)
\[ a = \frac{\text{net external force driving the system}}{\text{total mass}} \]
Step 2: isolate one object (tension is now external)
\[ F_{\text{net on that object}} = m_{\text{object}}\, a \;\Rightarrow\; \text{solve for } T \]

In step 2, pick the object with the fewest forces on it. For a train that is usually the last carriage.

Car towing a trailer (a straight line)

A 1200 kg car tows a 400 kg trailer along a level road. The driving force on the car is 2400 N. Resistive forces are 300 N on the car and 100 N on the trailer.

(a) Find the acceleration. (b) Find the tension in the tow bar.

Whole system. Tension is internal. External horizontal forces: driving force forward, both resistances backward.

\[ a = \frac{2400 - 300 - 100}{1200 + 400} = \frac{2000}{1600} = 1.25\ \text{m s}^{-2} \]

Isolate the trailer. Its horizontal forces are tension \(T\) forward and 100 N resistance backward.

\[ T - 100 = 400(1.25) \Rightarrow T = 600\ \text{N} \]

Check using the car. Forces on the car: 2400 N forward, 300 N back, and the tow bar pulls it back with 600 N (third law).

\[ 2400 - 300 - 600 = 1500 = 1200(1.25)\ ✓ \]

(a) 1.25 m s⁻² (b) 600 N.

In a train pulled from the front, the tension is largest in the coupling nearest the engine and smallest at the back, because each coupling has to accelerate everything behind it. With \(n\) identical carriages behind a coupling (and no friction), \(T = n\,m_{\text{carriage}}\,a\).

Pulley systems

Mass on a table pulled by a hanging mass

Block A (3.0 kg) sits on a frictionless table. A light string runs from A over an ideal pulley to block B (2.0 kg), which hangs freely. The system is released from rest.

(a) Find the acceleration. (b) Find the tension in the string.

Whole system. The only external force that drives the motion is B's weight (A's weight is balanced by the table's normal force).

\[ a = \frac{m_B g}{m_A + m_B} = \frac{2.0 \times 9.8}{5.0} = 3.92\ \text{m s}^{-2} \]

Isolate A (only one horizontal force: tension).

\[ T = m_A a = 3.0 \times 3.92 = 11.8\ \text{N} \]

Check with B (down positive).

\[ m_B g - T = 19.6 - 11.8 = 7.8 = 2.0 \times 3.92\ ✓ \]

(a) 3.9 m s⁻² (b) 12 N. The tension is less than B's weight (19.6 N): if it were equal, B would not accelerate.

While the system accelerates, \(T < m_B g\). Tension equals the hanging weight only if the system is at rest or moving at constant velocity. This is one of the most common errors in the whole course.

Atwood machine (two hanging masses)

Atwood machine
\[ a = \frac{(m_1 - m_2)\,g}{m_1 + m_2}, \qquad T = m_2(g + a) = m_1(g - a) \]

Don't memorise these. Derive them with the two-step method every time; examiners change the set-up.

A 5.0 kg mass and a 3.0 kg mass hang on either side of an ideal pulley and are released from rest. Find the acceleration, the tension, and the speed of the masses after they have moved 1.0 m.

Whole system. Take “5.0 kg side down” as positive. The weights oppose each other.

\[ a = \frac{(5.0 - 3.0)(9.8)}{5.0 + 3.0} = \frac{19.6}{8.0} = 2.45\ \text{m s}^{-2} \]

Isolate the 3.0 kg mass (accelerating upward):

\[ T - 29.4 = 3.0(2.45) \Rightarrow T = 36.75\ \text{N} \]

Check with the 5.0 kg mass: \(49 - 36.75 = 12.25 = 5.0 \times 2.45\) ✓.

Speed after 1.0 m (constant acceleration from rest):

\[ v^2 = u^2 + 2as = 0 + 2(2.45)(1.0) \Rightarrow v = 2.2\ \text{m s}^{-1} \]

\(a = 2.5\ \text{m s}^{-2}\), \(T = 37\ \text{N}\), \(v = 2.2\ \text{m s}^{-1}\). Notice \(29.4 < T < 49\): tension sits between the two weights.

Mass on a slope pulled by a hanging mass

A 4.0 kg block sits on a frictionless slope inclined at 30°. A string parallel to the slope runs over an ideal pulley at the top of the slope to a 3.0 kg mass hanging vertically. Which way does the system move, and what are the acceleration and tension?

Compare the two forces that drive the motion.

\[ \text{hanging weight} = 3.0 \times 9.8 = 29.4\ \text{N} \]\[ \text{block's weight down the slope} = 4.0 \times 9.8 \times \sin 30^\circ = 19.6\ \text{N} \]

The hanging weight wins, so the hanging mass falls and the block moves up the slope.

Whole system.

\[ a = \frac{29.4 - 19.6}{4.0 + 3.0} = \frac{9.8}{7.0} = 1.4\ \text{m s}^{-2} \]

Isolate the hanging mass (down positive):

\[ 29.4 - T = 3.0(1.4) \Rightarrow T = 25.2\ \text{N} \]

Check the block: \(T - 19.6 = 5.6 = 4.0 \times 1.4\) ✓.

The block moves up the slope with \(a = 1.4\ \text{m s}^{-2}\); \(T = 25\ \text{N}\). Always compare the driving forces first so your signs are right.

Objects pushed in contact

Blocks pushed side by side behave like a train, except the link is a contact (normal) force instead of tension.

A 4.0 kg block and a 2.0 kg block sit side by side on a frictionless floor. A 30 N horizontal force pushes the 4.0 kg block, which pushes the 2.0 kg block. Find the contact force between them. What changes if the 30 N force pushes the 2.0 kg block instead?

Whole system: \(a = 30/6.0 = 5.0\ \text{m s}^{-2}\).

Isolate the front block (2.0 kg). The only horizontal force on it is the contact force: \(F_c = 2.0 \times 5.0 = 10\ \text{N}\).

Reversed. Now the front block is the 4.0 kg one: \(F_c = 4.0 \times 5.0 = 20\ \text{N}\).

10 N, or 20 N if pushed from the other side. The contact force equals whatever is needed to accelerate the block(s) in front.

Tension in two dimensions: hanging objects

When an object hangs from two ropes at angles, it is in equilibrium: the vector sum of the forces on the knot is zero. Resolve every tension into horizontal and vertical components.

A 20 kg sign hangs from two ropes, each at 30° to the horizontal ceiling. Find the tension in each rope. What would it be if each rope were at 10° to the horizontal?

Vertical equilibrium. By symmetry both tensions are equal. Their vertical components hold up the weight:

\[ 2T\sin 30^\circ = mg \Rightarrow T = \frac{20 \times 9.8}{2 \times 0.5} = 196\ \text{N} \]

At 10°:

\[ T = \frac{196}{2\sin 10^\circ} = \frac{196}{0.347} = 564\ \text{N} \]

196 N each (equal to the whole weight!), rising to 564 N at 10°. The flatter the ropes, the larger the tension. A perfectly horizontal rope would need infinite tension to support any weight.

A 10 kg mass hangs from two ropes. Rope 1 makes 30° with the horizontal and rope 2 makes 60° with the horizontal. Find both tensions.

Horizontal components cancel:

\[ T_1\cos 30^\circ = T_2\cos 60^\circ \Rightarrow T_2 = T_1\frac{\cos 30^\circ}{\cos 60^\circ} = 1.732\,T_1 \]

Vertical components support the weight:

\[ T_1\sin 30^\circ + T_2\sin 60^\circ = 98 \]\[ 0.5\,T_1 + (1.732)(0.866)\,T_1 = 0.5\,T_1 + 1.5\,T_1 = 2.0\,T_1 = 98 \]

Solve: \(T_1 = 49\ \text{N}\) and \(T_2 = 1.732 \times 49 = 84.9\ \text{N}\).

\(T_1 = 49\ \text{N}\) (30° rope), \(T_2 = 85\ \text{N}\) (60° rope). The steeper rope carries more of the load.

Tension in accelerating vertical cables

A cable holding a load in a lift or crane works exactly like the normal force in a lift:

\[ T - mg = ma \Rightarrow T = m(g + a) \quad (\text{up positive}) \]

A crane lifting an 800 kg load with an upward acceleration of 0.50 m s⁻² needs \(T = 800(9.8 + 0.50) = 8.24\times10^3\ \text{N}\). Lowering the same load while slowing it (acceleration upward) needs exactly the same tension.

Practice

Two students have a tug of war. Each pulls on the rope with a force of 150 N and nobody moves. The tension in the rope is:

  1. 0 N
  2. 150 N
  3. 300 N
  4. 75 N

The rope pulls on each student with 150 N, so the tension is 150 N. It is the same as tying one end to a tree and pulling with 150 N. The forces on the rope balance, which is why it doesn't accelerate, but the tension is not zero.

A 2.0 kg mass hangs from each end of a string that passes over two frictionless pulleys. A spring balance is spliced into the middle of the string. The balance reads:

  1. 0 N
  2. 39.2 N
  3. 19.6 N
  4. 9.8 N

The system is in equilibrium, so the tension equals the weight on either side: 19.6 N. The second mass does the same job a wall hook would do. Doubling to 39.2 N is the classic mistake.

An engine pulls three identical carriages along a straight frictionless track with constant acceleration. Which coupling has the greatest tension?

  1. between the engine and the first carriage
  2. between the first and second carriages
  3. between the second and third carriages
  4. all couplings have the same tension

The coupling behind the engine must accelerate all three carriages (\(T = 3ma\)). The next accelerates two (\(2ma\)), the last one (\(ma\)).

Block A on a frictionless table is connected over a pulley to hanging block B, and the system accelerates. The tension in the string is:

  1. equal to the weight of B
  2. greater than the weight of B
  3. equal to the weight of A
  4. less than the weight of B

B accelerates downward, so the net force on B is downward: \(m_B g - T = m_B a > 0\), so \(T < m_B g\).

A lift is moving upward and slowing down. Compared with the weight of the lift, the tension in the lift cable is:

  1. greater
  2. smaller
  3. equal
  4. zero

Moving up and slowing means acceleration is downward, so \(T = m(g + a)\) with \(a < 0\), giving \(T < mg\).

A picture is hung from a nail by a wire. Which arrangement gives the smallest tension in the wire?

  1. Wire nearly horizontal, making 5° with the horizontal on each side
  2. Wire at 30° to the horizontal on each side
  3. Wire at 70° to the horizontal on each side
  4. The tension is the same in all cases

\(2T\sin\theta = mg\), so \(T = mg/(2\sin\theta)\). The larger the angle to the horizontal (the more vertical the wire), the smaller the tension.

Three blocks of mass 4.0 kg, 3.0 kg and 2.0 kg sit in a line on a frictionless surface, joined by light strings. A 36 N force pulls the 4.0 kg block horizontally, away from the others. Find the tension in each string.

System: \(a = 36/9.0 = 4.0\ \text{m s}^{-2}\).

String between the 4.0 kg and 3.0 kg blocks accelerates the 3.0 kg and 2.0 kg blocks: \(T_1 = (3.0 + 2.0)(4.0) = 20\ \text{N}\).

String between the 3.0 kg and 2.0 kg blocks accelerates only the 2.0 kg block: \(T_2 = 2.0 \times 4.0 = 8.0\ \text{N}\).

A 5.0 kg block on a rough horizontal table is connected over an ideal pulley to a hanging 2.0 kg mass. Friction on the block is 10 N. Calculate the acceleration of the system and the tension in the string.

System: driving force is the hanging weight, 19.6 N. Friction opposes the motion.

\(a = \dfrac{19.6 - 10}{5.0 + 2.0} = \dfrac{9.6}{7.0} = 1.37\ \text{m s}^{-2}\)

Hanging mass (down positive): \(19.6 - T = 2.0(1.37) \Rightarrow T = 16.9\ \text{N}\).

Check the block: \(T - 10 = 6.9 \approx 5.0 \times 1.37\) ✓.

In an Atwood machine, a 3.0 kg and a 5.0 kg mass are released from rest. After 1.5 s the string is cut. Describe the motion of the 3.0 kg mass immediately after the cut, including its velocity and acceleration.

Before the cut: \(a = 2.45\ \text{m s}^{-2}\) (3.0 kg mass upward), so after 1.5 s: \(v = 2.45 \times 1.5 = 3.7\ \text{m s}^{-1}\) upward.

After the cut, the tension vanishes and the only force is weight, so the acceleration is 9.8 m s⁻² downward. But velocity cannot change instantly: the mass is still moving upward at 3.7 m s⁻¹. It rises further, stops, then falls. Students often say it “immediately falls”; it doesn't.

A 60 kg climber hangs at rest from a rope tied between two anchor points at the same height. The rope sags so that each side makes an angle of 15° with the horizontal. The rope's breaking strength is 1500 N.

(a) Calculate the tension in the rope. (b) Will the rope hold? (c) Explain why pulling the rope tighter (less sag) makes it more likely to break.

(a) \(2T\sin 15^\circ = mg \Rightarrow T = \dfrac{60 \times 9.8}{2 \times 0.259} = 1.14\times10^3\ \text{N}\). [2]

(b) Yes, 1140 N is below the 1500 N breaking strength (just). [1]

(c) Less sag means a smaller angle to the horizontal, so \(\sin\theta\) is smaller and each side's tension must be larger for the vertical components to support the same weight. [1]

Summary

  • Step 1: treat the whole system to find \(a\) (tension cancels). Step 2: isolate one body to find \(T\).
  • An accelerating hanging mass has \(T \neq mg\). Tension equals the weight only at rest or constant velocity.
  • Couplings nearest the pulling force carry the most tension.
  • For angled ropes: horizontal components cancel, vertical components support the weight. Flatter ropes → bigger tension.
