The experiment

In 1801 Thomas Young shone light through two narrow, closely spaced slits onto a screen. Instead of two bright strips (what a stream of particles would give), he saw a row of evenly spaced bright and dark bands (fringes). This interference pattern is strong evidence that light behaves as a wave.

Superposition and path difference

Light from the two slits spreads out (diffracts) and overlaps. Where two waves meet, their displacements add (superposition):

  • Constructive interference (bright band): the waves arrive in phase, crest on crest.
  • Destructive interference (dark band): the waves arrive exactly out of phase, crest on trough, and cancel.

Whether they arrive in phase depends on the path difference: how much further the light from one slit travels than the light from the other.

Path difference conditions
\[ \text{bright: } \ S_2P - S_1P = n\lambda \quad (n = 0, 1, 2, \ldots) \]\[ \text{dark: } \ S_2P - S_1P = \left(n - \tfrac12\right)\lambda \quad (n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots) \]

The central band has path difference zero and is usually not counted as the “first” bright band. The third dark band from the centre has a path difference of \(2.5\lambda\), not \(3\lambda\) or \(3.5\lambda\). Draw the table above in the margin before answering.

A stable pattern needs coherent sources: same frequency and a constant phase relationship. Young used one source and split it with the two slits (or passed sunlight through a single slit first). Today we use a laser. Two separate light bulbs never give stable fringes.

Fringe spacing

Fringe spacing (when \(L \gg d\))
\[ \Delta x = \frac{\lambda L}{d} \]

\(\Delta x\) = distance between adjacent bright bands (or adjacent dark bands); \(\lambda\) = wavelength; \(L\) = slit-to-screen distance; \(d\) = slit separation (centre to centre). Use the same units for everything (metres).

ChangeEffect on fringe spacing
Longer wavelength (e.g. red instead of blue)Wider fringes
Screen further away (larger \(L\))Wider fringes
Slits closer together (smaller \(d\))Wider fringes
Brighter light (more intense)Brighter fringes, same spacing
White lightWhite central band; coloured fringes beyond, violet/blue on the inner edge and red on the outer edge (red has the longest λ so spreads furthest)

Light of wavelength 600 nm passes through two slits 0.20 mm apart onto a screen 2.0 m away. Find the spacing between adjacent bright bands.

\[ \Delta x = \frac{\lambda L}{d} = \frac{(600\times10^{-9})(2.0)}{0.20\times10^{-3}} = 6.0\times10^{-3}\ \text{m} \]

6.0 mm. Convert nm (×10⁻⁹) and mm (×10⁻³) before substituting.

Students shine a laser through slits 0.25 mm apart onto a wall 1.20 m away. They measure the distance across 10 fringe spacings as 27 mm. Find the laser's wavelength.

One fringe spacing: \(\Delta x = 27/10 = 2.7\ \text{mm}\). (Measuring across many fringes and dividing reduces the uncertainty.)

\[ \lambda = \frac{\Delta x\, d}{L} = \frac{(2.7\times10^{-3})(0.25\times10^{-3})}{1.20} = 5.6\times10^{-7}\ \text{m} \]

563 nm (green-yellow).

In a double slit experiment with 500 nm light, point Q on the screen is 1250 nm further from one slit than the other. Is Q bright or dark, and which band is it? What happens at Q if the wavelength is changed to 625 nm?

Path difference in wavelengths: \(1250/500 = 2.5\lambda\). A half-number, so destructive: dark.

Which dark band? \((n - \tfrac12) = 2.5 \Rightarrow n = 3\): the third dark band from the centre.

With 625 nm: \(1250/625 = 2.0\lambda\), a whole number, so Q becomes the second bright band.

Third dark band; with 625 nm it becomes the second bright band.

Why this is evidence for waves

  • A particle model (Newton's “corpuscles”) predicts two bright strips behind the two slits, not a pattern of many fringes.
  • Dark bands appear where light from both slits arrives. Adding more light and getting darkness can only be explained by waves cancelling.
  • The spacing depends on \(\lambda\) exactly as the wave model predicts.

Later experiments with very dim light showed that the pattern builds up one photon at a time (see Wave–particle duality), which is where the story gets strange.

Practice

In a double slit pattern, the path difference to the second dark band on one side of the centre is:

  1. \(2\lambda\)
  2. \(1.5\lambda\)
  3. \(2.5\lambda\)
  4. \(0.5\lambda\)

Dark bands are at \((n - \tfrac12)\lambda\): first 0.5λ, second 1.5λ, third 2.5λ.

Which change would make the fringes in a double slit pattern further apart?

  1. moving the screen closer to the slits
  2. using blue light instead of red light
  3. moving the slits further apart
  4. moving the slits closer together

\(\Delta x = \lambda L/d\): smaller \(d\) → larger \(\Delta x\). The other options all decrease the spacing.

The intensity of the laser in a double slit experiment is doubled. The fringe spacing:

  1. stays the same
  2. doubles
  3. halves
  4. increases by √2

Intensity doesn't appear in \(\Delta x = \lambda L/d\). The bright fringes just get brighter.

Young's double slit experiment supports the wave model of light because:

  1. light travels in straight lines through the slits
  2. the slits reflect light onto the screen
  3. the pattern of bright and dark bands can only be explained by interference of waves
  4. light is shown to consist of photons

Interference (especially dark bands where light from both slits meets) is a wave property that a classical particle model can't explain.

A double slit experiment is done with white light. The first bright band beside the central band appears:

  1. white
  2. as a spectrum with violet closest to the centre
  3. as a spectrum with red closest to the centre
  4. as a single red band

Each colour's fringe spacing is proportional to its wavelength. Violet (shortest λ) forms its first fringe closest to the centre, red furthest out. Only the central band, where every colour has zero path difference, is white.

A 650 nm laser gives a fringe spacing of 3.9 mm on a screen 1.5 m from a double slit. Find the slit separation.

\(d = \dfrac{\lambda L}{\Delta x} = \dfrac{(650\times10^{-9})(1.5)}{3.9\times10^{-3}} = 2.5\times10^{-4}\ \text{m} = 0.25\ \text{mm}\).

In a double slit experiment, the centre of the 4th bright band beside the central maximum is 12 mm from the centre of the central maximum. The slits are 0.30 mm apart and the screen is 1.6 m away. Find the wavelength.

From the central maximum to the 4th bright band is 4 fringe spacings: \(\Delta x = 12/4 = 3.0\ \text{mm}\).

\(\lambda = \dfrac{\Delta x\,d}{L} = \dfrac{(3.0\times10^{-3})(3.0\times10^{-4})}{1.6} = 5.6\times10^{-7}\ \text{m}\) (563 nm).

Explain the formation of a dark band in Young's double slit experiment, with reference to path difference.

Light from the two slits is coherent and spreads out (diffracts), so waves from both slits reach every point on the screen. [1]

At a dark band, the path difference from the two slits is a half-number of wavelengths, \((n - \tfrac12)\lambda\), so the waves arrive exactly out of phase (crest meets trough). [1]

They interfere destructively and cancel, giving zero (minimum) intensity. [1]

Summary

  • Bright: path difference \(n\lambda\). Dark: \((n - \tfrac12)\lambda\). Central band: zero.
  • \(\Delta x = \lambda L/d\): bigger λ or L, smaller d → wider fringes. Intensity has no effect on spacing.
  • Needs coherent light. The pattern is evidence for the wave model.
