Describing uniform circular motion

An object in uniform circular motion travels around a circle at constant speed. Its velocity is not constant, because the direction keeps changing. A changing velocity means an acceleration, and by Newton's second law there must be a net force.

Describing the motion
\[ v = \frac{2\pi r}{T}, \qquad f = \frac{1}{T} \]

\(T\) is the period (time for one revolution, s), \(f\) the frequency (revolutions per second, Hz), \(r\) the radius (m) and \(v\) the speed (m s⁻¹).

Centripetal acceleration and net force

Centripetal acceleration and force (towards the centre)
\[ a_c = \frac{v^2}{r} = \frac{4\pi^2 r}{T^2} \qquad\qquad F_{\text{net}} = \frac{mv^2}{r} = \frac{4\pi^2 m r}{T^2} \]

“Centripetal” just means centre-seeking. The centripetal force is the net force, supplied by real forces such as tension, friction, a normal force or gravity.

SituationWhat provides the net (centripetal) force
Ball on a string, horizontal circle on a tableTension
Conical pendulum (mass on a string swinging in a horizontal circle)Horizontal component of tension
Car on a flat curveSideways friction from the road on the tyres
Car on a banked track at the design speedHorizontal component of the normal force
Satellite or moon in orbitGravity
Charged particle in a magnetic fieldMagnetic force

Never draw a centripetal force or a centrifugal (outward) force on a free-body diagram. When a car turns left, you feel “thrown” to the right because your body tends to keep going in a straight line (inertia) while the car turns under you. The door then pushes you inward. If a whirling ball's string snaps, the ball flies off along the tangent, not radially outward.

From \(F = mv^2/r\): double the speed → 4 × the force. From \(F = 4\pi^2 mr/T^2\): halve the period → 4 × the force. At constant speed, double the radius → half the force; at constant period, double the radius → double the force. Multiple-choice questions test these ratios constantly.

An object on the end of a string

A 0.50 kg ball on a 0.80 m string is whirled in a horizontal circle on a frictionless table at 2.0 revolutions per second. Calculate the tension in the string.

Period from frequency: \(T = 1/f = 1/2.0 = 0.50\ \text{s}\).

Speed:

\[ v = \frac{2\pi r}{T} = \frac{2\pi(0.80)}{0.50} = 10.05\ \text{m s}^{-1} \]

Tension provides the net force:

\[ T_{\text{string}} = \frac{mv^2}{r} = \frac{0.50 \times 10.05^2}{0.80} = 63.2\ \text{N} \]

63 N. (Using symbol \(T\) for both period and tension is a classic way to confuse yourself: label them clearly.)

Conical pendulum

A mass on a string swinging in a horizontal circle below the pivot is a conical pendulum. The string sweeps out a cone. The tension has a vertical component that balances weight and a horizontal component that provides the centripetal force.

\[ T\cos\theta = mg, \qquad T\sin\theta = \frac{mv^2}{r} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \tan\theta = \frac{v^2}{rg} \]

A 0.20 kg mass on a 1.0 m string moves in a horizontal circle so that the string makes 30° with the vertical. Find (a) the tension, (b) the speed and (c) the period.

(a) Vertical equilibrium:

\[ T\cos 30^\circ = mg \Rightarrow T = \frac{0.20 \times 9.8}{0.866} = 2.26\ \text{N} \]

Radius of the circle (not the string length!): \(r = L\sin 30^\circ = 1.0 \times 0.5 = 0.50\ \text{m}\).

(b) Speed from \(\tan\theta = v^2/(rg)\):

\[ v = \sqrt{rg\tan\theta} = \sqrt{0.50 \times 9.8 \times \tan 30^\circ} = 1.68\ \text{m s}^{-1} \]

(c) Period:

\[ T_{\text{period}} = \frac{2\pi r}{v} = \frac{2\pi(0.50)}{1.68} = 1.87\ \text{s} \]

(a) 2.3 N (b) 1.7 m s⁻¹ (c) 1.9 s. Using the string length as the radius is the trap here.

A vehicle on a flat circular road

On a flat road, the only horizontal force that can turn a car is sideways friction between the tyres and the road. The friction needed is \(F_f = mv^2/r\). If the road can't supply that much (ice, oil, too fast), the car can't follow the curve and slides towards the outside of the bend.

A 1200 kg car travels around a flat curve of radius 50 m at 15 m s⁻¹. What friction force must the road provide? What happens to this force if the speed doubles?

\[ F_f = \frac{mv^2}{r} = \frac{1200 \times 15^2}{50} = 5.4\times10^3\ \text{N} \]

Doubling \(v\) multiplies \(v^2\) by 4: the friction needed becomes \(2.16\times10^4\ \text{N}\).

5400 N towards the centre; four times as much at double the speed. This is why curves have much lower speed limits in the wet.

Banked tracks

Tilting the road towards the centre of the curve lets the normal force help. At one particular speed, the design speed, the horizontal component of the normal force supplies all of the centripetal force and no sideways friction is needed at all.

Banked track at the design speed (no friction)
\[ N\cos\theta = mg \qquad N\sin\theta = \frac{mv^2}{r} \]\[ \Rightarrow\quad \tan\theta = \frac{v^2}{rg}, \qquad v_{\text{design}} = \sqrt{rg\tan\theta}, \qquad N = \frac{mg}{\cos\theta} \]

Mass cancels: every vehicle has the same design speed on a given curve.

On a banked track the car moves in a horizontal circle, so the net force points horizontally towards the centre. It does not point down the slope, and it does not point along the normal force. Also, \(N = mg/\cos\theta\) is bigger than \(mg\), unlike a car on a ramp where \(N = mg\cos\theta\). Mixing these up is very common.

A curve of radius 80 m is banked at 15°. (a) Calculate the design speed. (b) For a 1000 kg car at this speed, calculate the normal force and the net force.

(a) Design speed:

\[ v = \sqrt{rg\tan\theta} = \sqrt{80 \times 9.8 \times \tan 15^\circ} = \sqrt{210} = 14.5\ \text{m s}^{-1}\ (52\ \text{km h}^{-1}) \]

(b) Normal force from vertical equilibrium:

\[ N = \frac{mg}{\cos\theta} = \frac{1000 \times 9.8}{\cos 15^\circ} = 1.01\times10^4\ \text{N} \]

Net force (horizontal):

\[ F_{\text{net}} = N\sin\theta = mg\tan\theta = 9800 \times \tan 15^\circ = 2.63\times10^3\ \text{N} \]

Check: \(mv^2/r = 1000 \times 210/80 = 2.63\times10^3\ \text{N}\) ✓.

(a) 14.5 m s⁻¹ (b) \(N = 1.0\times10^4\ \text{N}\) perpendicular to the road; \(F_{\text{net}} = 2.6\times10^3\ \text{N}\) horizontally towards the centre.

Faster or slower than the design speed

  • Faster than design speed: the car needs more centripetal force than \(N\sin\theta\) supplies. It tends to slide up and out, so friction acts down the slope (towards the centre side).
  • Slower than design speed: the car tends to slide down and in, so friction acts up the slope.

Use axes along and perpendicular to the slope, and resolve the horizontal net force \(mv^2/r\) into those directions. For a 1000 kg car at 20 m s⁻¹ on the 80 m, 15° curve above: \(mv^2/r = 5000\ \text{N}\).

Along the slope (friction \(f\) down the slope): \(f + mg\sin\theta = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}\cos\theta \Rightarrow f = 5000(0.966) - 9800(0.259) = 2.29\times10^3\ \text{N}\) down the slope.

Perpendicular: \(N - mg\cos\theta = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}\sin\theta \Rightarrow N = 9466 + 1294 = 1.08\times10^4\ \text{N}\).

VCE questions normally only ask for the direction of friction, but this is a good check that you understand the vectors.

Practice

A car travels around a banked curve at exactly the design speed. The direction of the net force on the car is:

  1. down the slope of the banking
  2. perpendicular to the road surface
  3. horizontal, towards the centre of the curve
  4. zero, because the car's speed is constant

The car moves in a horizontal circle, so the net (centripetal) force is horizontal towards the centre. Constant speed does not mean zero net force: the direction of the velocity is changing.

A stone is whirled in a horizontal circle on a string. At the instant the string breaks, the stone moves:

  1. radially outward, away from the centre
  2. along the tangent to the circle at that point
  3. in a spiral outward
  4. towards the centre

With the tension gone there is no horizontal force, so the stone keeps the velocity it had at that instant (first law). That velocity is tangential.

A mass on a string moves in a horizontal circle of fixed radius. If the period of revolution is halved, the tension becomes:

  1. half as large
  2. twice as large
  3. unchanged
  4. four times as large

\(F = 4\pi^2 mr/T^2\). Halving \(T\) multiplies \(1/T^2\) by 4.

A car goes around a banked curve slower than the design speed without sliding. The friction force from the road on the car acts:

  1. up the slope
  2. down the slope
  3. there is no friction
  4. in the direction of motion

Below the design speed, \(N\sin\theta\) is more than the centripetal force needed, so the car tends to slide down the bank. Friction opposes this by acting up the slope.

A heavy truck and a light car both take the same banked curve at its design speed. Compared with the car, the truck:

  1. needs a higher speed to avoid friction
  2. has the same design speed
  3. needs a lower speed to avoid friction
  4. cannot travel at the design speed

\(\tan\theta = v^2/(rg)\) contains no mass. Every vehicle has the same design speed, although the truck's normal force and net force are larger.

A 0.25 kg rubber stopper is whirled in a horizontal circle of radius 0.60 m with a period of 0.50 s (ignore gravity's effect on the string angle). Calculate its speed and the tension in the string.

\(v = \dfrac{2\pi r}{T} = \dfrac{2\pi(0.60)}{0.50} = 7.5\ \text{m s}^{-1}\)

\(F = \dfrac{mv^2}{r} = \dfrac{0.25 \times 7.54^2}{0.60} = 24\ \text{N}\) (or \(4\pi^2 mr/T^2 = 23.7\ \text{N}\)).

Engineers want a curve of radius 120 m to have a design speed of 90 km h⁻¹. At what angle should it be banked?

Convert: \(90\ \text{km h}^{-1} = 90/3.6 = 25\ \text{m s}^{-1}\).

\(\tan\theta = \dfrac{v^2}{rg} = \dfrac{25^2}{120 \times 9.8} = 0.531 \Rightarrow \theta = 28^\circ\).

Forgetting to convert km h⁻¹ gives a nonsense angle (tan θ ≈ 6.9, θ ≈ 82°).

A car travels at constant speed around a flat, icy roundabout and slides towards the outside of the curve. A passenger says “the centrifugal force became stronger than friction and threw us outward.” Explain what actually happened using Newton's laws.

To travel in a circle the car needs a net force towards the centre of \(mv^2/r\), which on a flat road must be supplied by sideways friction. [1]

On ice the maximum friction available was less than \(mv^2/r\), so the net inward force was too small to keep the car on that radius. [1]

By Newton's first law the car then tends to continue in a straighter line (along the tangent), which carries it towards the outside of the curve. No outward force acted. [1]

For the conical pendulum above (0.20 kg, 1.0 m string), the mass is made to go faster so the string makes 60° with the vertical. Find the new tension and explain why the string can never become horizontal.

\(T = \dfrac{mg}{\cos 60^\circ} = \dfrac{1.96}{0.5} = 3.9\ \text{N}\).

A horizontal string would have no vertical component, so nothing could balance the weight: \(T\cos\theta = mg\) needs \(T \to \infty\) as \(\theta \to 90^\circ\).

Summary

  • Constant speed in a circle is still acceleration: \(a = v^2/r\) towards the centre.
  • “Centripetal force” is the net force. Name the real force(s) supplying it; never draw it separately.
  • Banked track at design speed: \(\tan\theta = v^2/(rg)\), \(N = mg/\cos\theta\), net force horizontal.
  • Above design speed friction acts down the slope; below, up the slope.
