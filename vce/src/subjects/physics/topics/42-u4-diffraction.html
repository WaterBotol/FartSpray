What diffraction is

Diffraction is the spreading of a wave as it passes through a gap or around the edge of an obstacle. All waves diffract: water waves around a breakwater, sound around a doorway, radio waves around hills, light through a narrow slit.

The amount of diffraction depends on the ratio of the wavelength to the gap width (or obstacle size), \(\lambda/w\):

  • \(\lambda/w\) close to 1 or larger → significant spreading.
  • \(\lambda/w\) much less than 1 → very little spreading; the wave passes through almost as a straight beam with sharp shadows.

So diffraction increases if the gap is made narrower or the wavelength is made longer.

Single-slit diffraction of light

Light passing through a single narrow slit produces a pattern on a screen: a broad, bright central maximum with much dimmer, narrower bands on either side, separated by dark bands.

  • The central maximum is about twice as wide as the other bright bands.
  • Narrowing the slit (larger \(\lambda/w\)) makes the whole pattern spread out: wider central maximum.
  • Longer wavelength (red rather than blue) also spreads the pattern.
  • An obstacle (a thin wire or hair) of width \(w\) gives a similar pattern to a slit of width \(w\).

A double-slit pattern has evenly spaced fringes of similar width. A single-slit pattern has a central band twice as wide as the rest, with side bands fading quickly. Questions often show a pattern and ask which setup produced it. (Real double-slit patterns show both: the interference fringes sit inside a single-slit diffraction “envelope”.)

A doorway is 0.90 m wide. Compare the diffraction of (a) sound at 1000 Hz (speed 340 m s⁻¹) and (b) green light (550 nm) passing through it.

(a) Sound: \(\lambda = v/f = 340/1000 = 0.34\ \text{m}\), so \(\lambda/w = 0.34/0.90 = 0.38\). Comparable to 1, so the sound spreads widely into the room.

(b) Light: \(\lambda/w = 5.5\times10^{-7}/0.90 = 6.1\times10^{-7}\). Tiny, so the light hardly diffracts at all and casts a sharp shadow.

Sound (λ/w ≈ 0.4) diffracts strongly; light (λ/w ≈ 10⁻⁶) doesn't. That's why you can hear someone around a corner but not see them.

Diffraction limits imaging

Every imaging system (eye, camera, microscope, telescope) collects waves through an opening (aperture). Diffraction spreads the light from each point of the object into a small blurred disc. When two points are close together, their blurs overlap and they can't be told apart: they are not resolved.

  • Detail smaller than roughly the wavelength being used cannot be resolved. Light microscopes (\(\lambda\) ≈ 400–700 nm) can't show objects much smaller than about 200 nm, such as viruses or individual molecules.
  • To see finer detail, use shorter wavelengths: ultraviolet, X-rays, or electrons (whose de Broglie wavelengths can be around 0.01 nm; see Matter waves).
  • For telescopes, a larger aperture (smaller \(\lambda/w\)) reduces diffraction blurring, which is one reason telescope mirrors are huge. Radio telescopes must be enormous (or linked into arrays) because radio wavelengths are so long.

Practice

Light passes through a single slit. Which change produces the greatest amount of diffraction?

  1. wider slit, blue light
  2. wider slit, red light
  3. narrower slit, red light
  4. narrower slit, blue light

Diffraction increases with \(\lambda/w\): the longest wavelength (red) through the narrowest slit.

A screen shows a pattern with a very wide central bright band and much narrower, fainter bands on each side. The pattern was most likely produced by:

  1. a single slit
  2. a double slit with widely spaced slits
  3. a mirror
  4. two separate light bulbs

A central maximum about twice as wide as the others is the signature of single-slit diffraction. Double-slit fringes are evenly spaced.

Electron microscopes can resolve much finer detail than light microscopes mainly because:

  1. electrons travel faster than light
  2. electrons have charge and can be focused
  3. electrons are particles and don't diffract
  4. the electrons' wavelength is much shorter, so diffraction is much less

Electrons do diffract (they have wave properties), but their de Broglie wavelength is far smaller than visible light's, so \(\lambda/w\) is small and blurring is minimal.

AM radio (wavelength about 300 m) can be received in valleys behind hills where FM radio (wavelength about 3 m) can't. This is best explained by:

  1. AM radio travelling faster
  2. AM waves diffracting more around the hills because their wavelength is comparable to the hills' size
  3. FM radio being absorbed by air
  4. AM radio being a longitudinal wave

Both travel at \(c\). The longer AM wavelength gives a larger \(\lambda/w\) for hill-sized obstacles, so AM bends into the shadowed valleys.

Why do we observe diffraction of light easily with a gap of 0.01 mm but not with a gap of 1 cm?

For 500 nm light: \(\lambda/w = 5\times10^{-7}/1\times10^{-5} = 0.05\) for the 0.01 mm gap, large enough for noticeable spreading. For 1 cm, \(\lambda/w = 5\times10^{-5}\), so the spreading is negligible.

A biologist wants to image structures about 50 nm across. Explain why a visible-light microscope is unsuitable and suggest an alternative.

Visible light has wavelengths of about 400–700 nm, much larger than the 50 nm structures, so \(\lambda/w\) is large. [1]

The light diffracts significantly around such small structures, blurring their images so that detail can't be resolved. [1]

An electron microscope (or X-rays), with a wavelength much smaller than 50 nm, gives a small \(\lambda/w\), little diffraction and a resolved image. [1]

Summary

  • Diffraction = spreading through gaps or around obstacles; significant when \(\lambda/w \gtrsim 1\).
  • Single slit: wide central maximum; narrower slit or longer λ → more spreading.
  • Diffraction limits resolution: detail smaller than about λ is blurred. Use shorter wavelengths (electrons, X-rays) or larger apertures.
