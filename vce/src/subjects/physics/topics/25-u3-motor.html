The force on a current in a magnetic field

A current-carrying wire in a magnetic field feels a force because the moving charges in it are pushed sideways by the field. This motor effect is behind every electric motor, speaker and loudspeaker.

Force on a conductor
\[ F = nIlB \quad (I \perp B) \qquad\qquad F = 0 \quad (I \parallel B) \]

\(n\) = number of conductors (e.g. turns of wire along that side of a coil), \(I\) = current (A), \(l\) = length of wire inside the field (m), \(B\) = magnetic flux density (T).

Hold your right hand flat. Fingers point along the field \(B\) (N → S). Thumb points along the conventional current \(I\). Your palm pushes in the direction of the force \(F\).

The force is always perpendicular to both the current and the field.

  • If the wire runs parallel to the field, there is no force, however large the current.
  • The rule uses conventional current. For a flow of electrons, point your thumb the opposite way.
  • \(l\) is only the length in the field. A 2 m wire with 5 cm between the magnet poles has \(l = 0.05\ \text{m}\).

A straight wire carries 4.0 A horizontally from west to east through a uniform 0.50 T magnetic field pointing north. 0.25 m of the wire is in the field. Find the size and direction of the force on it. What would the force be if the wire ran north–south?

A current-carrying wire feels a force only from the component of the field perpendicular to it. The right-hand slap rule gives the direction; a wire parallel to the field feels no force.

Size (current perpendicular to field):

\[ F = nIlB = 1 \times 4.0 \times 0.25 \times 0.50 = 0.50\ \text{N} \]

Direction: fingers north (B), thumb east (I), palm faces up. (Check: \(\text{east} \times \text{north} = \text{up}\).)

North–south wire: current parallel (or antiparallel) to \(B\), so \(F = 0\).

0.50 N vertically upward; zero if the wire is parallel to the field.

The simple DC motor

A DC motor has a rectangular coil of \(n\) turns that can rotate about an axis between the poles of a magnet (a uniform field). Current enters and leaves the coil through brushes that press on a split-ring commutator.

How it turns, step by step

  1. Current flows along one side of the coil and back along the opposite side, so those two sides carry current in opposite directions.
  2. Both sides are perpendicular to the field, so each feels a force \(F = nIlB\), one up and one down. This pair of forces produces a torque that turns the coil.
  3. The torque is maximum when the plane of the coil is parallel to the field (forces furthest from the axis, at right angles to the coil).
  4. As the coil turns towards the vertical, the forces keep the same size and direction but act more and more in line with the axis, so the torque drops. When the plane is perpendicular to the field, the torque is zero.
  5. At exactly that moment the split-ring commutator reverses the current in the coil. The coil's momentum carries it past the vertical, and with the current reversed the forces now turn it the same way for the next half turn.

The split-ring commutator

  • It reverses the current in the coil every half turn (when the coil is perpendicular to the field). It does not reverse the current from the supply, and it does not reverse the field.
  • Its purpose is to keep the torque acting in the same rotational direction, so the coil spins continuously instead of rocking back and forth.
  • If slip rings replaced the commutator on a DC motor, the coil would swing to the vertical position and oscillate there, then stop. It would not rotate continuously.
  • When the coil is perpendicular to the field the forces on the long sides are not zero; only the torque is zero. (In real motors the brushes briefly touch the gap, so there's no current at that instant anyway.)

A DC motor coil has 50 turns and is square, with sides of 4.0 cm. It carries 2.0 A in a uniform 0.30 T field. The coil can rotate about an axis through the middle of two opposite sides.

(a) Find the force on each side that is perpendicular to the field when the coil's plane is parallel to the field. (b) What is the force on each of these sides when the plane is perpendicular to the field? (c) What is the force on the other two sides when the plane is parallel to the field?

The force on each side depends on the angle between that side and the field, not on the angle of the coil. What changes as the coil turns is the torque, because the force's line of action moves closer to the axis.

(a) Each side has 50 conductors of length 0.040 m, perpendicular to \(B\):

\[ F = nIlB = 50 \times 2.0 \times 0.040 \times 0.30 = 1.2\ \text{N} \]

(b) Those sides are still perpendicular to \(B\) (they are parallel to the axis, which is perpendicular to the field), so the force is still 1.2 N. It just points along the plane of the coil, so it produces no torque.

(c) When the plane is parallel to the field, the other two sides run parallel to \(B\), so \(F = 0\).

(a) 1.2 N (b) still 1.2 N, but zero torque (c) 0 N. Part (b) is the trap: students often write zero.

Making a better motor

  • Larger torque: more turns (\(n\)), more current (\(I\)), a stronger field (\(B\)), or longer sides / a wider coil.
  • Smoother rotation: use several coils at different angles, each with its own commutator segments, so there's always a coil near maximum torque.
  • Curved pole pieces and an iron core make the field radial, so the sides are always cutting across the field and the torque stays near maximum for most of the turn.

Experiments often measure a magnetic force by balancing it against a known weight on a beam or balance. If the beam is pivoted at its centre and the two forces act at equal distances from the pivot, they have equal turning effects when balanced, so \(F_B = mg\). If the distances were unequal, the forces wouldn't be equal at balance. Also check which parts of the wire are parallel to the field: they feel no force (\(F = 0\) when \(I \parallel B\)).

Practice

In a DC motor, the torque on the coil is zero when:

  1. the plane of the coil is parallel to the magnetic field
  2. the current in the coil is at its maximum
  3. the forces on the sides of the coil are zero
  4. the plane of the coil is perpendicular to the magnetic field

When the plane is perpendicular to the field, the forces on the long sides point along the plane of the coil (in line with the axis), so they produce no turning effect even though they are not zero.

The main function of the split-ring commutator in a DC motor is to:

  1. reverse the direction of the magnetic field every half turn
  2. reverse the direction of the current in the coil every half turn
  3. convert DC from the supply into AC in the external circuit
  4. reduce friction between the brushes and the coil

Reversing the coil current every half turn keeps the torque in the same rotational direction, so the motor spins continuously.

A 0.10 m length of wire carries 3.0 A at right angles to a 0.20 T field. The force on it is:

  1. 0.060 N
  2. 0.60 N
  3. 6.0 N
  4. 0 N

\(F = IlB = 3.0 \times 0.10 \times 0.20 = 0.060\ \text{N}\).

A wire carrying current is placed along the direction of a uniform magnetic field. The current is doubled. The force on the wire:

  1. doubles
  2. quadruples
  3. remains zero
  4. halves

A current parallel to the field experiences no force. Twice zero is still zero.

The split-ring commutator of a working DC motor is replaced by slip rings, keeping the DC supply. The coil will most likely:

  1. rotate continuously at the same speed
  2. rotate until its plane is perpendicular to the field, then oscillate about that position and stop
  3. rotate continuously in the opposite direction
  4. rotate faster, because slip rings have less friction

Without the reversal, the torque changes direction every half turn. The coil overshoots the perpendicular position, is pulled back, and settles there.

A 20-turn rectangular coil has sides of 5.0 cm (perpendicular to the field) and 3.0 cm. It carries 1.5 A in a 0.40 T field. Find the force on each 5.0 cm side when the coil plane is parallel to the field, and describe the directions of these two forces.

\(F = nIlB = 20 \times 1.5 \times 0.050 \times 0.40 = 0.60\ \text{N}\) on each 5.0 cm side.

The currents in the two sides are in opposite directions, so the forces are opposite: one side is pushed up, the other down, forming a turning pair (torque).

A “current balance” consists of a horizontal wire 5.0 cm long held between magnet poles sitting on an electronic scale. When 3.0 A flows, the scale reading changes by 3.1 g. Find the magnetic field strength.

Change in force: \(F = mg = 0.0031 \times 9.8 = 0.030\ \text{N}\).

\(B = \dfrac{F}{Il} = \dfrac{0.030}{3.0 \times 0.050} = 0.20\ \text{T}\).

(By Newton's third law, the force the field exerts on the wire is matched by a force on the magnet, which is what the scale detects.)

Explain how a simple DC motor keeps rotating in one direction. Refer to the forces on the coil, the torque, and the role of the split-ring commutator.

The two sides of the coil perpendicular to the field carry current in opposite directions, so they experience equal and opposite forces (\(F = nIlB\)). [1]

These forces produce a torque that rotates the coil; the torque is greatest when the coil plane is parallel to the field and zero when it is perpendicular. [1]

At the perpendicular position, the split-ring commutator swaps which brush each end of the coil touches, reversing the current in the coil. [1]

The coil's momentum carries it past this point, and with the current reversed the forces again produce torque in the same rotational direction, so rotation continues. [1]

Summary

  • \(F = nIlB\) when \(I \perp B\); zero when parallel. Right-hand rule: fingers B, thumb I, palm F.
  • Motor torque: maximum with coil plane ∥ B, zero with plane ⊥ B (forces not zero).
  • Split-ring commutator reverses coil current every half turn to keep the rotation one way.
