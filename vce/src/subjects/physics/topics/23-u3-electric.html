Coulomb's law

Force between two point charges
\[ F = k\frac{q_1 q_2}{r^2} \]

\(k = 8.99\times10^9\ \text{N m}^2\,\text{C}^{-2}\); charges in coulombs (C); \(r\) between the centres (m). Like charges repel, unlike charges attract. The forces on the two charges are equal and opposite.

Substitute the sizes of the charges (ignore the signs) to get the magnitude of the force, then state “attractive” or “repulsive” from the signs. Plugging in negative signs and reporting a “negative force” without explanation loses marks.

Charge prefixes you'll meet: μC = \(10^{-6}\) C, nC = \(10^{-9}\) C. The charge of one electron is \(-1.60\times10^{-19}\ \text{C}\) and of one proton \(+1.60\times10^{-19}\ \text{C}\).

A \(+3.0\ \mu\text{C}\) charge and a \(-5.0\ \mu\text{C}\) charge are 0.20 m apart. Find the force between them.

Coulomb's law gives the size of the force between point charges; the signs tell you only whether it attracts or repels. Use magnitudes in the formula and state the direction in words.

\[ F = \frac{(8.99\times10^9)(3.0\times10^{-6})(5.0\times10^{-6})}{(0.20)^2} = \frac{0.135}{0.040} = 3.4\ \text{N} \]

3.4 N, attractive (opposite charges). Each charge feels 3.4 N towards the other.

Electric field of a point charge

Field strength
\[ E = \frac{F}{q} \qquad\qquad E = k\frac{q}{r^2}\ \text{(point charge)} \]

Units: N C⁻¹ (equivalently V m⁻¹). Direction: away from a positive source charge, towards a negative one.

Find the electric field 0.10 m from the centre of a small sphere carrying \(+2.0\ \mu\text{C}\). What force would an electron experience there?

The field strength at a point is the force per coulomb on a positive test charge. A negative charge feels a force opposite to the field direction.

\[ E = \frac{(8.99\times10^9)(2.0\times10^{-6})}{(0.10)^2} = 1.8\times10^6\ \text{N C}^{-1}\ \text{(away from the sphere)} \]
\[ F = qE = (1.60\times10^{-19})(1.8\times10^6) = 2.9\times10^{-13}\ \text{N} \]

\(1.8\times10^6\ \text{N C}^{-1}\) outward; the electron feels \(2.9\times10^{-13}\ \text{N}\) towards the sphere (opposite to the field, because it is negative).

Fields from two charges (along a line)

Fields are vectors, so add them. On the line between two charges, work out each field's size and direction separately, then combine.

A \(+4.0\ \mu\text{C}\) charge and a \(+1.0\ \mu\text{C}\) charge are 0.30 m apart. Where between them is the electric field zero?

Between two like charges the fields point in opposite directions, so they cancel at one point. It lies closer to the smaller charge. Square-rooting the equation turns it into a simple linear equation.

Between two like charges the fields point in opposite directions, so they can cancel. Let \(x\) be the distance from the 4.0 μC charge:

\[ \frac{k(4.0)}{x^2} = \frac{k(1.0)}{(0.30 - x)^2} \]

Square-root: \(\dfrac{2.0}{x} = \dfrac{1.0}{0.30 - x} \Rightarrow 0.60 - 2.0x = x \Rightarrow x = 0.20\ \text{m}\).

0.20 m from the 4.0 μC charge (closer to the smaller charge, as expected).

Fields from two charges (at right angles)

When the two fields at a point aren't along the same line, draw each field arrow (away from positive charges, towards negative charges), then add them as vectors: Pythagoras for the size and trigonometry for the direction.

Point P is 0.30 m east of a \(+5.0\ \mu\text{C}\) charge and 0.20 m south of a \(+2.0\ \mu\text{C}\) charge. Find the magnitude and direction of the resultant electric field at P.

Fields are vectors, so fields from separate charges add by vector addition, not by adding magnitudes. Each field points away from a positive charge (towards a negative one). When the two are perpendicular, use Pythagoras and trigonometry.

Field from the 5.0 μC charge: \(E_1 = \dfrac{(8.99\times10^9)(5.0\times10^{-6})}{0.30^2} = 5.0\times10^5\ \text{N C}^{-1}\), pointing east (away from a positive charge).

Field from the 2.0 μC charge: \(E_2 = \dfrac{(8.99\times10^9)(2.0\times10^{-6})}{0.20^2} = 4.5\times10^5\ \text{N C}^{-1}\), pointing south.

The fields are at right angles: \(E = \sqrt{(5.0\times10^5)^2 + (4.5\times10^5)^2} = 6.7\times10^5\ \text{N C}^{-1}\).

Direction: \(\tan\theta = 4.5/5.0 \Rightarrow \theta = 42^\circ\). \(6.7\times10^5\ \text{N C}^{-1}\), 42° south of east. Just adding the two magnitudes (\(9.5\times10^5\)) is the classic error.

Uniform fields between parallel plates

Two parallel plates connected to a voltage supply create a uniform field between them, pointing from the positive plate to the negative plate.

Uniform field
\[ E = \frac{V}{d} \qquad F = qE \]

\(V\) = potential difference between the plates (V), \(d\) = plate separation (m).

In a uniform field, a charge experiences exactly the same force next to the positive plate, in the middle, or next to the negative plate. Don't use \(kq/r^2\) for plates: that formula is only for point charges. Doubling the separation at constant voltage halves the field and the force.

Two parallel plates 2.0 cm apart are connected to a 500 V supply. Find the field strength, the force on an electron between the plates and its acceleration (\(m_e = 9.1\times10^{-31}\ \text{kg}\)).

Between parallel plates the field is uniform, so the force on a charge is the same everywhere between them. The acceleration is huge because an electron's mass is so small.

Field: \(E = V/d = 500/0.020 = 2.5\times10^4\ \text{V m}^{-1}\).

Force: \(F = qE = (1.60\times10^{-19})(2.5\times10^4) = 4.0\times10^{-15}\ \text{N}\), towards the positive plate.

Acceleration: \(a = F/m = 4.0\times10^{-15}/9.1\times10^{-31} = 4.4\times10^{15}\ \text{m s}^{-2}\).

\(2.5\times10^4\ \text{V m}^{-1}\); \(4.0\times10^{-15}\ \text{N}\) towards the + plate; \(4.4\times10^{15}\ \text{m s}^{-2}\). Gravity on the electron (\(\sim10^{-29}\ \text{N}\)) is utterly negligible.

Accelerating charges: \(W = qV\)

When a charge moves through a potential difference \(V\), the field does work \(W = qV\) on it. If it starts from rest and nothing else acts, all of that work becomes kinetic energy:

Energy gained through a potential difference
\[ W = qV = \Delta E_k \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \tfrac12 mv^2 = qV \ \ (\text{from rest, } v \ll c) \]

One electron-volt (eV) is the energy gained by one electron (charge \(e\)) accelerated through a potential difference of 1 V: \(1\ \text{eV} = 1.60\times10^{-19}\ \text{J}\). An electron accelerated through 2000 V gains 2000 eV = 2.0 keV. To convert eV → J, multiply by \(1.60\times10^{-19}\).

Electrons start from rest and are accelerated through 2000 V in an electron gun. Find (a) their kinetic energy in eV and in J, and (b) their final speed. (c) What speed would a proton reach through the same voltage?

The work done on a charge by an accelerating voltage is \(qV\), and it becomes kinetic energy. Equal charges gain equal energy, but the lighter particle ends up much faster.

(a) \(E_k = qV = e(2000) = 2000\ \text{eV} = 2000 \times 1.60\times10^{-19} = 3.2\times10^{-16}\ \text{J}\).

(b) \(\tfrac12 m_ev^2 = 3.2\times10^{-16}\):

\[ v = \sqrt{\frac{2(3.2\times10^{-16})}{9.1\times10^{-31}}} = 2.65\times10^7\ \text{m s}^{-1} \]

That's under 9% of \(c\), so the non-relativistic formula is fine.

(c) Proton: same charge size, so the same kinetic energy, but 1835 times the mass:

\[ v = \sqrt{\frac{2(3.2\times10^{-16})}{1.67\times10^{-27}}} = 6.2\times10^5\ \text{m s}^{-1} \]

(a) 2000 eV = \(3.2\times10^{-16}\ \text{J}\) (b) \(2.7\times10^7\ \text{m s}^{-1}\) (c) \(6.2\times10^5\ \text{m s}^{-1}\).

The kinetic energy gained is \(qV\), independent of the plate separation. Moving the plates further apart (same voltage) weakens the field and the force, but the force acts over a longer distance, so the work (\(Fd = qEd = qV\)) is unchanged.

Electric vs gravitational forces

In a hydrogen atom the electron is about \(5.3\times10^{-11}\ \text{m}\) from the proton. Compare the electric and gravitational forces between them.

Both forces follow inverse-square laws, so their ratio doesn't depend on distance. The comparison shows how weak gravity is between individual particles.

\[ F_e = \frac{(8.99\times10^9)(1.60\times10^{-19})^2}{(5.3\times10^{-11})^2} = 8.2\times10^{-8}\ \text{N} \]
\[ F_g = \frac{(6.67\times10^{-11})(9.1\times10^{-31})(1.67\times10^{-27})}{(5.3\times10^{-11})^2} = 3.6\times10^{-47}\ \text{N} \]

The electric force is about \(2\times10^{39}\) times stronger. Gravity only dominates on large scales because matter is almost perfectly electrically neutral, while mass always adds up.

An electron fired between horizontal plates (parallel to them) behaves like a projectile: constant horizontal velocity and a constant sideways acceleration \(a = eE/m\). For a 2.0 × 10⁷ m s⁻¹ electron passing 5.0 cm long plates with \(E = 2000\ \text{V m}^{-1}\): \(t = 0.050/2.0\times10^7 = 2.5\times10^{-9}\ \text{s}\), \(a = 3.5\times10^{14}\ \text{m s}^{-2}\), deflection \(= \tfrac12 at^2 = 1.1\ \text{mm}\), in a parabolic path towards the positive plate.

Practice

An electron is placed at three positions between two charged parallel plates: near the positive plate, in the middle, and near the negative plate. The force on it is:

  1. greatest near the positive plate
  2. greatest near the negative plate
  3. the same at all three positions
  4. zero in the middle

The field between parallel plates is uniform, so \(F = qE\) is identical everywhere between them.

The distance between two point charges is halved. The electric force between them:

  1. halves
  2. becomes four times larger
  3. doubles
  4. becomes one quarter

\(F \propto 1/r^2\): halving \(r\) multiplies \(F\) by 4.

The voltage across a pair of parallel plates is kept constant while the plate separation is doubled. The kinetic energy gained by an electron crossing from one plate to the other:

  1. stays the same
  2. halves
  3. doubles
  4. quarters

\(E_k = qV\) depends only on the voltage. The field halves, but the distance doubles, so the work is the same.

An alpha particle (charge \(+2e\)) is accelerated from rest through a potential difference of 5000 V. Its kinetic energy is:

  1. 2500 eV
  2. 5000 eV
  3. \(8.0\times10^{-16}\ \text{eV}\)
  4. 10 000 eV

\(E_k = qV = (2e)(5000\ \text{V}) = 10\,000\ \text{eV}\) (\(1.6\times10^{-15}\ \text{J}\)). The eV unit is per electron charge, so double the charge means double the energy.

An electron and a proton are released from rest in the same uniform electric field. Which statement is correct?

  1. They experience forces in the same direction with different sizes.
  2. They experience equal-sized forces in opposite directions, and the electron's acceleration is much larger.
  3. They have the same acceleration because they have the same size charge.
  4. The proton's acceleration is larger because it is heavier.

\(F = qE\), and the charges are equal in size but opposite in sign. \(a = F/m\): the electron's mass is 1835 times smaller, so its acceleration is 1835 times larger.

A charge of \(+2.0\ \mu\text{C}\) moves from the negative plate to the positive plate of a pair of plates with a potential difference of 300 V. (a) How much work must be done on it? (b) Does its electric potential energy increase or decrease?

(a) \(W = qV = (2.0\times10^{-6})(300) = 6.0\times10^{-4}\ \text{J}\).

(b) A positive charge is pushed towards the negative plate by the field; moving it to the positive plate is against the field force, so its electric potential energy increases by \(6.0\times10^{-4}\ \text{J}\).

An electron is accelerated from rest across a 2.0 cm gap between plates with a potential difference of 500 V. Find (a) its final speed using energy, and (b) the time taken to cross the gap.

(a) \(\tfrac12 mv^2 = eV \Rightarrow v = \sqrt{2(1.60\times10^{-19})(500)/9.1\times10^{-31}} = 1.33\times10^7\ \text{m s}^{-1}\).

(b) Uniform acceleration from rest: \(d = \tfrac12(u + v)t \Rightarrow t = \dfrac{2d}{v} = \dfrac{2(0.020)}{1.33\times10^7} = 3.0\times10^{-9}\ \text{s}\).

A student states: “An electron accelerated through 100 V gains 100 J of energy.” Identify the error and give the correct value.

The energy gained is \(qV\), not \(V\). [1]

\(E_k = (1.60\times10^{-19}\ \text{C})(100\ \text{V}) = 1.6\times10^{-17}\ \text{J}\), [1]

which is 100 eV. The student confused the electron-volt with the joule. [1]

Summary

  • \(F = kq_1q_2/r^2\), \(E = kq/r^2\) for point charges; use magnitudes, then give direction.
  • Parallel plates: \(E = V/d\), uniform; \(F = qE\) everywhere between them.
  • \(W = qV = \Delta E_k\); 1 eV = \(1.60\times10^{-19}\) J.
  • Electric forces dwarf gravity at atomic scales.
