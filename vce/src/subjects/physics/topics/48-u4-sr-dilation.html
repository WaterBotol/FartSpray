The Lorentz factor

Lorentz factor
\[ \gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - \dfrac{v^2}{c^2}}} \]

\(\gamma\) is always ≥ 1. It is essentially 1 at everyday speeds and grows without limit as \(v \to c\).

\(v\)0.10c0.50c0.60c0.80c0.90c0.99c0.995c
\(\gamma\)1.0051.1551.251.6672.2947.0910.0

If \(v\) is given as a fraction of \(c\), write \(\beta = v/c\) and use \(\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - \beta^2}\). For \(v = 0.8c\): \(1 - 0.64 = 0.36\), \(\sqrt{0.36} = 0.6\), \(\gamma = 1/0.6 = 1.667\). Keep \(\gamma\) unrounded until the end.

Time dilation

The time interval between two events measured in the frame of reference in which the two events occur at the same place. It is measured by a single clock that is present at both events.

Time dilation
\[ t = \gamma t_0 \]

\(t\) is the time between the same two events measured by an observer for whom the events happen at different places (they see the clock moving). Since \(\gamma \geq 1\), \(t \geq t_0\): moving clocks run slow. Proper time is always the shortest.

Einstein's light clock (photon clock)

This thought experiment is where \(t = \gamma t_0\) comes from. A photon bounces between two mirrors a distance \(d\) apart.

  • In the clock's own frame the photon goes straight up and down: one tick takes \(t_0 = 2d/c\). This is proper time (both ends of the tick happen at the same place).
  • An observer who sees the clock moving at \(v\) sees the photon follow a longer diagonal path.
  • Newton would say the tick takes the same time, so the photon must move faster than \(c\) along the diagonal. Einstein's second postulate says the photon travels at \(c\) for both observers, so the longer path must take longer: the moving clock runs slow.
  • Pythagoras on half a tick: \(\left(\tfrac{ct}{2}\right)^2 = d^2 + \left(\tfrac{vt}{2}\right)^2\), which rearranges to \(t = \dfrac{t_0}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}} = \gamma t_0\).

Length contraction

The length of an object (or distance between two points) measured in the frame in which the object (or the two points) is at rest.

Length contraction
\[ L = \frac{L_0}{\gamma} \]

An observer who sees the object moving measures it shorter, only along the direction of motion. Proper length is always the longest.

A spaceship flying horizontally at \(0.8c\) is measured shorter in length, but its height and width are unchanged. A square moving sideways becomes a narrower rectangle, not a smaller square.

Deciding who measures proper time and proper length

This is the skill that decides most relativity marks. Ask two questions:

  1. Proper time: in which frame do the start and end events happen at the same place? (Usually: whoever carries the clock, travels with the particle, or rides the ship.)
  2. Proper length: in which frame is the length at rest? (The ship's crew for the ship's length; Earth for the Earth–star distance or the thickness of the atmosphere.)

For a trip to a star, the astronaut measures the proper time but a contracted distance; Earth measures the proper distance but a dilated time. Neither observer has both “proper” quantities. Mixing \(t_0\) with \(L_0\) in one speed calculation is a very common error.

A spaceship travels at \(0.80c\) from Earth to a star 12 light-years away (as measured from Earth). Ignore acceleration.

(a) How long does the trip take according to Earth observers? (b) How long does it take according to the astronaut? (c) How far is it to the star according to the astronaut? (d) Show that both observers agree on the relative speed.

Decide who measures proper time (the frame where both events happen at the same place: the ship) and proper length (the frame where the distance is at rest: Earth). Everyone else measures dilated time or contracted length, and both frames agree on the relative speed.

\(\gamma\): \(\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - 0.64} = 1/0.6 = 1.667\).

(a) Earth frame: distance 12 ly at \(0.80c\): \(t = \dfrac{12\ \text{ly}}{0.80c} = 15\ \text{years}\). (A light-year is the distance light travels in a year, so ly ÷ c gives years.)

(b) The astronaut measures proper time (departure and arrival both happen at the ship):

\[ t_0 = \frac{t}{\gamma} = \frac{15}{1.667} = 9.0\ \text{years} \]

(c) The Earth–star distance is proper length in Earth's frame, so the astronaut measures it contracted:

\[ L = \frac{L_0}{\gamma} = \frac{12}{1.667} = 7.2\ \text{ly} \]

(d) Earth: \(12\ \text{ly}/15\ \text{y} = 0.80c\). Astronaut: \(7.2\ \text{ly}/9.0\ \text{y} = 0.80c\). ✓

(a) 15 years (b) 9.0 years (c) 7.2 ly (d) both get \(0.80c\). Time dilation (Earth's view) and length contraction (the astronaut's view) are two descriptions of the same physics.

A spaceship is 100 m long when measured by its crew. What length does an observer on a space station measure as it passes at \(0.60c\)? What does the crew measure for the ship's length?

Proper length is measured in the frame where the object is at rest (the crew's). Observers who see it moving measure it shorter, but only along the direction of motion.

\(\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - 0.36} = 1.25\). The crew measure proper length (100 m).

Station: \(L = L_0/\gamma = 100/1.25 = 80\ \text{m}\).

80 m for the station; 100 m for the crew. The crew notice nothing unusual about their own ship.

Evidence: muon decay

Muons are unstable particles created when cosmic rays hit the upper atmosphere, about 10 km up. At rest, a muon's mean lifetime is 2.2 μs. They are created moving at about \(0.998c\).

Muons are created 10 km above the ground moving at \(0.998c\). Their mean lifetime at rest is 2.2 μs. (a) Classically, how far would a typical muon travel before decaying? (b) Explain, from Earth's frame, why many reach the ground. (c) Explain the same result from the muon's frame.

Muons are the classic evidence for relativity. Earth observers see the muon's clock run slow (time dilation); the muon sees the atmosphere contracted (length contraction). Both explanations give the same prediction, and each stays within one frame of reference.

(a) Classical: \(d = vt = (0.998)(3.0\times10^8)(2.2\times10^{-6}) = 660\ \text{m}\). Almost none should reach the ground 10 km below.

\(\gamma\) at \(0.998c\): \(\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - 0.998^2} = 15.8\).

(b) Earth frame: time dilation. The 2.2 μs lifetime is proper time (measured in the muon's frame). Earth observers measure it dilated:

\[ t = \gamma t_0 = 15.8 \times 2.2\ \mu\text{s} = 34.8\ \mu\text{s} \]

In that time: \(d = (0.998c)(34.8\times10^{-6}) = 1.04\times10^4\ \text{m} \approx 10\ \text{km}\). Enough to reach the ground.

(c) Muon frame: length contraction. The muon is at rest; the atmosphere rushes past at \(0.998c\). The 10 km thickness is proper length in Earth's frame, so the muon measures it contracted:

\[ L = \frac{L_0}{\gamma} = \frac{10\,000}{15.8} = 632\ \text{m} \]

Time to cover 632 m at \(0.998c\): \(632/(2.99\times10^8) = 2.1\ \mu\text{s}\), within its 2.2 μs lifetime.

(a) about 660 m (b) the muon's clock runs slow: 34.8 μs in Earth's frame, enough to travel ~10 km (c) the atmosphere is only ~630 m thick in the muon's frame. The large number of muons detected at sea level is direct experimental evidence for both effects.

In Earth's frame, use time dilation (the atmosphere is its proper thickness). In the muon's frame, use length contraction (its lifetime is proper time). Never apply both in the same frame.

Relativity in action: particle accelerators and GPS

Particle accelerators

  • Longer lifetimes: unstable particles moving close to \(c\) in an accelerator live longer in the lab by exactly the factor \(\gamma\). In a 1977 CERN storage-ring experiment, muons with \(\gamma \approx 29\) had a lab lifetime of about 64 μs instead of 2.2 μs, matching time dilation to within about 0.1%.
  • The speed limit: at the Australian Synchrotron, 3 GeV electrons have \(\gamma \approx 5900\) but travel at about \(0.99999999c\). Adding more energy raises \(\gamma\) (and the energy) enormously while the speed barely changes, which is why accelerator designs must use relativistic equations (see mass–energy).

GPS satellites

GPS receivers find position by timing signals from satellites, so the satellites' atomic clocks must agree with ground clocks to within nanoseconds. A GPS satellite orbits at about 3.9 km s⁻¹, so special relativity makes its clock run slow, relative to the ground, by about 7 μs per day. (General relativity, which isn't in the course, makes it run fast by about 45 μs per day, so the net effect is about 38 μs fast per day.) Uncorrected, 38 μs of timing error corresponds to \(c \times 38\ \mu\text{s} \approx 11\ \text{km}\) of position error building up every day, so the satellite clocks are deliberately adjusted.

A GPS satellite moves at \(3.87\times10^3\ \text{m s}^{-1}\). Using \(\gamma - 1 \approx \tfrac12\left(\frac{v}{c}\right)^2\) for small speeds, estimate how far its clock falls behind a ground clock in one day because of special relativity alone.

At everyday speeds \(\gamma\) is only slightly above 1, but atomic clocks are precise enough to detect the difference. GPS clocks must be corrected for it.

\(\frac{v}{c} = \frac{3.87\times10^3}{3.0\times10^8} = 1.29\times10^{-5}\), so \(\gamma - 1 \approx \tfrac12(1.29\times10^{-5})^2 = 8.3\times10^{-11}\).

In one day (86 400 s) the difference is \(86\,400 \times 8.3\times10^{-11} = 7.2\times10^{-6}\ \text{s}\).

About 7 μs per day. Tiny, but light travels about 2 km in 7 μs, so it matters for navigation.

Each inertial observer sees the other's clock running slow. There's no contradiction because they disagree about which events are simultaneous. The famous “twin paradox” is resolved because the travelling twin must accelerate to turn around, so their frame is not inertial the whole time.

Practice

An astronaut travels at high speed to a distant star. Who measures the proper time for the journey?

  1. the astronaut
  2. an observer on Earth
  3. an observer on the star
  4. both Earth and star observers together

Departure and arrival both happen at the astronaut's location, so only the astronaut's clock is present at both events.

For the same journey, who measures the proper distance between Earth and the star?

  1. the astronaut
  2. no-one, because distances are always contracted
  3. an observer on Earth
  4. both measure the same distance

Earth and the star are at rest relative to each other in Earth's frame, so Earth measures the proper length. The astronaut measures a contracted distance.

A spaceship passes Earth at \(0.60c\). Its crew measure a 1.0 hour interval on their clock. An Earth observer measures this interval as:

  1. 0.80 h
  2. 1.25 h
  3. 1.0 h
  4. 1.67 h

The crew measure proper time. \(t = \gamma t_0 = 1.25 \times 1.0 = 1.25\ \text{h}\).

A cube-shaped spaceship with 10 m sides flies past at \(0.80c\) in the direction of one of its edges. An external observer measures its dimensions as:

  1. 6 m × 6 m × 6 m
  2. 10 m × 10 m × 10 m
  3. 16.7 m × 10 m × 10 m
  4. 6 m × 10 m × 10 m

Only the dimension along the motion contracts: \(10/1.667 = 6\ \text{m}\). The other two stay 10 m.

Muons reaching the Earth's surface are evidence for special relativity because:

  1. they travel faster than light in the atmosphere
  2. their lifetime is longer than classical physics predicts in every frame, including their own
  3. without time dilation (or length contraction) far fewer would survive the trip through the atmosphere
  4. their mass increases as they fall

Classically most would decay within about 660 m. In their own frame their lifetime is the normal 2.2 μs; it's Earth observers who measure it dilated.

A particle with a proper lifetime of \(2.6\times10^{-8}\ \text{s}\) moves through a lab at \(0.95c\). Find its lifetime as measured in the lab and the distance it travels in the lab before decaying.

\(\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - 0.9025} = 3.20\).

\(t = \gamma t_0 = 3.20 \times 2.6\times10^{-8} = 8.3\times10^{-8}\ \text{s}\).

\(d = vt = 0.95 \times 3.0\times10^8 \times 8.3\times10^{-8} = 24\ \text{m}\) (classically only 7.4 m).

How fast must a spaceship travel so that its crew measure a journey to a star 20 ly away (Earth frame) as taking only 10 years?

In the crew's frame the distance is \(L = 20/\gamma\) ly and the time is 10 y, so \(v = \dfrac{20/\gamma}{10}c = \dfrac{2}{\gamma}c\), i.e. \(\beta = 2\sqrt{1 - \beta^2}\).

Square: \(\beta^2 = 4(1 - \beta^2) \Rightarrow 5\beta^2 = 4 \Rightarrow \beta = 0.894\).

\(v = 0.89c\) (check: \(\gamma = 2.24\), distance 8.94 ly, time 8.94/0.894 = 10 y ✓).

Explain how the detection of large numbers of muons at sea level supports special relativity. Refer to the frames of reference of both an Earth observer and the muon.

Muons are produced ~10 km up with a proper mean lifetime of about 2.2 μs; classically, even at nearly \(c\), they'd travel only ~660 m before decaying, so very few should reach sea level. [1]

Earth frame: the muon's lifetime is proper time in its own frame; Earth observers measure it dilated (\(t = \gamma t_0\), about 35 μs for \(\gamma \approx 16\)), long enough to travel ~10 km. [1]

Muon frame: the atmosphere's thickness is proper length in Earth's frame; the muon measures it contracted (\(L = L_0/\gamma\), ~630 m), which it can cross within its normal 2.2 μs lifetime. [1]

Both frames predict that many muons reach the ground, which matches observation and contradicts classical physics. [1]

Without relativistic corrections, GPS positions would drift by kilometres per day. Special relativity alone predicts that a GPS satellite clock, compared with a ground clock,

  1. runs fast, because the satellite is higher up
  2. runs slow, because the satellite is moving relative to the ground
  3. runs at exactly the same rate, because both are in inertial frames
  4. stops, because it is in free fall

Moving clocks are measured to run slow (time dilation), by about 7 μs per day for GPS. The “runs fast because it is higher” effect in A is general relativity, which isn't part of the course.

Summary

  • \(\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}\); \(t = \gamma t_0\); \(L = L_0/\gamma\).
  • Proper time: events at the same place (the traveller). Proper length: object at rest (usually Earth for Earth–star distances).
  • Contraction only along the motion.
  • Muons: Earth frame → time dilation; muon frame → length contraction. Same answer.
  • Particle accelerators (longer lifetimes, speed limit) and GPS clocks (about 7 μs per day from special relativity) are everyday evidence.
