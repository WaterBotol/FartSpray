Frames of reference

A frame of reference is the point of view from which you measure positions, velocities and times, like a coordinate system that travels with an observer.

A frame of reference that is not accelerating: it is at rest or moving at constant velocity. In an inertial frame, Newton's first law holds (an object with no net force stays at rest or moves in a straight line at constant speed).

  • Inertial: a train cruising at constant velocity on a straight track; a spaceship drifting with engines off far from any planet. Inside, a ball dropped falls straight down relative to you, and nothing you do inside can tell you whether you are moving.
  • Non-inertial: a car braking or turning; a spinning merry-go-round. Objects appear to be pushed by forces with no source (you lurch forward when braking).

A car going around a roundabout at a constant 40 km h⁻¹ is not an inertial frame: its velocity is changing direction, so it is accelerating. Only constant velocity (straight line) counts.

Classical (Galilean) relativity

Galileo and Newton already had a principle of relativity: the laws of mechanics are the same in all inertial frames. Classical physics also assumes:

  • Time is absolute: clocks tick at the same rate for everyone, whatever their motion.
  • Length is absolute: a rod has the same length for every observer.
  • Velocities simply add: a ball thrown forward at 20 m s⁻¹ from a train moving at 30 m s⁻¹ moves at 50 m s⁻¹ relative to the ground.

The problem with light

Maxwell's equations predict that light travels at \(c = 3.0\times10^8\ \text{m s}^{-1}\), but they don't say “relative to what”. Physicists assumed light travelled through an invisible medium, the aether, and that \(c\) was its speed relative to the aether. Since Earth moves through space, light should travel at different speeds in different directions relative to us.

In 1887 Michelson and Morley built an extremely sensitive interferometer to detect this difference. They found no difference at all in any direction or at any time of year: the famous null result. The speed of light was the same in every direction.

Einstein's two postulates (1905)

The laws of physics are the same in all inertial (non-accelerated) frames of reference.

The speed of light in a vacuum has the same value, \(c\), for all observers, regardless of their motion or the motion of the source.

Postulate 1 extends Galileo's idea from mechanics to all of physics, including electromagnetism. So no experiment in a closed laboratory can tell whether it is “really” moving, and there is no special aether frame. Postulate 2 follows from Postulate 1 (Maxwell's equations must work the same way in every frame) and is supported by the Michelson–Morley result.

A spaceship moving at \(0.5c\) switches on its headlights. Classical velocity addition says the ship's crew measure the light at \(c\) and an observer on Earth measures \(1.5c\). Einstein says both measure exactly \(c\). For both to be right, measurements of time and distance must differ between the two frames. That leads directly to time dilation and length contraction (next page).

The limitation of classical mechanics

At speeds approaching \(c\), classical mechanics makes predictions that are simply wrong:

  • It predicts that a constant force would keep accelerating an electron past \(c\). In accelerators, electrons get closer and closer to \(c\) but never reach it, however much energy is added.
  • It predicts that fast-moving unstable particles (muons) decay after their normal lifetime. Observed muons last far longer.
  • It predicts that the speed of light depends on the observer. Every measurement gives \(c\).

Relativity doesn't throw out Newton: at everyday speeds the relativistic formulas give the same answers to many decimal places. Classical mechanics is an approximation that is excellent when \(v \ll c\).

A light flashes in the exact middle of a moving train carriage. For passengers, the light reaches the front and back walls at the same time. For someone on the platform, the back wall moves towards the light and the front wall moves away, and since the light travels at \(c\) in their frame too, it reaches the back wall first. Two events that are simultaneous in one frame are not simultaneous in another. This is the root of every relativistic effect.

Which of the following is an inertial frame of reference?

  2. a lift accelerating upward
  3. a rocket firing its engines to speed up

An inertial frame has zero acceleration. Turning at constant speed is still acceleration (direction changes).

A spaceship travelling towards Earth at \(0.60c\) sends a laser pulse towards Earth. An observer on Earth measures the speed of the pulse as:

  1. \(1.6c\)
  2. \(c\)
  3. \(0.4c\)
  4. \(0.6c\)

Einstein's second postulate: light travels at \(c\) for every observer, regardless of the motion of the source.

The Michelson–Morley experiment is significant because it:

  1. found no evidence for the aether: the speed of light was the same in all directions
  2. measured the speed of light accurately for the first time
  3. showed that light is a particle
  4. proved that time dilation occurs

The null result: no change in the speed of light with direction, so no aether wind. It supports Einstein's second postulate.

A physicist in a windowless laboratory moving at constant velocity wants to measure the lab's speed through space. Einstein's first postulate implies that:

  1. she can measure it by timing a pendulum
  2. she can measure it with light beams, because light is special
  3. no experiment inside the lab can detect its constant-velocity motion
  4. she can measure it only if the lab moves faster than \(0.5c\)

If the laws of physics are the same in all inertial frames, every experiment gives the same result whether the lab is “at rest” or moving uniformly. There is no absolute rest.

State Einstein's two postulates of special relativity.

1. The laws of physics are the same in all inertial (non-accelerating) frames of reference.

2. The speed of light in a vacuum is the same for all observers, regardless of the motion of the observer or the source.

Compare the classical view of time with the view of time in Einstein's special relativity, and explain why Einstein's postulates require time to be treated differently.

In classical physics time is absolute: all observers measure the same time interval between two events, whatever their motion. [1]

In special relativity, time intervals depend on the observer's frame: a clock moving relative to an observer is measured to run slow (time dilation). [1]

Since the speed of light is the same for all observers (postulate 2), but observers in relative motion see light travel different distances between the same two events, they must measure different times so that distance ÷ time equals \(c\) for both. [1]

Summary

  • Inertial frame = not accelerating. Constant speed around a curve is not inertial.
  • Michelson–Morley: no aether, \(c\) the same in all directions.
  • Postulates: physics is the same in all inertial frames; \(c\) is the same for all observers.
  • Classical mechanics works for \(v \ll c\) but fails near \(c\).
