Newton's law of universal gravitation

Every mass in the universe attracts every other mass. Newton realised that the force that makes an apple fall is the same force that keeps the Moon in orbit.

Universal gravitation
\[ F_g = G\frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2} \]

\(G = 6.67\times10^{-11}\ \text{N m}^2\,\text{kg}^{-2}\); \(r\) is the distance between the centres of the two masses (m).

  • The force on each mass is the same size (Newton's third law), even if one is a planet and the other an apple.
  • It is always attractive, along the line joining the centres.
  • It is extremely weak: you only notice it when at least one mass is enormous.

Gravitational field strength

The gravitational field strength at a point is the force per kilogram on a mass placed there. It is also the free-fall acceleration at that point.

Field strength of a planet or star (outside it)
\[ g = \frac{F_g}{m} = \frac{GM}{r^2} \]

Units: N kg⁻¹, which is the same as m s⁻². \(M\) is the mass creating the field and \(r\) the distance from its centre.

Use data from the formula sheet (\(M_E = 5.98\times10^{24}\ \text{kg}\), \(R_E = 6.37\times10^6\ \text{m}\)) to calculate the gravitational field strength at Earth's surface.

\[ g = \frac{GM_E}{R_E^2} = \frac{(6.67\times10^{-11})(5.98\times10^{24})}{(6.37\times10^6)^2} = \frac{3.99\times10^{14}}{4.06\times10^{13}} = 9.83\ \text{N kg}^{-1} \]

9.8 N kg⁻¹. Enter powers of ten with your calculator's EXP / ×10ˣ key, and square the whole radius including its power of ten.

The ISS orbits 400 km above Earth's surface. (a) Calculate \(g\) at this altitude. (b) What is the weight of a 70 kg astronaut there? (c) Why do astronauts describe themselves as “weightless”?

Distance from Earth's centre: \(r = 6.37\times10^6 + 4.0\times10^5 = 6.77\times10^6\ \text{m}\).

(a)

\[ g = \frac{GM_E}{r^2} = \frac{3.99\times10^{14}}{(6.77\times10^6)^2} = 8.70\ \text{N kg}^{-1} \]

That's 89% of the surface value.

(b) \(F_g = mg = 70 \times 8.70 = 609\ \text{N}\).

(c) The astronaut and the station are both in free fall around Earth with the same acceleration \(g\), so the floor does not need to push on the astronaut: the normal force is zero. They feel weightless (apparent weightlessness) even though gravity is almost as strong as on the ground.

(a) 8.7 N kg⁻¹ (b) about 610 N (c) apparent weightlessness: \(N = 0\) because everything falls together. “There is no gravity in space” is wrong and loses marks.

Questions give altitude (height above the surface). Formulas need \(r\), the distance from the centre: \(r = R_{\text{planet}} + \text{altitude}\). Also watch for km: 400 km = \(4.00\times10^5\ \text{m}\).

The Moon (\(7.35\times10^{22}\ \text{kg}\)) orbits \(3.84\times10^8\ \text{m}\) from Earth's centre. Find the gravitational force between them. Which exerts the bigger force?

\[ F = \frac{GM_Em_M}{r^2} = \frac{(6.67\times10^{-11})(5.98\times10^{24})(7.35\times10^{22})}{(3.84\times10^8)^2} = 1.99\times10^{20}\ \text{N} \]

\(2.0\times10^{20}\ \text{N}\). Both exert the same size force on each other (third-law pair). Earth accelerates far less because its mass is 81 times larger.

Ratio reasoning with \(g = GM/r^2\)

Many questions compare planets without giving \(G\). Write \(g \propto M/r^2\) and take ratios:

\[ \frac{g_{\text{planet}}}{g_{\text{Earth}}} = \frac{M_{\text{planet}}/M_E}{(R_{\text{planet}}/R_E)^2} \]

Planet X has twice Earth's mass and twice Earth's radius. What is the gravitational field strength at its surface?

\[ g_X = 9.8 \times \frac{2}{2^2} = 9.8 \times \frac12 = 4.9\ \text{N kg}^{-1} \]

4.9 N kg⁻¹. Doubling the radius has a bigger effect (÷4) than doubling the mass (×2).

Energy changes in a non-uniform field

\(\Delta E_g = mg\Delta h\) only works when \(g\) is constant, i.e. for height changes that are small compared with the planet's radius. Over large distances \(g\) falls off, and you must use a graph:

Energy from graphs
\[ \Delta E_g = \text{area under } F_g\text{–}r \text{ graph} \qquad \Delta E_g = m \times (\text{area under } g\text{–}r \text{ graph}) \]

The area under a \(g\)–\(r\) graph has units N kg⁻¹ × m = J kg⁻¹ (energy per kilogram).

Use the graph to estimate the energy needed to lift a 500 kg satellite from Earth's surface to a height of one Earth radius (\(r = 2R_E\)). Compare with the answer from \(mg\Delta h\).

Count squares under the curve between \(1R_E\) and \(2R_E\): about 9.8 squares (count full squares, then combine the part-squares).

Energy per kilogram: \(9.8 \times 3.19\times10^6 = 3.1\times10^7\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\).

Multiply by the mass: \(\Delta E_g = 500 \times 3.1\times10^7 = 1.6\times10^{10}\ \text{J}\).

Compare: \(mg\Delta h = 500 \times 9.8 \times 6.37\times10^6 = 3.1\times10^{10}\ \text{J}\), about double. \(mg\Delta h\) assumes \(g\) stays at 9.8 N kg⁻¹ all the way up, but it falls to 2.45 N kg⁻¹.

About \(1.6\times10^{10}\ \text{J}\). Using \(mg\Delta h\) over large distances is a classic trap.

Work out what one grid square is worth in the graph's units first (multiply the x-value of one square by the y-value). Count whole squares, then pair up part-squares. VCAA accepts a range of answers for estimated areas, but your working must show the value of one square and the number counted.

The Earth is 81.4 times as massive as the Moon, and their centres are \(3.84\times10^8\ \text{m}\) apart. How far from Earth's centre is the point on the line between them where the net gravitational field is zero?

Set the two fields equal at distance \(x\) from Earth:

\[ \frac{GM_E}{x^2} = \frac{GM_M}{(d - x)^2} \]

Square-root both sides:

\[ \frac{x}{d - x} = \sqrt{\frac{M_E}{M_M}} = \sqrt{81.4} = 9.02 \]

Solve: \(x = 9.02(d - x) \Rightarrow x = \dfrac{9.02}{10.02}d = 0.900 \times 3.84\times10^8 = 3.46\times10^8\ \text{m}\).

\(3.46\times10^8\ \text{m}\) from Earth's centre, 90% of the way to the Moon.

Practice

A satellite orbits at an altitude equal to Earth's radius. The gravitational field strength at the satellite is closest to:

  1. 0 N kg⁻¹
  2. 9.8 N kg⁻¹
  3. 4.9 N kg⁻¹
  4. 2.5 N kg⁻¹

Altitude \(R_E\) means \(r = 2R_E\) from the centre, so \(g = 9.8/4 = 2.45\ \text{N kg}^{-1}\).

The distance between two asteroids is tripled. The gravitational force between them becomes:

  1. \(\tfrac13\) of the original
  2. \(\tfrac19\) of the original
  3. 3 times the original
  4. 9 times the original

\(F \propto 1/r^2\), so tripling \(r\) divides \(F\) by 9.

An apple falls towards Earth. Compared with the force Earth exerts on the apple, the force the apple exerts on Earth is:

  1. exactly the same size
  2. much smaller, because the apple's mass is small
  3. zero, because the apple is not a planet
  4. much larger, because Earth accelerates less

Newton's third law (and the formula \(F = Gm_1m_2/r^2\), which is symmetric) gives equal forces. Earth's acceleration is tiny because its mass is huge.

Mars has a mass of \(6.42\times10^{23}\ \text{kg}\) and a radius of \(3.39\times10^6\ \text{m}\). The gravitational field strength at its surface is:

  1. 9.8 N kg⁻¹
  2. 12.6 N kg⁻¹
  3. 3.7 N kg⁻¹
  4. 1.3 × 10⁻³ N kg⁻¹

\(g = \dfrac{(6.67\times10^{-11})(6.42\times10^{23})}{(3.39\times10^6)^2} = 3.73\ \text{N kg}^{-1}\). Option D forgets to square the radius.

The area under a graph of gravitational field strength versus distance between two points represents:

  1. the force on a 1 kg mass
  2. the change in gravitational potential energy per kilogram
  3. the total gravitational potential energy
  4. the work done per metre

N kg⁻¹ × m = J kg⁻¹. Multiply by the mass to get the energy change in joules.

Calculate the gravitational force on a 1000 kg satellite at a distance of \(2R_E\) from Earth's centre.

\(F = \dfrac{GM_Em}{r^2} = \dfrac{(6.67\times10^{-11})(5.98\times10^{24})(1000)}{(1.274\times10^7)^2} = 2.46\times10^3\ \text{N}\), a quarter of its surface weight of 9800 N.

On planet Y, which has the same density as Earth but half its radius, what is the surface gravitational field strength? (Hint: \(M \propto R^3\) at constant density.)

Half the radius at the same density means \(\tfrac18\) the mass (\(M \propto R^3\)).

\(g \propto M/R^2 = \dfrac{1/8}{(1/2)^2} = \dfrac{1/8}{1/4} = \tfrac12\).

\(g_Y = 4.9\ \text{N kg}^{-1}\).

Explain why \(\Delta E_g = mg\Delta h\) gives a good answer for lifting a crate onto a truck but a poor answer for launching a satellite to a high orbit.

\(mg\Delta h\) assumes the field strength \(g\) is constant over the height change. [1]

For a truck (a height change of a metre or so compared with \(R_E = 6.37\times10^6\ \text{m}\)), \(g\) is effectively constant, so the formula is accurate. [1]

For a high orbit, \(r\) changes by a large fraction of \(R_E\) and \(g\) decreases according to the inverse square law; \(mg\Delta h\) then overestimates the energy. The area under the \(g\)–\(r\) (or \(F\)–\(r\)) graph must be used instead. [1]

Summary

  • \(F = Gm_1m_2/r^2\) and \(g = GM/r^2\), with \(r\) from the centre.
  • Ratio method: \(g \propto M/r^2\).
  • Astronauts in orbit are in free fall, not in zero gravity.
  • Energy over large distances = area under \(F\)–\(r\) graph, or \(m\) × area under \(g\)–\(r\) graph.
