Quantised energy levels

Electrons in an atom can only have certain allowed energies, called energy levels. They can't have energies in between. We say the energy is quantised.

  • The lowest level is the ground state; higher levels are excited states.
  • Energies are usually quoted as negative numbers, with 0 eV meaning the electron has just escaped the atom. The energy needed to free an electron from the ground state is the ionisation energy (13.6 eV for hydrogen).
  • Levels get closer together towards the top.

Emission spectra

When an atom is excited (by heating, an electric discharge, or collisions), an electron jumps to a higher level. It soon drops back down, emitting a photon whose energy exactly equals the energy difference between the two levels:

Photon emitted or absorbed in a transition
\[ \Delta E = E_{\text{upper}} - E_{\text{lower}} = hf = \frac{hc}{\lambda} \]

Shortcut: \(\lambda\ (\text{nm}) \approx \dfrac{1240}{\Delta E\ (\text{eV})}\), using \(hc = 1.24\times10^{-6}\ \text{eV m}\).

Because only certain energy differences are possible, only certain photon energies (wavelengths) come out: a line emission spectrum of bright coloured lines on a dark background. Each element's pattern is unique, like a fingerprint.

Metal vapour lamps (sodium street lights, mercury lamps) work this way: an electric current through the vapour excites the atoms, which emit their characteristic lines. Sodium's dominant yellow line is at 589 nm (2.1 eV).

A hydrogen electron drops from n = 3 (−1.51 eV) to n = 2 (−3.40 eV). Find the energy, frequency and wavelength of the emitted photon.

Energy difference: \(\Delta E = -1.51 - (-3.40) = 1.89\ \text{eV}\).

Frequency: \(f = \Delta E/h = 1.89/4.14\times10^{-15} = 4.57\times10^{14}\ \text{Hz}\).

Wavelength: \(\lambda = c/f = 3.0\times10^8/4.57\times10^{14} = 6.57\times10^{-7}\ \text{m}\).

1.89 eV, \(4.6\times10^{14}\ \text{Hz}\), 657 nm (red). Subtract energies carefully with the signs.

How many lines?

An electron in level \(n\) can reach the ground state by many routes, each step giving a photon. The number of different photon energies possible from level \(n\) is

\[ \text{number of lines} = \frac{n(n-1)}{2} \]

From n = 4: \(4 \times 3/2 = 6\) lines (4→3, 4→2, 4→1, 3→2, 3→1, 2→1).

“Atoms are excited to n = 4. How many spectral lines can be observed?” The answer is 6, not 3 (the number of levels below) and not 4. Draw the levels and list every pair.

Absorption spectra

When white light (all wavelengths) passes through a cool gas, atoms absorb photons whose energy exactly matches an energy gap, jumping to higher levels. Those wavelengths are missing from the transmitted light: a line absorption spectrum of dark lines on a continuous background. The dark lines are at exactly the same wavelengths as that element's emission lines. (The re-emitted photons go off in all directions, so very few continue in the original direction.)

Dark lines in the Sun's spectrum (Fraunhofer lines) reveal which elements are in its outer layers.

  • A photon is absorbed only if its energy exactly equals an energy gap (or is enough to ionise the atom). A 3.0 eV photon hitting ground-state hydrogen is not absorbed at all, because no level lies 3.0 eV above the ground state.
  • A colliding electron can give up part of its kinetic energy. A 12.0 eV electron hitting ground-state hydrogen can excite it to n = 2 (10.2 eV) and carry on with the remaining 1.8 eV. It can't reach n = 3 (needs 12.09 eV).

Mercury atoms have excited levels at 4.9 eV and 6.7 eV above the ground state. Find the wavelength of the photon emitted in (a) the 4.9 eV → ground transition and (b) the 6.7 eV → 4.9 eV transition. Which part of the spectrum is each in?

(a) \(\lambda = \dfrac{hc}{\Delta E} = \dfrac{1.24\times10^{-6}}{4.9} = 2.53\times10^{-7}\ \text{m}\) (253 nm), ultraviolet.

(b) \(\Delta E = 6.7 - 4.9 = 1.8\ \text{eV}\); \(\lambda = 1.24\times10^{-6}/1.8 = 6.9\times10^{-7}\ \text{m}\) (690 nm), red.

(a) 253 nm, UV (b) 690 nm, red. Mercury's strong UV line is why fluorescent tubes need a phosphor coating to convert UV to visible light.

How different light sources produce light

Four sources are worth comparing, because each makes light in a different way and so gives a different spectrum:

SourceHow the light is producedSpectrumCoherence and direction
Incandescent bulbA filament is heated to about 2500–3000 K; the hot, dense solid emits thermal radiationContinuous and broad; most of the energy is emitted as infrared, so it's inefficientIncoherent (random phases), emitted in all directions

LaserStimulated emission: an incoming photon triggers an excited atom to emit an identical photon, and the light is amplified between mirrorsEssentially one wavelength (monochromatic)Coherent (same frequency and phase), highly directional, intense narrow beam

  • Coherent ≠ bright. Coherent means a constant phase relationship. A synchrotron beam is far brighter than a laser but covers a wide range of wavelengths.
  • An LED isn't a laser. Both can look like one colour, but LED light comes from spontaneous emission and is incoherent.
  • Why coherence matters: a stable interference pattern (Young's experiment) needs coherent sources, which is why lasers are used.

Why levels are quantised: electron standing waves

Bohr simply assumed electrons have fixed orbits. De Broglie's matter waves explained why. Treat the electron in an orbit of radius \(r\) as a wave travelling around the nucleus. It will only be stable if it forms a standing wave, joining up smoothly with itself after one lap:

Standing-wave condition
\[ n\lambda = 2\pi r \qquad (n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots) \]

A whole number of de Broglie wavelengths must fit around the circumference.

Since \(\lambda = h/(mv)\), only certain wavelengths, and so certain speeds and energies, are allowed: the energy levels are quantised. This is evidence for the dual nature of matter: an electron's wave properties explain the discrete energy levels that produce line spectra.

Hydrogen atoms are excited to the n = 4 level. How many different wavelengths of light can they emit as they return to the ground state?

  1. 3
  2. 4
  3. 6
  4. 10

\(n(n-1)/2 = 4 \times 3/2 = 6\): 4→3, 4→2, 4→1, 3→2, 3→1, 2→1.

A photon of energy 11.0 eV strikes a hydrogen atom in its ground state (levels: −13.6, −3.4, −1.51 eV). The photon is:

  1. absorbed, raising the electron to n = 2 with 0.8 eV left over
  2. not absorbed
  3. absorbed, ionising the atom
  4. absorbed, raising the electron to n = 3

A photon must be absorbed whole, and 11.0 eV doesn't match any gap from the ground state (10.2 eV or 12.09 eV). It passes straight through. An electron with 11.0 eV could excite to n = 2 and keep 0.8 eV.

Absorption and emission involve the same energy gaps, so the same photon energies and wavelengths.

A hydrogen electron drops from n = 4 (−0.85 eV) to n = 2 (−3.40 eV). Find the wavelength of the photon and state its colour.

\(\Delta E = 3.40 - 0.85 = 2.55\ \text{eV}\).

\(\lambda = hc/\Delta E = 1.24\times10^{-6}/2.55 = 4.86\times10^{-7}\ \text{m}\) (486 nm): blue-green (cyan).

Electrons with 12.5 eV of kinetic energy are fired through hydrogen gas in its ground state. Which levels can be excited? What photon wavelengths might then be emitted?

Energies needed from the ground state: n = 2 needs 10.2 eV, n = 3 needs 12.09 eV, n = 4 needs 12.75 eV. With 12.5 eV, electrons can excite up to n = 3.

Possible emissions: 3→2 (1.89 eV, 657 nm), 3→1 (12.09 eV, 103 nm) and 2→1 (10.2 eV, 122 nm).

Which light source produces coherent light by stimulated emission?

  1. an incandescent bulb
  2. an LED
  3. a laser
  4. a synchrotron

Stimulated emission produces photons identical in frequency, phase and direction. LEDs and incandescent bulbs rely on spontaneous emission or thermal radiation; a synchrotron's light comes from electrons accelerated in magnetic fields.

Researchers need an extremely bright beam of X-rays whose wavelength can be tuned to study a protein crystal. The most suitable source is:

  1. a red laser
  2. a synchrotron
  3. a white LED
  4. a sodium vapour lamp

Synchrotrons give a very bright, broad spectrum (IR to X-rays) from which a narrow band can be selected. The Australian Synchrotron in Melbourne is used for exactly this.

Compare how light is produced by an incandescent bulb and by a laser, and explain why only one of them would produce a clear interference pattern in a double slit experiment without extra filtering.

Incandescent: a hot filament emits thermal radiation from a dense solid, giving a continuous spectrum. [1]

Laser: stimulated emission produces photons with the same frequency and phase, giving monochromatic, directional light. [1]

A clear, stable interference pattern needs coherent light of a single wavelength, so the two slits act as sources with a constant phase difference and \(\Delta x = \lambda L/d\) has one value. [1]

The laser satisfies this. The bulb's light is incoherent (unless it's first passed through a narrow single slit, as Young did) and contains many wavelengths, so any fringes are coloured and blur together beyond the first few orders. [1]

Explain why a sodium vapour lamp emits light of only certain wavelengths, whereas an incandescent bulb emits a continuous spectrum.

In the sodium lamp, the current excites isolated sodium atoms; their electrons can only occupy discrete (quantised) energy levels. [1]

When electrons drop between levels they emit photons with \(E = hf = \Delta E\), and since only certain \(\Delta E\) values exist, only certain wavelengths (e.g. 589 nm) are emitted: a line spectrum. [1]

An incandescent filament is a hot, dense solid in which atoms interact strongly, producing thermal radiation with a continuous range of energies, so all wavelengths are emitted. [1]

Summary

  • Energy levels are quantised; transitions emit or absorb photons with \(\Delta E = hf = hc/\lambda\).
  • Emission: bright lines. Absorption: dark lines at the same wavelengths.
  • Photons must match a gap exactly; colliding electrons can give part of their energy.
  • Lines from level \(n\): \(n(n-1)/2\).
  • Incandescent (thermal, continuous), LED (band gap, narrow band), laser (stimulated emission, coherent), synchrotron (accelerated electrons, broad and very bright).
  • Standing waves: \(n\lambda = 2\pi r\) explains quantisation and is evidence for matter waves.
