Section A – Multiple-choice questions

Answer all questions. Choose the response that is correct or best answers the question. A correct answer scores 1; an incorrect answer scores 0. No marks are deducted for incorrect answers.

Two blocks, 2.0 kg and 3.0 kg, sit in contact on a smooth horizontal surface. A 15 N horizontal force pushes on the 2.0 kg block, and the contact force between the blocks is 9.0 N. The same 15 N force is instead applied to the 3.0 kg block, pushing from the other side. The contact force between the blocks is now

  1. 9.0 N
  2. 6.0 N
  3. 15 N
  4. 5.0 N

The acceleration is still \(\frac{15}{5.0} = 3.0\ \text{m s}^{-2}\). The contact force now only has to accelerate the 2.0 kg block: \(2.0 \times 3.0 = 6.0\ \text{N}\).

The contact force always accelerates whichever block is not being pushed. A (9.0 N) assumes it doesn’t depend on which side is pushed.

A 1200 kg car tows an 800 kg trailer along a level road with an acceleration of \(1.5\ \text{m s}^{-2}\). A constant resistive force of 400 N acts on the trailer. The tension in the tow bar is

  1. 800 N
  2. 1200 N
  3. 1600 N
  4. 3400 N

Consider the trailer alone: \(T - 400 = 800 \times 1.5\), so \(T = 1600\ \text{N}\).

B forgets the resistance on the trailer. D is the net driving force needed for the car plus trailer. Isolate the object the tension acts on.

A 0.20 kg mass on a 0.50 m string moves in a horizontal circle as a conical pendulum. The string makes \(30^\circ\) with the vertical. The period of the motion is closest to

  1. 0.95 s
  2. 1.3 s
  3. 1.4 s
  4. 2.6 s

\(r = 0.50\sin 30^\circ = 0.25\ \text{m}\). From \(\tan\theta = \frac{v^2}{rg}\), \(v^2 = 0.25 \times 9.8 \times \tan 30^\circ = 1.41\), so \(v = 1.19\ \text{m s}^{-1}\). Then \(T = \frac{2\pi r}{v} = \frac{2\pi(0.25)}{1.19} = 1.3\ \text{s}\).

C is \(2\pi\sqrt{L/g}\), the simple-pendulum formula. It isn’t on the course and doesn’t apply here. Using the string length (0.50 m) as the radius was the other common error.

A 0.20 kg ball on a 0.80 m string moves in a vertical circle. At the lowest point its speed is \(6.0\ \text{m s}^{-1}\). No energy is added after this. Which statement is correct?

  1. The tension at the top of the circle is 1.2 N.
  2. The tension at the top of the circle is 3.1 N.
  3. The string goes slack before the ball reaches the top.
  4. The ball reaches the top at 6.0 m s⁻¹.

Energy: \(v_{top}^2 = 6.0^2 - 2g(1.6) = 36 - 31.4 = 4.6\ \text{m}^2\text{s}^{-2}\). To stay in the circle at the top it needs \(v^2 \ge gr = 7.8\ \text{m}^2\text{s}^{-2}\). It doesn’t have enough speed, so the string goes slack before the top.

A is \(\frac{mv^2}{r}\) with the weight ignored. B adds the weight instead of subtracting it. Both assume the ball gets there. Always check \(v^2 \ge gr\) before calculating a tension at the top.

A ball is launched from ground level at \(20\ \text{m s}^{-1}\) at \(30^\circ\) above the horizontal. It lands on top of a platform 3.0 m high while falling. The horizontal distance from the launch point to the landing point is closest to

  1. 6.3 m
  2. 29 m
  3. 35 m
  4. 41 m

Vertical: \(3.0 = 10t - 4.9t^2\), so \(4.9t^2 - 10t + 3.0 = 0\) and \(t = 0.37\ \text{s}\) or \(1.68\ \text{s}\). It is falling, so take the later time: \(x = 20\cos 30^\circ \times 1.68 = 29\ \text{m}\).

A uses the earlier root, when the ball passes 3.0 m on the way up. C is the range on level ground.

A 0.16 kg ball travelling at \(20\ \text{m s}^{-1}\) is hit straight back by a bat. The force on the ball rises linearly from zero to 400 N and falls linearly back to zero over a total of 0.050 s. The speed of the ball after the hit is

  1. \(42.5\ \text{m s}^{-1}\)
  2. \(62.5\ \text{m s}^{-1}\)
  3. \(82.5\ \text{m s}^{-1}\)
  4. \(105\ \text{m s}^{-1}\)

Impulse = area of the triangle \(= \tfrac12(400)(0.050) = 10\ \text{N s}\). Taking the rebound direction as positive: \(0.16v = -0.16(20) + 10\), so \(v = 42.5\ \text{m s}^{-1}\).

B treats \(\frac{10}{0.16}\) as the final speed, ignoring the initial momentum. D forgets the \(\tfrac12\) for the triangle’s area.

A 1200 kg car drives up a \(5.0^\circ\) slope at a constant \(25\ \text{m s}^{-1}\) against a total resistive force of 600 N. The power delivered by the engine is closest to

  1. 15 kW
  2. 26 kW
  3. 41 kW
  4. 309 kW

At constant speed the driving force \(= mg\sin\theta + 600 = 1025 + 600 = 1625\ \text{N}\). \(P = Fv = 1625 \times 25 = 41\ \text{kW}\).

A only overcomes the resistance and B only lifts the car. D uses the full weight \(mg\) instead of the component along the slope.

A 0.50 kg mass hangs from an unstretched spring (\(k = 50\ \text{N m}^{-1}\)) and is released from rest. The maximum extension of the spring is closest to

  1. 0.098 m
  2. 0.196 m
  3. 0.294 m
  4. 0.392 m

At the lowest point the mass is momentarily at rest, so all the lost GPE is stored in the spring: \(mgx = \tfrac12 kx^2\), giving \(x = \frac{2mg}{k} = 0.196\ \text{m}\).

A is the equilibrium extension \(\frac{mg}{k}\). The mass overshoots equilibrium and oscillates about it, so the maximum extension is twice as far.

The Earth’s mass is 81 times the Moon’s, and their centres are a distance \(D\) apart. The point on the line between them where the net gravitational field is zero is at what distance from the centre of the Earth?

  1. \(0.10D\)
  2. \(0.50D\)
  3. \(0.90D\)
  4. \(0.99D\)

\(\frac{GM_E}{d^2} = \frac{GM_M}{(D-d)^2}\), so \(\frac{d}{D-d} = \sqrt{81} = 9\) and \(d = 0.90D\).

D uses the mass ratio without taking the square root. A measures the distance from the Moon.

A satellite is moved from a low circular orbit into a higher circular orbit around the Earth. Compared with the original orbit, in the new orbit

  1. its speed is higher and its period is longer.
  2. its speed is lower and its total mechanical energy is lower.
  3. its speed is lower and its total mechanical energy is higher.
  4. its speed is higher and its period is shorter.

\(v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}\), so the speed falls as \(r\) increases. GPE rises by more than the KE falls, so the total energy (\(-\frac{GMm}{2r}\)) becomes less negative, i.e. higher. Energy has to be supplied (rocket burns) to raise an orbit.

B was the most popular wrong answer: lower speed does not mean lower total energy.

An electron is accelerated from rest through a potential difference of 2000 V. Its final speed is closest to

  1. \(6.2 \times 10^{5}\ \text{m s}^{-1}\)
  2. \(1.9 \times 10^{7}\ \text{m s}^{-1}\)
  3. \(2.7 \times 10^{7}\ \text{m s}^{-1}\)
  4. \(7.0 \times 10^{14}\ \text{m s}^{-1}\)

\(qV = \tfrac12 mv^2\), so \(v = \sqrt{\frac{2qV}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{2(1.6\times10^{-19})(2000)}{9.1\times10^{-31}}} = 2.7\times10^{7}\ \text{m s}^{-1}\).

A uses the proton mass. B drops the 2. D forgets the square root, which gives a speed faster than light and should have been an instant red flag.

An alpha particle and a proton enter the same uniform magnetic field at the same speed, perpendicular to the field. The ratio \(\frac{r_\alpha}{r_p}\) of the radii of their paths is

  1. 0.5
  2. 1
  3. 2
  4. 4

\(r = \frac{mv}{qB}\). The alpha has 4 times the mass and 2 times the charge, so \(\frac{r_\alpha}{r_p} = \frac{4}{2} = 2\).

D forgets that the alpha’s charge is +2e.

A 20-turn DC motor coil carries 2.0 A. Each side parallel to the axis of rotation is 0.050 m long and sits in a uniform 0.40 T field. When the plane of the coil is perpendicular to the field, the magnetic force on one of these sides is

  1. 0 N, because the torque is zero.
  2. 0.80 N, but the torque is zero.
  3. 0.80 N, and the torque is at its maximum.
  4. 0.040 N, and the torque is zero.

The side is still perpendicular to \(B\), so \(F = nIlB = 20 \times 2.0 \times 0.050 \times 0.40 = 0.80\ \text{N}\). In this position the forces on the two sides point along the plane of the coil in opposite directions. They pull the coil apart and don’t turn it, so the torque is zero.

Force and torque are different things. The force on the sides parallel to the axis is constant in size. Only its turning effect changes as the coil rotates.

A single rectangular loop of resistance 0.20 Ω is pulled out of a 0.50 T magnetic field at a constant \(2.0\ \text{m s}^{-1}\). The side still inside the field is 0.10 m long. The force needed to keep the loop moving at constant speed is

  1. 0.025 N
  2. 0.050 N
  3. 0.10 N
  4. 0.25 N

\(\varepsilon = Blv = 0.50 \times 0.10 \times 2.0 = 0.10\ \text{V}\) and \(I = \frac{0.10}{0.20} = 0.50\ \text{A}\). The opposing force is \(F = BIl = 0.50 \times 0.50 \times 0.10 = 0.025\ \text{N}\), and the applied force must balance it.

Chaining EMF → current → force is a classic three-step problem. C gives the EMF value with newtons as the unit.

A generator coil is rotated at twice its original frequency, and the number of turns in the coil is halved. Compared with the original output, the peak EMF is

  1. unchanged, and the period is halved.
  2. doubled, and the period is halved.
  3. halved, and the period is doubled.
  4. unchanged, and the period is doubled.

Peak EMF \(\propto N \times \frac{\Delta\Phi}{\Delta t}\). Doubling the frequency doubles the rate of flux change, and halving \(N\) halves it again, so the peak EMF is unchanged. Doubling the frequency halves the period.

A power station supplies 200 kW at 5000 V RMS through transmission lines with a total resistance of 4.0 Ω. At the town, an ideal 20 : 1 step-down transformer supplies homes. The RMS voltage supplied to the homes is closest to

  1. 250 V
  2. 242 V
  3. 234 V
  4. 8.0 V

\(I = \frac{200\,000}{5000} = 40\ \text{A}\). The voltage drop in the lines is \(40 \times 4.0 = 160\ \text{V}\), so the transformer receives \(4840\ \text{V}\) and outputs \(\frac{4840}{20} = 242\ \text{V}\).

A ignores the line voltage drop. D steps the line drop down instead of the voltage at the town.

A string 0.90 m long, fixed at both ends, forms a standing wave with three antinodes at 300 Hz. The next higher frequency that produces a standing wave on this string is

  1. 400 Hz
  2. 450 Hz
  3. 600 Hz
  4. 900 Hz

Three antinodes means the third harmonic, so the fundamental is \(\frac{300}{3} = 100\ \text{Hz}\). Harmonics are whole-number multiples of the fundamental, so the next one is 400 Hz.

C doubles the frequency. For a string fixed at both ends, every whole-number harmonic is allowed.

In a double-slit experiment with light of wavelength 600 nm, the path difference from the slits to point P on the screen is \(1.5 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{m}\). Point P is on

  1. the third bright band either side of the central maximum.
  2. the second dark band either side of the central maximum.
  3. the third dark band either side of the central maximum.
  4. the central bright band.

\(\frac{1.5\times10^{-6}}{600\times10^{-9}} = 2.5\), so the path difference is \(2.5\lambda\). Dark bands occur at \(0.5\lambda\), \(1.5\lambda\) and \(2.5\lambda\), so P is on the third dark band.

A comes from rounding 2.5 to 3. B counts \(0.5\lambda\) as the “zeroth” dark band.

Light of wavelength 400 nm produces photoelectrons from a metal, and the stopping voltage is 1.2 V. With light of wavelength 500 nm on the same metal, the stopping voltage is closest to

  1. 0.58 V
  2. 0.96 V
  3. 1.2 V
  4. 1.5 V

\(E = \frac{hc}{\lambda} = \frac{1.24\times10^{-6}}{400\times10^{-9}} = 3.10\ \text{eV}\), so \(\phi = 3.10 - 1.2 = 1.90\ \text{eV}\). At 500 nm, \(E = 2.48\ \text{eV}\) and \(V_0 = 2.48 - 1.90 = 0.58\ \text{V}\).

B scales the stopping voltage in proportion to \(\frac{1}{\lambda}\). That ignores the work function: \(E_k\) is a straight line with a non-zero intercept, not a proportionality.

An electron and a proton have the same de Broglie wavelength. Which statement is correct?

  1. They have the same speed.
  2. They have the same kinetic energy.
  3. They have the same momentum, and the electron has more kinetic energy.
  4. They have the same momentum, and the proton has more kinetic energy.

\(\lambda = \frac{h}{p}\), so equal wavelengths mean equal momenta. \(E_k = \frac{p^2}{2m}\), so the lighter electron has much more kinetic energy.

D reverses the relationship. For the same momentum, the smaller mass has the greater kinetic energy.

Section B – Short-answer questions

Answer all questions in the spaces provided. Show working for questions worth more than 1 mark.

A 4.0 kg block rests on a smooth ramp inclined at 30° to the horizontal. A light string parallel to the ramp runs from the block over a frictionless pulley at the top of the ramp to a hanging 3.0 kg mass. The system is released from rest.

Calculate the magnitude of the acceleration of the system and state which way the block moves.

  • Weight component down the slope \(= 4.0 \times 9.8 \times \sin 30^\circ = 19.6\ \text{N}\); hanging weight \(= 29.4\ \text{N}\)
  • Net force \(= 9.8\ \text{N}\) on the 7.0 kg system
  • \(a = 1.4\ \text{m s}^{-2}\), block moves up the ramp

Net force \(= 29.4 - 19.6 = 9.8\ \text{N}\). \(a = \frac{9.8}{7.0} = 1.4\ \text{m s}^{-2}\). The hanging mass falls and the block moves up the ramp.

Calculate the tension in the string.

  • Newton’s second law applied to one mass, e.g. \(3.0g - T = 3.0a\)
  • \(T = 25\ \text{N}\)

\(T = 3.0(9.8 - 1.4) = 25.2\ \text{N}\). Check with the block: \(T - 19.6 = 4.0 \times 1.4\), which also gives \(25.2\ \text{N}\).

Many wrote \(T = 29.4\ \text{N}\). If the tension equalled the hanging weight, the hanging mass could not accelerate.

The ramp is replaced by a rough one on which friction can exert a force of up to 12 N on the block. The system is released from rest. Determine whether it moves, and state the size and direction of the friction force on the block.

  • Driving force (9.8 N) is less than the maximum friction (12 N), so the system stays at rest
  • Friction \(= 9.8\ \text{N}\), directed down the ramp

The unbalanced driving force is only 9.8 N. Friction can supply up to 12 N, so it supplies exactly 9.8 N (down the ramp, opposing the tendency to slide up) and the system stays at rest.

The most common error was subtracting the full 12 N and getting a negative acceleration “down the ramp”. Friction only supplies as much force as is needed to prevent motion, up to its maximum.

A 0.50 kg toy car is released from rest at height \(h\) above the bottom of a frictionless track. It then travels around a vertical loop of radius 0.40 m.

Calculate the minimum speed the car needs at the top of the loop to stay on the track.

  • \(v = \sqrt{gr} = 2.0\ \text{m s}^{-1}\)

At the minimum speed, gravity alone provides the centripetal force: \(v = \sqrt{9.8 \times 0.40} = 1.98\ \text{m s}^{-1}\).

Calculate the minimum release height \(h\).

  • Energy conservation from release to the top of the loop
  • Uses \(v^2 = gr\) at the top, with the top of the loop at height 0.80 m
  • \(h = 1.0\ \text{m}\)

\(mgh = mg(2r) + \tfrac12 m(gr)\), so \(h = 2.5r = 1.0\ \text{m}\).

The most common wrong answer was 0.80 m, which assumes the car can have zero speed at the top. A car with zero speed at the top falls off the track.

The car is now released from 1.4 m. Calculate the size and direction of the normal force on the car at the top of the loop.

  • \(v^2 = 2g(1.4 - 0.80) = 11.8\ \text{m}^2\text{s}^{-2}\)
  • \(N + mg = \frac{mv^2}{r}\)
  • \(N = 9.8\ \text{N}\), directed down (towards the centre)

\(v^2 = 2 \times 9.8 \times 0.60 = 11.76\). \(N = \frac{0.50 \times 11.76}{0.40} - 0.50 \times 9.8 = 14.7 - 4.9 = 9.8\ \text{N}\), downwards.

A footballer kicks a ball from ground level at 30° above the horizontal. To score, the ball must pass over a crossbar 3.0 m high, 40 m away horizontally.

Calculate the minimum launch speed needed for the ball to just clear the crossbar.

  • Horizontal: \(t = \frac{40}{v\cos 30^\circ}\)
  • Substitutes into the vertical equation: \(3.0 = (v\sin 30^\circ)t - 4.9t^2\)
  • \(v = 23\ \text{m s}^{-1}\) (22.8)

Eliminate \(t\): \(3.0 = 40\tan 30^\circ - \frac{9.8(40)^2}{2v^2\cos^2 30^\circ}\). So \(\frac{15\,680}{1.5v^2} = 23.1 - 3.0 = 20.1\), giving \(v^2 = 520\) and \(v = 22.8\ \text{m s}^{-1}\).

The hardest part of the paper for many. Strong students wrote \(t\) in terms of \(v\) from the horizontal motion and substituted. Guess-and-check scored at most 1 mark.

Calculate the time taken to reach the crossbar.

  • \(t = 2.0\ \text{s}\)

\(t = \frac{40}{22.8\cos 30^\circ} = \frac{40}{19.7} = 2.0\ \text{s}\).

Determine whether the ball is rising or falling as it passes over the crossbar. Show your reasoning.

  • \(v_y = 22.8\sin 30^\circ - 9.8(2.03) = -8.4\ \text{m s}^{-1}\) (or time to maximum height \(= 1.2\ \text{s} < 2.0\ \text{s}\))
  • Falling

\(v_y = 11.4 - 19.9 = -8.4\ \text{m s}^{-1}\). It is negative, so the ball is falling. Equivalently, it reached its maximum height at \(t = \frac{11.4}{9.8} = 1.2\ \text{s}\).

Explain how the minimum launch speed would change if air resistance were significant.

  • Air resistance reduces the horizontal (and vertical) velocity, so the ball takes longer to reach the posts and rises less
  • A greater launch speed is needed

Air resistance slows the ball horizontally, so it takes longer to reach 40 m and gravity has longer to pull it down. It also reduces the height gained. The kicker would need a greater launch speed.

A 20 g bullet is fired horizontally into a 2.00 kg wooden block hanging from light strings. The bullet embeds in the block, and the block swings up until its centre of mass is 0.18 m higher than its starting point.

Calculate the speed of the block (with the bullet) just after the collision.

  • Energy conservation during the swing: KE → GPE
  • \(v = 1.9\ \text{m s}^{-1}\)

\(\tfrac12 v^2 = gh\), so \(v = \sqrt{2 \times 9.8 \times 0.18} = 1.88\ \text{m s}^{-1}\).

Calculate the speed of the bullet before it hit the block.

  • Momentum conserved in the collision
  • \(u = 1.9 \times 10^{2}\ \text{m s}^{-1}\)

\(0.020u = 2.02 \times 1.88\), so \(u = 190\ \text{m s}^{-1}\).

The classic error is to set the bullet’s KE equal to the final GPE, which gives about \(19\ \text{m s}^{-1}\). Use momentum for the collision and energy for the swing, never energy across an inelastic collision.

Calculate the percentage of the bullet’s kinetic energy that was transformed into other forms during the collision.

  • KE before \(\approx 360\ \text{J}\); KE after \(\approx 3.6\ \text{J}\)
  • \(\approx 99\%\) transformed (heat, sound, deformation)

Before: \(\tfrac12(0.020)(190)^2 = 360\ \text{J}\). After: \(\tfrac12(2.02)(1.88)^2 = 3.6\ \text{J}\). About 99% becomes heat, sound and deformation of the wood.

A 2.0 kg mass is dropped from rest 0.30 m above the top of a vertical spring (\(k = 400\ \text{N m}^{-1}\)). The mass lands on the spring and compresses it.

Calculate the maximum compression of the spring.

  • Energy: \(mg(0.30 + x) = \tfrac12 kx^2\)
  • Correct quadratic, \(200x^2 - 19.6x - 5.88 = 0\)
  • \(x = 0.23\ \text{m}\)

The mass falls \(0.30 + x\) in total. \(19.6(0.30 + x) = 200x^2\), and solving the quadratic gives \(x = 0.227\ \text{m}\).

Many used a fall height of only 0.30 m. The mass keeps losing GPE while it compresses the spring.

Explain where the mass reaches its maximum speed, and calculate that speed.

  • Maximum speed where the net force is zero (spring force = weight)
  • \(x = \frac{mg}{k} = 0.049\ \text{m}\)
  • \(v = 2.5\ \text{m s}^{-1}\)

The mass keeps accelerating until the spring force equals its weight, at \(x = \frac{19.6}{400} = 0.049\ \text{m}\). Energy: \(\tfrac12(2.0)v^2 = 19.6(0.349) - \tfrac12(400)(0.049)^2 = 6.84 - 0.48 = 6.36\ \text{J}\), so \(v = 2.5\ \text{m s}^{-1}\).

The most common error was assuming the maximum speed is at first contact (\(2.4\ \text{m s}^{-1}\)). At that instant the net force is still downwards, so the mass is still speeding up.

A space probe is in a circular orbit 400 km above the surface of Mars. Mass of Mars \(= 6.42 \times 10^{23}\ \text{kg}\). Radius of Mars \(= 3.39 \times 10^{6}\ \text{m}\).

Calculate the orbital period of the probe.

  • \(r = 3.79 \times 10^{6}\ \text{m}\) (radius plus altitude)
  • \(\frac{GMm}{r^2} = \frac{4\pi^2 mr}{T^2}\) rearranged to \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{r^3}{GM}}\)
  • \(T = 7.1 \times 10^{3}\ \text{s}\)

\(T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{(3.79\times10^{6})^3}{6.67\times10^{-11} \times 6.42\times10^{23}}} = 7.1 \times 10^{3}\ \text{s}\) (about 2.0 hours).

Forgetting to add the altitude was the most common error (it gives \(6.2 \times 10^{3}\ \text{s}\)). Using 400 m instead of 400 km produced some impossible answers that students didn’t question.

Calculate the gravitational field strength at the probe’s orbit.

  • \(g = \frac{GM}{r^2}\) with \(r = 3.79 \times 10^{6}\ \text{m}\)
  • \(g = 3.0\ \text{N kg}^{-1}\)

\(g = \frac{4.28 \times 10^{13}}{(3.79\times10^{6})^2} = 2.98\ \text{N kg}^{-1}\).

An instrument inside the probe appears weightless, even though the gravitational field there is about 3 N kg⁻¹. Explain why.

  • The probe and the instrument are both in free fall with the same acceleration (gravity is the only force)
  • So there is no normal (contact) force between them: the apparent weight is zero

The probe and the instrument fall towards Mars with the same acceleration, \(g\), and gravity is the only force on each. Nothing needs to push on the instrument, so the normal force (apparent weight) is zero.

“There is no gravity in space” scored zero. Part (b) had just shown that there is.

Positive ions with charge \(+1.6 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}\) enter a region with an electric field of \(4.0 \times 10^{4}\ \text{V m}^{-1}\) and a magnetic field of 0.20 T, at right angles to each other and to the ions’ velocity. Some ions pass straight through. These ions then enter a region containing only the 0.20 T magnetic field, travel a semicircle, and hit a detector 4.2 cm from where they entered.

Calculate the speed of the ions that pass straight through the crossed fields.

  • \(qE = qvB\) (forces balance)
  • \(v = 2.0 \times 10^{5}\ \text{m s}^{-1}\)

\(v = \frac{E}{B} = \frac{4.0\times10^{4}}{0.20} = 2.0 \times 10^{5}\ \text{m s}^{-1}\).

Calculate the mass of each ion.

  • \(r = 2.1\ \text{cm}\) (half of 4.2 cm)
  • \(qvB = \frac{mv^2}{r}\), so \(m = \frac{qBr}{v}\)
  • \(m = 3.4 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}\)

\(m = \frac{(1.6\times10^{-19})(0.20)(0.021)}{2.0\times10^{5}} = 3.4 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}\). That is about twice the proton mass, so these are deuterium ions.

Using the 4.2 cm diameter as the radius doubles the mass. It was the most common error in the paper.

Explain why the ions’ speed does not change while they travel the semicircle.

  • The magnetic force is always perpendicular to the velocity
  • So it does no work, and the kinetic energy (speed) stays constant

The magnetic force is always perpendicular to the ions’ velocity, so it has no component along their motion. It does no work, so the kinetic energy and speed stay constant. Only the direction changes.

Ions moving faster than \(2.0 \times 10^{5}\ \text{m s}^{-1}\) enter the crossed fields. State which way they are deflected, and why.

  • Deflected in the direction of the magnetic force, because \(qvB > qE\)

The magnetic force \(qvB\) grows with speed, but the electric force \(qE\) doesn’t. Faster ions are deflected in the direction of the magnetic force.

A square coil of 50 turns, each 0.10 m × 0.10 m, moves to the right at a constant 0.50 m s⁻¹. It passes through a region 0.30 m wide containing a uniform 0.40 T magnetic field directed into the page.

Calculate the magnitude of the average EMF induced while the coil is entering the field.

  • \(\Delta\Phi = BA = 4.0 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{Wb}\) and \(\Delta t = \frac{0.10}{0.50} = 0.20\ \text{s}\)
  • \(\varepsilon = N\frac{\Delta\Phi}{\Delta t} = 1.0\ \text{V}\)

\(\varepsilon = 50 \times \frac{0.40 \times 0.010}{0.20} = 1.0\ \text{V}\).

Forgetting \(N\) (giving 0.020 V) and using the width of the field region to find \(\Delta t\) were the main errors.

Explain why there is no induced EMF while the coil is completely inside the field.

  • The flux through the coil is constant, so there is no change of flux and no EMF

While the whole coil is inside the field, the flux through it doesn’t change. Faraday’s law needs a change of flux, so the EMF is zero.

State the direction of the induced current (clockwise or anticlockwise, as viewed) while the coil is entering the field. Justify your answer.

  • Flux into the page is increasing, so the induced field opposes it: out of the page inside the coil
  • Anticlockwise

The flux into the page is increasing. By Lenz’s law the induced current creates a field out of the page inside the coil, which needs an anticlockwise current.

Explain why an external force is needed to keep the coil moving at constant speed while it enters the field.

  • The induced current in the leading side (inside the field) experiences a magnetic force opposing the motion
  • An applied force must balance it for constant speed (the work done becomes electrical energy, then heat)

The induced current flows through the leading side, which is in the field, so that side experiences a magnetic force. By Lenz’s law the force opposes the motion. An equal applied force is needed to keep the speed constant, and the work it does becomes electrical energy, then heat in the coil.

In a generator, the flux through each turn of a 100-turn coil varies sinusoidally with time. The maximum flux is \(4.0 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{Wb}\) and the period is 20 ms.

Calculate the average EMF during a quarter turn, from maximum flux to zero flux.

  • \(\Delta t = \frac{T}{4} = 5.0\ \text{ms}\)
  • \(\varepsilon_{avg} = 80\ \text{V}\)

\(\varepsilon = 100 \times \frac{4.0\times10^{-3}}{5.0\times10^{-3}} = 80\ \text{V}\).

The peak EMF is 126 V. The coil is connected through slip rings to a 20 Ω resistor. Calculate the RMS current in the resistor.

  • \(V_{rms} = \frac{126}{\sqrt 2} = 89\ \text{V}\)
  • \(I_{rms} = 4.5\ \text{A}\)

\(I_{rms} = \frac{89}{20} = 4.5\ \text{A}\) (4.45).

Calculate the average power dissipated in the resistor.

  • \(P = 4.0 \times 10^{2}\ \text{W}\)

\(P = V_{rms}I_{rms} = 89 \times 4.45 = 4.0 \times 10^{2}\ \text{W}\).

Using peak values gives 790 W, double the correct answer.

The slip rings are replaced with a split-ring commutator. Describe the new output.

  • The EMF no longer reverses: pulsating DC with the same peak (126 V) and the same frequency of turns

The output no longer reverses direction. It is a pulsating DC with the same 126 V peak.

A community solar farm uses inverters to produce 60 kW of AC power at 400 V RMS. It supplies a township through transmission lines with a total resistance of 0.50 Ω.

Calculate the power lost in the lines if the power is transmitted at 400 V.

  • \(I = \frac{60\,000}{400} = 150\ \text{A}\)
  • \(P_{loss} = I^2R = 1.1 \times 10^{4}\ \text{W}\)

\(P_{loss} = 150^2 \times 0.50 = 11\,250\ \text{W}\), almost 19% of the output.

An ideal 1 : 25 step-up transformer is installed at the farm. Calculate the new power loss.

  • \(V = 10\,000\ \text{V}\), so \(I = 6.0\ \text{A}\)
  • \(P_{loss} = 18\ \text{W}\)

\(I = \frac{60\,000}{10\,000} = 6.0\ \text{A}\), so \(P_{loss} = 6.0^2 \times 0.50 = 18\ \text{W}\).

\(P = \frac{V^2}{R}\) with the transmission voltage gives an absurd loss. That \(V\) is not the voltage across the lines. Use \(P = I^2R\).

The voltage increased by a factor of 25, but the power loss fell by a factor of 625. Explain why.

  • \(P_{loss} = I^2R\); the current fell by a factor of 25, so the loss fell by \(25^2 = 625\)

At the same power, the current is 25 times smaller. The loss depends on \(I^2\), so it falls by a factor of \(25^2 = 625\).

Solar panels produce DC. Explain why the farm needs inverters (DC → AC) before it can use transformers.

  • A transformer needs a changing magnetic flux in its core to induce an EMF in the secondary (Faraday’s law)
  • Steady DC produces a constant flux, so no EMF is induced

A transformer works by induction. The changing current in the primary produces a changing flux, which induces an EMF in the secondary. Steady DC produces a constant flux, so no EMF is induced.

Ultraviolet light of wavelength 250 nm shines on a sodium surface. The work function of sodium is 2.3 eV.

Calculate the energy of each photon in eV.

  • \(E = 5.0\ \text{eV}\) (4.97)

\(E = \frac{hc}{\lambda} = \frac{4.14\times10^{-15} \times 3.0\times10^{8}}{250\times10^{-9}} = 4.97\ \text{eV}\).

Calculate the maximum kinetic energy of the photoelectrons in eV.

  • \(E_{k\,max} = 2.7\ \text{eV}\)

\(4.97 - 2.3 = 2.7\ \text{eV}\).

Calculate the maximum speed of the photoelectrons.

  • Converts to joules: \(2.67 \times 1.6\times10^{-19} = 4.3 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}\)
  • \(v = 9.7 \times 10^{5}\ \text{m s}^{-1}\)

\(v = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 4.27\times10^{-19}}{9.1\times10^{-31}}} = 9.7 \times 10^{5}\ \text{m s}^{-1}\).

Leaving the energy in eV gave speeds around 2 m s⁻¹, and too many students didn’t notice.

Calculate the de Broglie wavelength of the fastest photoelectrons.

  • \(p = mv = 8.8 \times 10^{-25}\ \text{kg m s}^{-1}\)
  • \(\lambda = 7.5 \times 10^{-10}\ \text{m}\)

\(\lambda = \frac{h}{mv} = \frac{6.63\times10^{-34}}{9.1\times10^{-31} \times 9.7\times10^{5}} = 7.5 \times 10^{-10}\ \text{m}\).

Red light (700 nm) of any intensity ejects no electrons from sodium. Explain why the wave model of light cannot account for this, but the photon model can.

  • Photon model: a 700 nm photon has 1.8 eV, less than the 2.3 eV work function, and each electron absorbs one photon. Higher intensity only means more photons
  • Wave model: energy depends on intensity, so bright enough (or long enough) light of any frequency should eventually eject electrons, which is not observed

In the photon model each electron absorbs one photon. A 700 nm photon carries only 1.8 eV, less than the work function, so no electron can escape however many photons arrive. In the wave model energy is delivered continuously and depends on intensity, so bright enough red light should eventually eject electrons. That doesn’t happen.

A hypothetical atom has these energy levels: \(n = 1\): −10.0 eV, \(n = 2\): −4.5 eV, \(n = 3\): −2.0 eV, \(n = 4\): −0.8 eV.

How many different photon energies can be emitted by atoms excited to \(n = 4\)?

  • 6

Six: 4→3, 4→2, 4→1, 3→2, 3→1, 2→1.

Calculate the longest wavelength that can be emitted.

  • Smallest energy gap: \(n = 4 \to 3\), 1.2 eV
  • \(\lambda = \frac{hc}{E} = 1.0 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{m}\)

\(\lambda = \frac{1.24\times10^{-6}}{1.2} = 1.0 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{m}\) (infrared).

Longest wavelength means smallest energy gap. Many chose the largest gap.

A 6.0 eV photon strikes an atom in the ground state. Explain what happens.

  • Transition energies from \(n = 1\) are 5.5, 8.0 and 9.2 eV (ionisation 10.0 eV); 6.0 eV matches none of them
  • The photon is not absorbed (it passes through)

A photon is absorbed only if its energy exactly matches a transition. From the ground state these are 5.5, 8.0 and 9.2 eV, and ionisation needs 10.0 eV. 6.0 eV matches none, so the photon is not absorbed.

A 6.0 eV electron collides with an atom in the ground state. Can the atom be excited? Explain briefly.

  • Yes: it can be excited to \(n = 2\), because an electron can give up part of its energy. The electron keeps 0.5 eV

Yes. A colliding electron can transfer part of its energy, so the atom can take 5.5 eV (to \(n = 2\)) and the electron leaves with 0.5 eV.

Parts (c) and (d) are a deliberate pair. A photon is all-or-nothing, but an electron can transfer part of its energy.

Muons are created 15 km above the ground (measured in the Earth frame) and travel straight down at \(0.995c\). In their own rest frame, muons have a half-life of \(1.52\ \mu\text{s}\).

Calculate \(\gamma\).

  • \(\gamma = 10\)

\(\gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - 0.995^2}} = 10.0\).

Calculate the time for the muons to reach the ground, measured in the Earth frame.

  • \(t = 5.0 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{s}\)

\(t = \frac{15\,000}{0.995 \times 3.0\times10^{8}} = 5.0 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{s}\) (50 μs).

Calculate the fraction of muons that survive to reach the ground.

  • Proper time \(t_0 = \frac{t}{\gamma} = 5.0\ \mu\text{s}\)
  • Number of half-lives \(= \frac{5.0}{1.52} = 3.3\)
  • Fraction \(= 0.5^{3.3} \approx 0.10\) (10%)

\(t_0 = \frac{50}{10.0} = 5.0\ \mu\text{s}\), which is \(3.3\) half-lives. Fraction surviving \(= 0.5^{3.3} = 0.10\). (Without relativity it would be 33 half-lives, so essentially none would survive.)

Applying the half-life to the Earth-frame time is the key error. The muon’s half-life is measured by its own “clock” (proper time).

Explain how an observer travelling with the muons accounts for the same survival fraction.

  • In the muon frame the atmosphere is length-contracted: \(L = \frac{L_0}{\gamma} = 1.5\ \text{km}\) (15 km is the proper length)
  • Time to reach the ground \(= \frac{1.5\ \text{km}}{0.995c} = 5.0\ \mu\text{s}\), the same number of half-lives

To the muon, the Earth’s atmosphere rushes past at \(0.995c\) and is contracted to \(\frac{15}{10} = 1.5\ \text{km}\). The 15 km is the proper length, because the atmosphere is at rest in the Earth frame. The ground arrives after \(5.0\ \mu\text{s}\) of muon time, the same 3.3 half-lives.

An electron (rest energy 0.511 MeV) is accelerated to a kinetic energy of 1.0 MeV.

Calculate the Lorentz factor \(\gamma\) of the electron.

  • Total energy \(= E_k + mc^2 = 1.51\ \text{MeV}\)
  • \(\gamma = \frac{1.511}{0.511} = 3.0\) (2.96)

\(E_k = (\gamma - 1)mc^2\), so \(\gamma = 1 + \frac{1.0}{0.511} = 2.96\).

Calculate the speed of the electron as a fraction of \(c\).

  • \(v = c\sqrt{1 - \frac{1}{\gamma^2}}\)
  • \(v = 0.94c\)

\(v = c\sqrt{1 - \frac{1}{2.96^2}} = 0.94c\).

Explain why the classical formula \(E_k = \tfrac12 mv^2\) cannot be used here.

  • It gives \(v \approx 2c\), which is impossible. At high speeds \(E_k = (\gamma - 1)mc^2\)

\(\tfrac12 mv^2 = 1.0\ \text{MeV}\) gives \(\frac{v^2}{c^2} = \frac{2 \times 1.0}{0.511}\), so \(v \approx 2.0c\), which is impossible. Relativistic kinetic energy is \((\gamma - 1)mc^2\).

Students use a double slit (slit separation \(d = 0.25\ \text{mm}\)) to find the wavelength of a laser. They measure the fringe spacing \(\Delta x\) at several slit-to-screen distances \(L\).

\(L\) (m)1.001.502.002.503.00
\(\Delta x\) (mm)2.63.95.16.47.6

State what the gradient of a graph of \(\Delta x\) against \(L\) represents.

  • \(\frac{\lambda}{d}\)

\(\Delta x = \frac{\lambda}{d}L\), so the gradient is \(\frac{\lambda}{d}\).

Determine the gradient of the line of best fit.

  • Uses two widely separated points on the line of best fit (not adjacent data points)
  • Gradient \(= 2.5 \times 10^{-3}\) (no units, once both lengths are in metres)

Gradient \(= \frac{(7.6 - 2.6)\times10^{-3}}{3.00 - 1.00} = 2.5 \times 10^{-3}\).

Mixing millimetres and metres without converting was the main issue.

Calculate the wavelength of the laser.

  • \(\lambda = 6.3 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m}\) (625 nm)

\(\lambda = 2.5\times10^{-3} \times 0.25\times10^{-3} = 6.3 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m}\).

The students measured across 10 fringe spacings and divided by 10. Explain how this improves their results.

  • It reduces the percentage uncertainty in each spacing from the ruler’s resolution (a random error); it does not remove systematic error

The ruler’s reading uncertainty is shared over 10 spacings, so the percentage uncertainty in each spacing falls. This reduces random error only. A systematic error (e.g. a wrong value of \(d\)) is unaffected.

Assessor’s report

General comments

This paper was deliberately harder than a VCAA exam. It had fewer “plug into one formula” marks and more questions that needed a plan: which stage uses momentum and which uses energy, which time is proper time, which root of the quadratic is physical. A median of about 55% is typical for a paper at this level. Anything above 70% is excellent.

What separated the top scripts

  • Stages were named. Strong answers to Question 4 wrote “collision: momentum conserved” and “swing: energy conserved” before any numbers. Weaker answers applied energy conservation across the collision and lost almost every mark.
  • Sanity checks. A speed faster than light, an electron at 2 m s⁻¹ or an orbit period of a few seconds should make you stop and find the error. Top students checked their answers.
  • Algebra before numbers. Question 3a can’t be done by plugging in. Writing \(t\) in terms of \(v\) and substituting is the kind of setup the hardest VCAA questions reward.
  • Precise language about causes. “The flux is changing”, not “there is flux”. “Friction only supplies what is needed”, not “friction is 12 N”. “The same acceleration, so no normal force”, not “no gravity”.

Traps in this paper (and in real exams)

  • Checking whether something happens at all before calculating it: the ball that never reaches the top of the circle (MC 4), and the system that doesn’t move on the rough ramp (1c).
  • Choosing the physically correct root: the landing time while falling (MC 5), and the compression rather than the negative root (5a).
  • Diameter versus radius in mass spectrometers (7b).
  • Photons are all-or-nothing, but electrons can transfer part of their energy (13c versus 13d).
  • The half-life belongs to the muon’s own clock (14c).

Advice

If you scored under 50%, go back to the medium paper and aim for 80% there first. The ideas are the same, and this paper just stacks them. If you scored over 65%, spend your remaining time on timed full papers and on writing tight explanations. Marks are now more likely to go on wording than on physics.
