The effect

When light of high enough frequency shines on a clean metal surface, electrons are knocked out of it. These are photoelectrons, and the current they form is the photocurrent. The effect is studied with a vacuum tube: light hits a metal plate (the emitter or cathode), and emitted electrons travel to a collector (anode). A variable voltage can help or hinder them, and an ammeter measures the photocurrent.

What experiments show

  1. There is a threshold frequency \(f_0\) for each metal. Below it, no electrons are emitted, no matter how bright the light or how long you wait.
  2. Above \(f_0\), electrons are emitted instantly, even with very dim light.
  3. The maximum kinetic energy of the photoelectrons depends on the frequency of the light, not its intensity. It increases linearly with frequency.
  4. Increasing the intensity (above \(f_0\)) increases the number of electrons emitted per second (the photocurrent), but not their maximum kinetic energy.

Einstein's photon model

In 1905 Einstein proposed that light energy comes in discrete packets called photons, each with energy proportional to the frequency:

Photon energy
\[ E = hf = \frac{hc}{\lambda} \]

\(h = 6.63\times10^{-34}\ \text{J s} = 4.14\times10^{-15}\ \text{eV s}\). Handy shortcut: \(hc = 1.24\times10^{-6}\ \text{eV m}\), so \(E\ (\text{eV}) \approx \dfrac{1240}{\lambda\ (\text{nm})}\).

  • Each photon interacts with one electron, giving it all of its energy (or none).
  • An electron needs a minimum energy, the work function \(\phi\), to escape the metal. If \(hf < \phi\), no electron can escape, however many photons arrive.
  • Any energy left over becomes the electron's kinetic energy. Electrons from the surface (which need exactly \(\phi\)) come out with the maximum kinetic energy.
Einstein's photoelectric equation
\[ E_{k\max} = hf - \phi \qquad \text{threshold: } f_0 = \frac{\phi}{h} \]

Brighter light of the same frequency means more photons per second, each with the same energy. More photons → more electrons per second → bigger photocurrent. But each electron still gets just one photon's worth of energy, so \(E_{k\max}\) is unchanged.

Stopping voltage

Making the collector negative repels electrons. The stopping voltage \(V_0\) is just enough to stop even the most energetic electrons, so the photocurrent drops to zero:

\[ eV_0 = E_{k\max} \]

In electron-volts this is neat: if \(E_{k\max} = 1.5\ \text{eV}\), then \(V_0 = 1.5\ \text{V}\).

Sodium has a work function of 2.3 eV. It is lit with 400 nm light. Find (a) the photon energy in eV and J, (b) the maximum kinetic energy of the photoelectrons, (c) the stopping voltage and (d) the threshold frequency and threshold wavelength.

(a) Photon energy:

\[ E = \frac{hc}{\lambda} = \frac{(4.14\times10^{-15})(3.0\times10^8)}{400\times10^{-9}} = 3.1\ \text{eV} = 3.1 \times 1.60\times10^{-19} = 5.0\times10^{-19}\ \text{J} \]

(b) \(E_{k\max} = hf - \phi = 3.1 - 2.3 = 0.8\ \text{eV}\) (\(1.3\times10^{-19}\ \text{J}\)).

(c) \(V_0 = E_{k\max}/e = 0.8\ \text{V}\).

(d) \(f_0 = \phi/h = 2.3/4.14\times10^{-15} = 5.6\times10^{14}\ \text{Hz}\); \(\lambda_0 = c/f_0 = 5.4\times10^{-7}\ \text{m} = 540\ \text{nm}\).

(a) 3.1 eV = \(5.0\times10^{-19}\ \text{J}\) (b) 0.8 eV (c) 0.8 V (d) \(5.6\times10^{14}\ \text{Hz}\), 540 nm. Light with a wavelength longer than 540 nm (green, yellow, red) ejects no electrons from sodium.

Emission needs \(f \geq f_0\), which means \(\lambda \leq \lambda_0\). Light with a wavelength longer than the threshold wavelength does nothing. Students often flip this.

Graph 1: maximum kinetic energy vs frequency

A student measures \(E_{k\max} = 0.20\ \text{eV}\) at \(6.0\times10^{14}\ \text{Hz}\) and \(E_{k\max} = 1.03\ \text{eV}\) at \(8.0\times10^{14}\ \text{Hz}\) for a metal. Find (a) the experimental value of Planck's constant, (b) the threshold frequency and (c) the work function.

(a) Gradient:

\[ h = \frac{\Delta E_k}{\Delta f} = \frac{1.03 - 0.20}{(8.0 - 6.0)\times10^{14}} = 4.15\times10^{-15}\ \text{eV s} \]

(b) Extrapolate to \(E_k = 0\): from the first point, the line drops 0.20 eV more in \(0.20/4.15\times10^{-15} = 0.48\times10^{14}\ \text{Hz}\), so \(f_0 = 6.0\times10^{14} - 0.48\times10^{14} = 5.5\times10^{14}\ \text{Hz}\).

(c) \(\phi = hf_0 = (4.15\times10^{-15})(5.52\times10^{14}) = 2.3\ \text{eV}\).

(a) \(4.2\times10^{-15}\ \text{eV s}\) (b) \(5.5\times10^{14}\ \text{Hz}\) (c) 2.3 eV (probably sodium).

Graph 2: photocurrent vs voltage

  • Saturation current (the flat part) is proportional to the number of photoelectrons per second, which is proportional to the intensity (photons per second).
  • Stopping voltage (where the curve meets the axis on the negative side) depends only on frequency (and the metal).

At \(V = 0\) there is still a photocurrent: electrons leave the plate with kinetic energy and some reach the collector on their own. You need a negative collector voltage to stop them. That's why the curves cross the current axis above zero.

Practice

Light of a frequency above the threshold frequency shines on a metal. The intensity is doubled. Which is correct?

  1. The maximum kinetic energy of the photoelectrons doubles.
  2. The number of photoelectrons emitted per second doubles; their maximum kinetic energy is unchanged.
  3. Both the number of photoelectrons and their maximum kinetic energy double.
  4. The stopping voltage doubles.

Double intensity = double the photons per second at the same energy per photon: double the photocurrent, same \(E_{k\max}\) and same stopping voltage.

Very bright red light shines on a metal but no photoelectrons are emitted. Dim ultraviolet light does eject photoelectrons. This is best explained by:

  1. the wave model: UV waves have larger amplitude
  2. red light being absorbed by the air
  3. the red light needing more time to build up energy
  4. each red photon having less energy than the work function, while each UV photon has more

Emission depends on the energy of individual photons (\(hf\)), not the total energy delivered. No number of low-energy photons can free an electron one at a time.

  3. parallel, with Y's line to the left of X's

Both have gradient \(h\), so they're parallel. Y needs a higher threshold frequency, so its line is shifted right.

A metal has a work function of \(3.6\times10^{-19}\ \text{J}\). Light of frequency \(7.5\times10^{14}\ \text{Hz}\) falls on it. Find the maximum kinetic energy of the photoelectrons in joules and eV, and their maximum speed.

\(hf = 6.63\times10^{-34} \times 7.5\times10^{14} = 4.97\times10^{-19}\ \text{J}\).

\(E_{k\max} = 4.97\times10^{-19} - 3.6\times10^{-19} = 1.37\times10^{-19}\ \text{J} = 0.86\ \text{eV}\).

\(v = \sqrt{2E_k/m} = \sqrt{2(1.37\times10^{-19})/9.1\times10^{-31}} = 5.5\times10^5\ \text{m s}^{-1}\).

A 5.0 mW helium–neon laser emits 633 nm light. How many photons does it emit each second?

Energy per photon: \(E = hc/\lambda = (6.63\times10^{-34})(3.0\times10^8)/(633\times10^{-9}) = 3.14\times10^{-19}\ \text{J}\).

Photons per second \(= P/E = 5.0\times10^{-3}/3.14\times10^{-19} = 1.6\times10^{16}\).

Explain how the photoelectric effect provides evidence for the particle-like nature of light, referring to at least two experimental observations that the wave model cannot explain.

Observation 1: there is a threshold frequency below which no electrons are emitted regardless of intensity; the wave model predicts that sufficiently intense light of any frequency should eject electrons. [1]

Observation 2: the maximum kinetic energy depends on frequency, not intensity (and emission is instantaneous even for dim light); the wave model predicts more intense light gives more energetic electrons, and a time delay for dim light. [1]

In the photon model, light arrives as discrete photons of energy \(E = hf\); each photon transfers all its energy to a single electron. [1]

An electron escapes only if \(hf \geq \phi\), with \(E_{k\max} = hf - \phi\); intensity only changes the number of photons, and so the number of electrons. This particle behaviour explains all the observations. [1]

Summary

  • \(E = hf = hc/\lambda\); \(E_{k\max} = hf - \phi\); \(f_0 = \phi/h\); \(eV_0 = E_{k\max}\).
  • Frequency sets \(E_{k\max}\) and \(V_0\). Intensity sets the photocurrent.
  • \(E_k\)–\(f\) graph: gradient \(h\), x-intercept \(f_0\), y-intercept \(-\phi\).
  • Threshold, instant emission and frequency dependence can't be explained by waves: evidence for photons.
